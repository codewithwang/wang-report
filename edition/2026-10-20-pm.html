    Aya Nakamura, AI FOCUS

    AI-authored analysis · Fictional correspondent, real sources

    A successful test supplies evidence of skill. It leaves the buyer needing evidence of control.

    On October 15, AWS reported that its Security Agent, now part of AWS Continuum, exploited 33 of 37 new real-world vulnerabilities. For someone buying automated security testing, that is evidence the software can execute attacks against actual weaknesses. Giving it access to company systems puts that skill to work inside a boundary the buyer must define.

    The connection is straightforward. An agent testing a designated application needs permission to attack it. That permission must also stop it from reaching systems outside the assignment. A successful exploit tells the buyer something about the attack; it does not establish that the agent will respect those limits.

    AWS’s separate “Trust in Depth” guidance describes layers for limiting, observing and verifying agent actions, with human judgment retained for decisions requiring oversight. Read alongside the attack results, it makes the deployment requirement concrete: someone must decide which systems the agent can reach, then enforce that limit whenever it runs.

    The Philippine government has put that objection before the ambassador, or, more precisely, before the diplomatic institution that can receive a protest but cannot by receiving it settle how the next encounter at sea will unfold.

United States Bans DeepSeek From Civilian Government Agencies

Washington’s expanded restrictions make access to government business a question of national rivalry, with consequences that extend beyond an AI model’s capabilities.

The United States has banned DeepSeek technology from civilian government agencies, expanding restrictions on Chinese artificial intelligence amid intensifying rivalry with Beijing. The report carried by MSN puts a concrete administrative decision behind that competition: civilian agencies cannot use the technology. For DeepSeek, the consequence is exclusion from that government market. For Washington, the decision makes the origin of an AI system part of the terms on which government can adopt it.

The expansion matters because civilian government reaches beyond the military and intelligence institutions most readily associated with strategic competition. The supplied report establishes the ban, but gives no implementation timetable or account of how agencies will replace any affected technology. It also supplies no technical finding against DeepSeek. Those limits matter. A government restriction establishes what an agency is permitted to use; it does not, by itself, establish how well a model performs or what a particular deployment has done. The procurement decision is clear even where the technical case is not supplied.

There is a separate, practical question once an AI system is admitted to work, and Aya Nakamura’s AI desk has the reporting. AWS said on October 15 that its Security Agent exploited 33 of 37 new real-world vulnerabilities. Aya explains what that establishes in a clause: demonstrated attacking ability leaves access limits unproven. AWS’s separate guidance describes layers for limiting, observing and verifying agent actions. A successful security test is an impressive demonstration. It is also an unusual sales pitch: the software has proved it can break in.

Washington’s decision and the deployment question operate at different levels. The ban determines which supplier civilian agencies may use. Enforced permissions determine what an authorised agent can reach. Neither decision settles the other. The meaning of this expansion is that competition with Beijing now directly narrows civilian government’s AI choices, regardless of the technical merits agencies might otherwise weigh.
