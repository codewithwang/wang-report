  • 06:08 HKT Warlock ransomware group breaches water utility, telecom operator and university through SharePoint flaws Kai Tanner has the full case for why a document server, not a ministry or a bank, was the target here. read more

    Kai Tanner, CYBER INTEL

    BleepingComputer reports that Warlock, a ransomware group described as China-linked, gained initial access to a water utility, a telecom provider, a regional government body and a university by exploiting vulnerabilities in SharePoint.

    Four victims in four sectors. The reporting does not suggest the group chose them for who they were. It suggests the group chose them for what they were running.

    A ransomware crew with a national label would normally be expected to show a pattern in its targets: a ministry, a defence supplier, a bank. This one has a water utility and a university on the same list, which is the profile of an actor working through a list of reachable servers and reading the victim names afterwards.

    SharePoint is where organisations keep contracts, procedures, staff directories and, in practice, a good deal of what a ransomware operator would want to read before encrypting anything. The BleepingComputer report names the product as the way in. It does not name the flaws in the material available here, so the version question has to be answered from your own inventory.

    The same week, The Hacker News described a separate China-nexus campaign against government and policy organisations across Asia, with a backdoor called Antino that talks through Outlook and OneDrive. Different actor behaviour, same habit: both lean on Microsoft services that sit inside the trusted perimeter.

    The control that matters is unglamorous. Any SharePoint server answering from the public internet, running below the current patch level, is the exposure Warlock used. If a server has to be reachable from outside, it belongs behind authentication at the edge, not behind a patch window that the change freeze keeps moving.

  • 06:11 HKT A Fourth OpenAI Safety Employee Quits, Saying the Culture Is Broken Aya Nakamura on a fourth OpenAI safety departure, and why the buyer of an AI agent owns the controls that matter. read more

    Aya Nakamura, AI FOCUS

    Two days after the Wall Street Journal reported that OpenAI had cut ties with three safety researchers, another safety employee has resigned and said publicly that the company's culture is broken. TechCrunch reported it on October 3.

    Set the dates side by side. In late September OpenAI said its planned GPT-6.1 was too insecure to release, and it shipped a model, GPT-6.1 Sol, in its place. Ars Technica reported that the company put its IPO on hold over safety concerns. Then the safety staff began to leave. The company's stated reason for waiting and the departures of the people who do the safety work are now the same week's news.

    I only have the headline-level facts on the resignation: one employee, one claim about culture. I have no further detail on what prompted it, and nothing here should be read as more than that. What the sequence does establish is smaller and more useful. A company that says safety is the reason it is holding back its public listing has lost four people from that function within days.

    The person who feels this first is the chief information officer deciding whether to put an OpenAI agent near customer data. OpenAI's DevDay recap lists agents and a marketplace among its announcements. A marketplace of agents means software written by other parties, running on OpenAI's model, inside a customer's systems.

    Here the security community's own warnings apply. Cryptographer Matthew Green, quoted by Simon Willison on October 1, argues that sandboxing alone may not contain a compromised agent, because a payload that hijacks one agent can be handed on to the next agent it talks to. Microsoft's security team made a related point on September 30: agents reachable through messaging apps such as WhatsApp slip past URL blocking, so firms need other controls to stop data leaving.

    Both warnings point to controls the buyer has to own. AWS's published patterns for agents, including those on Bedrock AgentCore, show what that looks like in practice. In one, a bounded tool sits over a deterministic rules engine. In another, a single ask_human tool routes decisions to a person. Neither depends on a model vendor's internal safety team staying intact.

    That is the part of the machine to hold onto. A vendor's safety staff reduces risk inside the vendor's own model. The approvals, the data boundaries and the limits on what one agent can pass to another sit in the customer's stack, and OpenAI's IPO delay does not change who has to build them. Any agent rollout approved this quarter on the strength of the vendor's safety reputation was approved on a team that, as of October 3, is four people smaller.

  • 06:15 HKT SFC and AFRC sign new MoU extending cooperation to financial reporting of licensed firms and funds Rachel Lam on a new SFC and AFRC memorandum, which gives a licensed firm's accounts a second regulator reading along. read more

    Rachel Lam, FINANCE & RISK

    The SFC and the Accounting and Financial Reporting Council signed a new memorandum of understanding on 28 September (a document that, in the way of these things, says two regulators have agreed to talk to each other). The SFC's notice says the cooperation now extends to the financial reporting of licensed firms and funds.

    Start with who the AFRC is. It oversees the accounting profession and the audits of listed entities in Hong Kong. Until now, the natural reading is that a licensed corporation's accounts were the SFC's business as a licensing matter and the auditor's business as an opinion matter. The MoU puts the auditor's regulator and the licensing regulator in the same channel for the same set of numbers.

    The summary of the SFC's notice available to us does not say what the channel carries. It gives no list of triggers, no information-sharing thresholds and no date by which anything changes. Those details are the only part a compliance team could plan against, and they are absent.

    The same week, the SFC shared updates with the securities industry on scam prevention and cybersecurity, applying to licensed securities firms. Put the two side by side and the SFC's supervisory attention in the last seven days has run through client-facing fraud controls and the integrity of a firm's own books.

    So the finance head at a licensed firm or fund manager now has two readers for the same annual accounts. One grants the licence. The other regulates the people who sign off on them. A qualification in the audit report, or a disagreement with the auditor, has one fewer place to stay quiet.

  • 06:09 HKT Hong Kong public universities raise non-local undergraduate tuition up to 26% for 2027-28 Cheung Kwok-keung on the non-local tuition rise of up to 26 per cent, and who actually receives the letter. read more

    Cheung Kwok-keung, HK DESK

    Here is a shop tip. When you raise the price by a quarter, you don't also put out more tables. Unless you think the queue will stay.

    That is the deal Hong Kong's public universities are offering. From the 2027-28 academic year, undergraduate fees for non-local students on general courses go up by as much as 26 per cent. SCMP calls it the sharpest increase. The same report's headline says the quota is expanding too.

    So: more seats, higher price. Fine. Plenty of places run that way. Think of the airport express, or any restaurant with a view.

    Now think about who is actually writing the cheque. A student from the mainland or overseas, and behind them a family. They don't get a vote on this. They get a letter.

    "Up to 26 per cent" is doing a lot of work, by the way. It means some courses go up by less. Nobody has told us which ones yet, and I wouldn't guess. If you have a kid, a cousin or a tenant thinking about applying, check the fee for their exact course before you plan anything.

    The universities will say they are keeping standards up and the market can bear it. Maybe. We'll find out when the 2027 applications come in.

  • 06:07 HKT Taiwan Water-Cannons A Chinese Fishing Boat At Dongsha And Beijing Gets Its Footage Mei Chen on a Taiwanese water cannon at Dongsha, and why the first Beijing statement will say more than the spray. read more

    Mei Chen, GEOPOLITICAL

    Taiwan's coast guard used a water cannon on a Chinese fishing vessel near Dongsha Island, and the first thing to learn about the incident is how it is being sold. Taiwan says the vessel ignored three warnings before the cannon came out.

    The version circulating online runs under a headline saying Taiwan's navy fired on Chinese ships, and that Xi Jinping is preparing a response. Neither claim appears in the account of the incident itself. The account describes a coast guard, a water cannon and a fishing boat. A fishing boat is not a warship, and a water cannon is not a gun.

    That gap matters because the boat is the point. Beijing's pressure around Taiwan runs through vessels that can be called civilian, and the reply to a civilian vessel is a hose. The hose is what Taipei chose.

    Dongsha is Taiwan-held and sits in the northern South China Sea, far from the main island and nearer the Chinese coast. A coast guard crew there has no fleet behind it, which makes the decision to use force, even this much, a deliberate one. Taipei had the same three warnings and the same option to let the boat drift on. It sprayed the boat.

    Beijing has not yet said whether it treats this as a fishing dispute or as a sovereignty one. The wording of its first statement settles that. A line about the safety of fishermen is a complaint. A line about the waters is a claim, and a claim comes with coast guard patrols attached.

    The same week, Japan's press reported Beijing building up Antelope Reef in the Paracels. China is adding concrete in one corner of the South China Sea while Taiwan is spraying water in another, and only one of those is permanent.

    If Beijing's language treats the Dongsha spray as an attack on its waters rather than a fishermen's grievance, Dongsha becomes the next place Chinese coast guard ships come to stay.

  • 06:10 HKT Kyiv Closes Five of Its Six Bridges as Zelenskyy Promises Harder Strikes on Russian Refineries Vincent Lai on Kyiv's bridges and Zelenskyy's answer, which is addressed to the refinery accounts behind Moscow's war budget. read more

    Vincent Lai, GEOPOLITICAL

    Kyiv now has one open bridge out of six. Russia hit two of them this week, the city closed the other three, and France 24 reported on Saturday that a further major crossing had been struck, which is why the Dnipro has become a ration line for the capital's traffic.

    Zelenskyy's reply, reported by Euromaidan Press, is to hit Russian oil refineries harder. The reply is addressed to a ledger in Moscow.

    Russia's 2027 plan, according to media reports, raises military spending 27 percent, the highest level of the war so far. A budget of that shape needs a revenue line that rises with it, and in Russia that line runs through oil. A refinery sits in the middle of the chain, between crude that the Finance Ministry can tax at the wellhead and the fuel that Russian drivers, farmers and exporters need at the pump, or, more precisely, between the tax the state collects and the product it must still deliver at home. A strike on a refinery interrupts the second without touching the first, and the domestic shortage is what the government then has to explain.

    The two campaigns therefore sit on different kinds of account. Russia's strikes on the bridges raise the cost of living in one city, and Kyiv can route around them, slowly, with the one crossing left. Ukraine's strikes on refineries land on the Russian state's fuel supply, which it cannot route around in the same way, and which it must keep supplied while paying for a war budget 27 percent larger.

    Neither side has yet shown what its strikes buy. Kyiv's residents are already carrying the cost of the bridge campaign, and Russian drivers have not yet been shown to carry the cost of the refinery one. The Finance Ministry's October oil revenue figures are the first place the refinery campaign could register on Moscow's books.

  • 06:12 HKT Myostatin Inhibitors Grow Muscle That Fails to Get Stronger Sora Whitlam on why muscle that looks bigger on a scan may not make anyone stronger. read more

    Sora Whitlam, SCIENCE

    Myostatin is a protein your muscles make to stop themselves growing. Remove it and muscle keeps building. Belgian Blue cattle, which carry a myostatin defect, are the textbook demonstration: they look like they have been inflated from the inside.

    That biology made myostatin inhibitors an obvious drug target, and the scan results deliver. Lean mass goes up or holds steady. Peter Attia's latest post on the class describes the catch: drugs, new and old, that preserve lean mass fail to boost strength. The muscles get larger and the person does not get proportionally stronger.

    The mechanism is where the gap opens. Size is a count of tissue on a scan. Strength is what that tissue does when a nervous system recruits it, how well fibres transmit force to tendon, and how much of the added bulk is working contractile protein. A drug that tells muscle to grow addresses the first. It has no obvious hold on the rest. Picture a bigger engine with the same transmission.

    Lean mass is the number easy to measure, which is why it appears on so many slides. It is also why the claim deserves a second read. "Preserves muscle" and "keeps you able to climb stairs" are different statements, and only the second is what anyone buying the drug wants.

    The timing is awkward for the biomarker enthusiasts. FDA leaders joined aging researchers at ARDD 2026 to examine clinical trials, biomarkers and the regulatory challenges facing longevity therapies. A biomarker earns its place by tracking something a patient feels or does. Lean mass on a scan, in this drug class, tracks less of that than the pitch decks assume.

    The practical reader is the one scrolling supplement and protocol content on a Sunday night. Where a product advertises muscle retention, ask what was measured: grip strength, walking speed or chair rises, or only a body-composition scan. If a trial reports the scan alone, you have learned the muscle is bigger. You have not learned it is stronger.

    What the evidence establishes today: myostatin blockade can maintain lean mass. What it has not shown: that the added or preserved tissue translates into function. The next real test is any trial that reports strength and physical performance as its primary outcome rather than a footnote to the scan.

  • 06:14 HKT Boston Dynamics Gives Atlas A Four-Finger Hand, Built For Tools And Abuse Magnus Honeyfield on why Boston Dynamics removed a finger from Atlas, and what the demo has yet to prove. read more

    Magnus Honeyfield, SCIENCE

    The human hand has five fingers, and Boston Dynamics has just built a humanoid hand with four. The company unveiled the new hand for its Atlas robot this week, according to Robotics and Automation News, describing it as designed for complex manipulation and tool use while staying strong and durable.

    Start with why anyone would remove a finger. Every finger on a robot hand is a set of joints, and every joint needs a motor or a cable, plus a way to sense where it is. More fingers means more parts that can jam, snap or wear out. A hand that swings a tool and takes knocks needs to be tough, and the plainest way to make it tougher is to have fewer things inside it that can fail.

    The catch is that fewer fingers can mean less skill. Holding a screwdriver or a wrench means pressing on it from several sides at once, and each finger you lose is one fewer contact point. So the engineering question is how few fingers still hold a tool firmly and still let the robot reorient it. Boston Dynamics is betting that four is enough. The announcement says what the hand is for. It does not, in the material available to me, say how well it does it.

    That gap defines the stage. This is an unveiling of a design for a humanoid, with no durability figure, no grip-strength figure and no deployed-hours figure in the coverage I can see. A hand that looks right in a demo has not yet been through weeks of repeated grasping, which is where robot hands actually fail. The company is selling a promise of both dexterity and toughness; the company has shown the shape of the hand, and the second half of the promise is still unproven in public.

    The next gate is dull and decisive: footage of Atlas using ordinary tools on a task nobody rehearsed, followed by a number for how long the hand runs before someone has to open it up and fix it. Until then, the missing fifth finger is a hypothesis about how few fingers a working robot needs.
