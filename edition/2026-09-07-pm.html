Nine Licences Fall, Sanctions Desks Strain On Two Fronts

Hong Kong's tourism watchdog let a three-strikes system run its course, while Washington's sanctions machinery gets tested by a Pacific warship and a Gulf referral in the same week.

The Travel Industry Council pulled the licences of a tour guide and the two agencies that kept sending her out today, Cheung Kwok-keung reports from the HK Desk. This is the guide who cornered a tour group weeks ago and threatened them into a jade shop. Three strikes, and today the council said out. Cheung's desk makes the comparison: Hong Kong wants five clean years before it will let a human sit next to a driverless car in Tseung Kwan O, but this system took three strikes to stop trusting someone with a busload of tourists. Same government, two different clocks, and today one of them ran out.

Elsewhere, the machinery built to punish bad actors from a distance is being asked to do more than it was built for. North Korea commissioned its second nuclear-capable destroyer on September 6, timed to the opening day of a joint US-Japan-South Korea drill, and Vincent Lai's Geopolitical Ledger notes that the US Treasury's OFAC desk, not the Pentagon, has to draft the sanctions response. The same week, the US, UK, France and Germany moved to refer Iran to the UN Security Council over blocked nuclear inspections, the first such referral in two years. Lai's point is not that the two crises are related. It is that both sanctions programs draw on the same interagency drafting capacity in Washington, so a staffing choice made this month decides which regime gets the sharper follow-through. Separately, Mei Chen's desk counted 17 PLA vessels operating around Taiwan in the 24 hours ending today, a number that matters less for its size than for how ordinary it has become to log.

On the technical side of the ledger, Kai Tanner's Cyber Intel desk has a MikroTik campaign worth any HK bank's attention: attackers are taking full administrative control of internet-facing routers over SSH with no password at all, not through a new exploit but through a configuration default MikroTik has warned about for years. There is no CVE number and no patch coming, because there is no vulnerable code to patch, just a firewall rule nobody wrote. Tanner's advice is blunt: run the port scan against your own fleet this week rather than wait for an advisory that will not arrive.

The guide's badge and Washington's ledgers share the same lesson in different clothing. Systems that look slow, whether a licensing board or a sanctions regime, are still bound by capacity, not intention. Hong Kong had the paperwork to strip a badge the moment it chose to spend the enforcement effort. Washington has the same choice ahead on North Korea and Iran, and which track gets the follow-through will say more about priorities than about either country's behavior.

MikroTik Routers Hijacked Through SSH With No Password At All

Kai Tanner on MikroTik routers wide open over SSH, no password, no CVE, and no patch coming because there's nothing to patch.

Continue reading

Attackers are taking full administrative control of internet-facing MikroTik routers over SSH without credentials, and the exposure is a configuration default, not a zero-day.

Attackers are gaining full administrative control of MikroTik routers by reaching the device's Secure Shell service directly from the internet, according to The Hacker News reporting on the campaign. No password is required. The technique is not a novel exploit chain against a patched flaw; it is SSH left reachable on the public interface with no access-list restriction, which MikroTik's own hardening documentation has told operators to close for years. The distinction that matters here is between a vulnerability disclosure and a configuration audit finding. This one is the second kind, and it does not get a CVE number to make it easier to track.

An HKMA-regulated bank running MikroTik gear at a branch, ATM backhaul link, or vendor site inherits this as a Technology Risk Management Guideline finding, not a patch-Tuesday item: TRM's expectation that remote administrative interfaces sit behind network segmentation and are not internet-facing applies whether or not a CVE exists. The audit question is not "are we patched" but "which of our routers answer SSH on the WAN port right now," a question a vulnerability scanner tuned for CVE matching will not surface. CVE-2025-6205 and CVE-2026-16232 above are both authentication-bypass flaws in named products with KEV dates; this MikroTik campaign has no such anchor and is easier to miss for exactly that reason.

No vendor patch resolves this because there is no vulnerable code path to patch. The control that would have changed the outcome is a firewall rule restricting SSH access to a management VLAN, plus disabling the service entirely on units that do not need remote administration. A bank's network team should be running an external port scan against its own MikroTik fleet this week, not waiting on a MikroTik advisory that will not arrive.

North Korea's Destroyer Lands On A US Treasury Ledger

Vincent Lai on why a North Korean destroyer and an Iran UN referral are really the same Treasury desk's resourcing problem.

Continue reading

Pyongyang's second nuclear-capable destroyer arrived the same week Washington moved to refer Iran to the UN, and both tests run through the same OFAC desk.

North Korea commissioned its second nuclear-capable destroyer on September 6, 2026, timed to the opening day of a joint US-Japan-South Korea drill, and the US Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control is the desk that has to price it, not the Pentagon. OFAC's North Korea sanctions program has spent the past two years chasing the same problem it now has a second hull to show for: a shipbuilding effort funded substantially through crypto theft and ship-to-ship transfers that the existing designations were built to choke off and plainly have not. The same week, the US, UK, France and Germany moved to refer Iran to the UN Security Council over blocked nuclear inspections, the first such referral in two years, according to the coalition's own framing of the move.

Two enforcement tracks, one desk logic: sanctions regimes built to deter through designation lists are being tested simultaneously by a nuclear-adjacent naval commissioning in the Pacific and a Security Council referral in the Gulf, and neither program was designed to run at full capacity on two fronts at once. Or, more precisely, OFAC's North Korea unit and the State Department's Iran referral team draw on the same interagency sanctions-drafting capacity, which means a resourcing choice made in Washington this month, not a strategic one, decides which regime gets the sharper follow-through. The destroyer is already commissioned. The Security Council referral vote is not yet scheduled, and that is the date that tells you which track Washington actually prioritized.

China Sends 17 Ships To Taiwan's Median Line

Mei Chen counts 17 PLA vessels near Taiwan in a single day, a number whose size matters less than how normal it now reads.

Continue reading

A single-day naval count near Taiwan gives Beijing a data point it did not have to spend a shell to collect.

Taiwan's Ministry of National Defense counted 17 People's Liberation Army vessels operating around the island in the 24 hours ending September 7, 2026, alongside aircraft crossing the median line of the Taiwan Strait, a boundary Beijing has not formally recognized since 2020. The count is a number, not a novelty. Taipei's ministry has logged similar patrols through 2025 and into 2026 without pause, and the routine is the point: a presence that no longer requires a press release to register as normal.

What changes is not the ship count. It is the baseline against which Washington, Tokyo, and Taipei now have to measure any future spike, because a fleet this size has already been absorbed into what counts as an ordinary Sunday in the Strait. North Korea commissioned its second nuclear-capable destroyer on September 6, 2026, timed to the start of a joint US-Japan-South Korea drill, and the pairing matters more than either event alone: two Indo-Pacific militaries are demonstrating hardware in the same week, and neither needed the other's provocation as an excuse. The next Taiwan Strait number that matters will not be a ship count. It will be whichever side changes the count enough that the other has to say so out loud.
