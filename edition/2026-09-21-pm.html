Google's Mole Inside TeamPCP Gets a Name

Wired's account of the undercover placement that cracked open the worst software supply chain hack on record moves from anonymous tip to full operational picture, and the arms fight over Xi's Washington trip enters its final 48 hours unresolved.

This morning's story was that someone inside Google had been reading a hacking gang's internal chatter for months. Tonight, Kai Tanner's desk has the operational detail: the placement was inside TeamPCP's planning channels, the layer where the group decided who to hit and how, not the malware dumps researchers usually pick through after the damage is done. Thousands of companies were breached before any of this became public. The visibility Google is describing would have applied before those breaches happened, not after, which is the whole point of running an asset forward instead of reconstructing a crime scene backward.

Across the world, Beijing spent a week threatening to skip a summit over an arms sale, then didn't. Xi Jinping boards a plane for Washington on September 23 with a $14 billion Taiwan arms package still on the table, not shelved. As Mei Chen reports from the Geopolitical desk, Taiwan counted seventeen PLA warships near the island today, two days before Xi lands, and a shipment of attack drones once rejected by another buyer is now headed to Taipei anyway. None of it stopped the trip. A leader who cancels a summit over an arms sale is making a threat he intends to use. A leader who shows up anyway is telling you the cancellation threat was never the actual price of admission: it was leverage to slow the shipment, and Beijing has now signaled the summit is worth more than a delivery it hasn't stopped.

Closer to home, Rachel Lam's desk sorts through a pile of regulatory noise to find the one item that actually touches a Hong Kong license: not HKEX's newly opened listing framework review, not UBS chief Sergio Ermotti calling Switzerland's AT1 capital rules 'bearable' this weekend, but HKMA's routine Q2 stored-value and payment-card statistics, published quietly on September 18. Cheung Kwok-keung's desk flags a narrower, sadder gap: days after a fatal stray dog attack, AFCD is out checking feral dogs across the city, while the chip system that's supposed to identify a dog's breed still just repeats whatever the owner typed at registration, unverified.

The mole story and the summit story share the same lesson in different clothes. TeamPCP was invisible until someone was inside watching it happen in real time, not after. Taiwan's warships and drone shipments are visible in real time, and Beijing is choosing to attend the summit anyway rather than treat them as disqualifying. In both cases, what the watcher or the leader with the plane ticket decided to do about what they saw mattered more than the visibility itself.
