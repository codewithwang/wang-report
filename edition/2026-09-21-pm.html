Taiwan's warships and drone shipments are visible in real time, and Beijing is choosing to attend the summit anyway rather than treat them as disqualifying. In both cases, what the watcher or the leader with the plane ticket decided to do about what they saw mattered more than the visibility itself.

Google's Mole Inside TeamPCP Exposed the Supply Chain Model

A years-long undercover placement inside a hacking group behind thousands of software supply chain breaches shows what it actually takes to see the operation from inside, not from the wreckage.

Google's Threat Intelligence Group ran an analyst inside TeamPCP, the group responsible for what Wired describes as the worst software supply chain hacking spree on record, breaching thousands of companies before Google surfaced the operation from the inside rather than reconstructing it after the fact. The placement was inside the group's inner communications, the layer where operational planning happens, not the malware repositories researchers usually pick apart post-breach. Most supply chain attribution runs backward from a compromised build pipeline or a poisoned package. This one ran forward, from inside the group planning the poisoning.

The distinction matters because supply chain compromises are usually understood only once the payload has already shipped downstream to every company that pulled the dependency. An analyst embedded in the group's internal channels sees the target selection and tooling decisions before they reach a build server, which is a different intelligence product than a post-incident indicator-of-compromise list. Thousands of companies were breached before this was public. The visibility Google is describing would have applied to targeting decisions made before those breaches occurred, not after.

The named thing here is Google's Threat Intelligence Group and the TeamPCP operation itself, not a specific malware family or CVE, because Wired's reporting centers on the human placement rather than a technical artifact. That is itself notable: the most consequential supply chain finding of the week is a source, not a signature. Thousands of breached companies is the number that settles how serious TeamPCP's run actually was.

HKEX's Listing Review Meets a Quiet Insurance Print

Rachel Lam sorts three regulatory headlines and finds the one that's actually Hong Kong's problem, not Zurich's or the Listing Division's.

Continue reading

HKEX opened its Phase II listing framework review the same week HKMA quarterly data offered the only regulator-published numbers on the desk, leaving Basel and AT1 as bank-side, not Hong Kong, business.

Look, three things landed on a regulated desk's screen today and only one of them is actually Hong Kong's problem. HKEX opened Phase II of its listing framework review (its own notice, no closing date attached yet), which sits squarely on the Listing Division's turf, not the SFC's. UBS chief Sergio Ermotti spent the weekend fighting Swiss capital rules ahead of a vote he's calling make-or-break, and telling NZZ the AT1 capital compromise is "bearable" (bearable is doing a lot of work in that quote, the kind of adjective a CEO uses when the alternative was worse). Neither of those is a Hong Kong instrument. What is: HKMA published its stored-value-facility and payment-card statistics for Q2 on September 18, the only primary regulator filings in today's stack that actually name a Hong Kong-supervised population.

So here's the read for a firm sitting under HKMA supervision. The SVF and payment-card statistics aren't a new obligation, they're the routine quarterly data drop that Banking Conduct Department already collects under existing reporting lines, and neither this drop nor the HKEX listing review changes what a stored-value licensee or a card issuer has to do differently by tomorrow. If you're on a Basel III desk watching Zurich, the AT1 fight matters for your Swiss counterparty risk models, not for your Hong Kong prudential filings, since neither Swiss capital rules nor an HKEX listing-framework consultation is HKMA, SFC or IA business. The gap here isn't strange arithmetic, it's just two different regulatory jurisdictions publishing on the same news day.

HKEX's Phase II review is the item with an actual clock: listed issuers and their sponsors should watch for the consultation paper and its comment deadline, which HKEX has not yet published alongside today's announcement. Until that paper lands with a date attached, there is nothing here that changes a Hong Kong-regulated firm's obligations this week.

AFCD Checks Dogs After Attack, Chips Still Unverified

Cheung Kwok-keung on why rounding up strays after a fatal attack doesn't fix the chip registry that let it happen.

Continue reading

Days after a fatal stray dog attack, AFCD is out checking feral dog risk across the city, but the chip system that logs a dog's breed still takes the owner's word for it.

So there's been another fatal dog attack, and now AFCD is out doing "threat checks" on feral dogs around the city, rounding up strays near where it happened. Good, do that. A person died because of a system failure that has been known about for some time: the chip scanned on every dog in Hong Kong just repeats whatever the owner typed in at registration, and nobody checks the breed against the dog.

That's the same gap that let a suspected fighting breed get logged as a mongrel before it killed someone. Catching strays after the fact is triage. The actual fix is boring paperwork: verify what's on the chip before the attack, not after. Until AFCD does that, this week's stray roundups address the symptom, not the registration gap that let it happen.

Manchester City Wins Eight-Goal Thriller, Real Madrid Implodes

Dev Chatterjee on City's eight-goal survival and Real Madrid's ten-man derby collapse, and which one is actually a balance-sheet problem.

Continue reading

City survived a Brian Brobbey hat trick to beat Sunderland while a ten-man Real Madrid lost the Madrid derby, and neither result tells you who actually profits.

Manchester City won 5-3 at Sunderland on Sunday in a match that had no business being that close, Brian Brobbey scoring three for the home side before Erling Haaland, now with a goal against every team he has faced in England, dragged City to a seventh straight win anyway. Two hundred miles south in Spain the day got worse for a bigger name: Real Madrid went down to ten men and lost the first Madrid derby of the season 2-1 to Atletico, a result that leaves Barcelona sitting six points clear in La Liga without having to break a sweat themselves.

Here is the part neither scoreline explains. City's win extends a run funded by a Gulf ownership structure that has never needed matchday profit to justify itself, City Football Group now a portfolio of clubs on four continents built to sell Manchester City's method, not just its trophies. Real Madrid's meltdown, by contrast, threatens the one thing Florentino Perez actually monetizes at scale: a Champion's aura that sells shirts and Bernabeu tours whether or not the football holds up. Haaland scoring against everyone is a highlight. Madrid losing a derby with ten men on the pitch is a balance-sheet problem wearing a football result as a disguise.
