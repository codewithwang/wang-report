GitLab's Perfect-Score Path Traversal Hits Two Repo Platforms At Once

Kai Tanner has the full picture on GitLab's perfect-score flaw and the Gitea bug already being exploited in six countries.

Continue reading

A maximum-severity GitLab flaw and a separately weaponized Gitea bug both landed this week, putting exposed developer infrastructure back at the center of intrusion activity.

CVE-2026-85706 is a path traversal vulnerability in GitLab Community Edition and Enterprise Edition, and it carries a 10.0 CVSS score, the ceiling of the scale. Path traversal means an unauthenticated request can walk out of the web root and read arbitrary files on the server, source code, CI/CD secrets, SSH keys, whatever the GitLab process can see. CISA issued its own alert alongside the disclosure. HKCERT's bulletin the same week bundles it with separate GitLab flaws enabling remote code execution and cross-site scripting, which means a GitLab instance exposed to the internet this week is not choosing between one bad outcome and another.

The mechanism that makes this urgent is not the CVSS number, it is what a self-hosted GitLab instance actually stores. A bank's GitLab holds its CI/CD pipeline credentials, its deployment keys, and often hardcoded API tokens in commit history that nobody scrubbed. File theft off that server does not require a second stage. Separately, The Hacker News reported a suspected Chinese threat actor tracked as Red Heron compromising thirteen organizations across six countries by exploiting a Gitea remote code execution bug, not the same product, not the same vendor, but the same category of target: internet-facing code repository infrastructure that most security teams treat as internal tooling rather than a perimeter asset.

GitLab and Gitea are the two named platforms this week, and the distinction that matters is patch status, not brand. GitLab's flaw is disclosed with no confirmed exploitation yet reported. Red Heron's Gitea exploitation is already active across six countries. An APAC bank running self-hosted GitLab should treat its instance as internet-facing infrastructure holding deployment secrets, not as an internal dev tool, and patch on that basis rather than waiting for its own exploitation report to arrive first.

SFC Bans, Suspends, Skips the Disclosure Story

Rachel Lam on a quiet enforcement week at the SFC and the one deadline, 31 May 2026, that the silence doesn't excuse.

Continue reading

Two enforcement actions and no new cybersecurity guidance this week means the compliance desk's real homework is the OR-2 resilience deadline eight months out.

Look, this was a housekeeping week for the SFC's Enforcement Division, not a policy week. Mok Cheuk Ling drew a 42-month industry ban on September 11 (the SFC's release doesn't itemize the underlying conduct beyond the ban term, which is its own small data point about how thin these notices run). A day earlier, the Division suspended dealings in Cloudbreak Pharma Inc shares over suspected IPO rigging, an SFO Part XIV market-misconduct action rather than a Corporate Finance Division listing review. Neither action touches a licensed corporation's cybersecurity or AI controls. The HKMA's three notices this week were a Silver Bond allocation result, a fraudulent-website alert from HKICL, and a green fintech symposium announcement, public information, not supervisory guidance, so nothing in TM-E-1 or TM-G-1 moves either.

So the read for a Hong Kong compliance head this week is mostly what didn't happen: no GL20 update from the Insurance Authority, no new SFC circular on AI-enabled attacks beyond the existing 26EC32 framework, and the HKMA-HKEX-DFSA-Nasdaq Dubai Strategic Working Group announced September 10 is a market-access initiative for Banking Policy Department-adjacent planning, not a conduct or prudential obligation with a compliance deadline attached. The one live clock that does apply broadly, and that this week's silence doesn't excuse anyone from, is SPM OR-2: an authorized institution had to have its operational resilience framework in place by 31 May 2023 and needs to demonstrate actual resilience, not just a framework binder, by 31 May 2026.

Mark the calendar for 31 May 2026, not for anything in this week's press releases. That's when the Banking Supervision Department's operational and technology risk division stops accepting "we have a framework" as the answer and starts asking for evidence the tolerances hold under a real scenario.

A Second Brain Implant Finally Reads Imagined Speech

Magnus Honeyfield on a wireless implant that produced fluent speech from imagined words alone, no attempted articulation required.

Continue reading

A wireless brain-computer interface let a paralyzed woman produce speech from imagined words alone, without her attempting to move her mouth or throat.

The trick is what the electrodes are listening to. Most speech brain-computer interfaces so far have decoded attempted speech: the patient tries to move a paralyzed tongue, jaw, and larynx, and the implant reads the motor commands that never reach the muscles. The device reported in the New York Times on September 14, 2026, did something harder. It produced fluent speech output when the patient only imagined saying the words, no attempt at articulation required. That distinction matters because attempted speech is exhausting and slow for someone with advanced paralysis; imagined speech is closer to how the rest of us think in words before we say them. The implant is wireless, which removes the skull-mounted connector that has tethered earlier systems to a cart.

This is a first-in-human result, one patient, and the paper does not yet establish how the decoder tells "imagining a word" apart from unrelated inner monologue, which is the obvious next failure mode for a device meant to run all day. It also lands the same week Paradromics announced real-time speech from its Connexus implant and a separate team published simultaneous speech-and-gesture avatar control (medicalxpress.com, September 2026), which means three different groups are now converging on the same target: decode intent before the body tries to execute it, not after. UC Davis's pooled-pretraining work already showed that cutting calibration time is the bottleneck standing between a lab demo and a device a patient could use daily (2026-09-12). Whether the imagined-speech decoder survives contact with a second patient, a different electrode array, and a full day of ordinary inner monologue is the next readout to watch for.
