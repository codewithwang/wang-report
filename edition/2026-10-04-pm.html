  • 17:56 HKT Technical University of Denmark breach exposes data of up to 200,000 people Kai Tanner on the Danish university breach: the hedge, the verb, and the three missing answers that decide how bad 200,000 really is. read more

    Kai Tanner, CYBER INTEL

    The Technical University of Denmark says information belonging to up to 200,000 users may have been exposed. The access was to its identity and access management system, and BleepingComputer reports the attackers downloaded data from it.

    "May have been exposed" and "downloaded" appear in the same disclosure. One is a hedge. The other is a verb.

    The "up to" figure is a ceiling set by what the system contains. It is not a count of people whose records were taken, and the university has not said how close the two numbers are.

    An identity and access management system is the directory that decides who a person is and what they are allowed to open. It is the file an intruder wants first, because every later move borrows its authority. A university runs one for students, staff and a long tail of people who touched the place once and never left the list.

    The reporting available does not say how the attackers got in, how long they were inside, or what fields were taken. Those three answers decide whether 200,000 people need a password reset or something worse.

    The control that matters here is narrow. A bulk read of the full user directory is an unusual event on any network, and an alert on that single action, routed to a person who answers it, is the check that turns a download into an interruption. Whether DTU had one is not in the disclosure.

  • 18:01 HKT Hong Kong share sales hit record US$47.5 billion in third quarter, as nine-month IPO haul reaches HK$388 billion Rachel Lam on three record Hong Kong fundraising totals that do not match, and why none of them says what it counts. read more

    Rachel Lam, FINANCE & RISK

    An analyst building a credit committee deck this weekend has three Hong Kong figures to choose from, and a committee member who will ask where the number came from.

    The first is US$47.5 billion, the record Hong Kong share sales in the third quarter, "despite sell-off" in the wire headlines. The second is HK$388 billion, the nine-month IPO haul that Financial Secretary Paul Chan says has more than doubled, per the South China Morning Post. The third is US$92 billion, a record for Hong Kong IPO fundraising, per BusinessToday, which also credits the AI frenzy.

    Convert the second at the peg of 7.8 (a rate that has not moved in decades, so nobody can blame the currency) and HK$388 billion is about US$50 billion. One quarter is nearly the whole nine months. And the nine months is about half of the US$92 billion.

    So the three figures cannot be the same count. The Q3 headlines say "share sales", which usually takes in placings and follow-on offerings. The Chan figure says IPO. The US$92 billion headline says fundraising, and the reports do not say over what period. None of the three states what it includes.

    The first person to feel that is whoever has to defend the slide. A deck that cites the US$92 billion beside the HK$388 billion invites the question of why a record is twice the year to date. A deck that sets US$47.5 billion beside HK$388 billion invites the follow-up of what happened in the first half.

    The definitions sit with the people who compile the figures: HKEX's market statistics, the SFC, and the data vendors. Until their next release lines the totals up, the deck that carries the wrong one into a credit committee has to explain a discrepancy that the original reports created.

  • 17:57 HKT Kai Tak Hospital opens in phases Cheung Kwok-keung on Kai Tak Hospital: the building is open, the whole hospital is not, and the difference matters to the neighbours. read more

    Cheung Kwok-keung, HK DESK

    Picture an auntie in Kowloon East. She has watched that building go up for years. Now it says hospital on the front, and the news says it is open.

    So she figures it does everything. Her husband gets chest pains late one night, and she doesn't think twice. New hospital, close to home, go.

    Here is the catch. RTHK reports Kai Tak Hospital is opening in phases. That means some services start first and the rest follow later. The building is open. The whole hospital isn't, yet.

    Think of a new restaurant doing a soft opening. The sign is up and the lights are on. Half the menu is still "coming soon." Fine for noodles. Less fine at a hospital.

    I can't give you the list of what is running today. The RTHK report doesn't spell it out in what I have in front of me, and I won't guess. Guessing about someone's husband's chest pains is a bad hobby.

    So here is the plain version. If you live nearby, find out which services are open before you need one. Ask the hospital, or your family doctor. Do it this week, in daylight, over a cup of tea.

    Then find out when the next phase starts. That date is the one that tells you when the auntie's assumption comes true.

In the top of that card, where the best hitter in the sport usually sits, there is a hole where a name should be.     The hitters who stood in on Saturday did so with the biggest bat in the building absent from the on-deck circle, and the pitchers saw nothing to fear on the other side of the order. Rasmussen and Baker did what pitchers do with a gift: they kept it.

    The best-of-five format gives the Yankees more games to find a second hit, and arithmetic is on their side only if the card changes. Tampa Bay needs two more wins. New York needs a lineup that looks like New York.

    Game 2's card is the next thing to land. Check the top of the order first, and see whether the name that matters is on it.

Technical University of Denmark Breach Exposes Data of Up to 200,000

The attackers reached the directory that records who everyone is and what they may open, so the 200,000 figure measures the size of the list, not the harm done.

The Technical University of Denmark says information belonging to up to 200,000 users may have been exposed, and BleepingComputer reports that the attackers downloaded data from the university's identity and access management system. That system is the directory of who each person is and what each person is allowed to open. It is the file an intruder wants first, because every later move borrows its authority.

Kai Tanner's Cyber Intel Desk has the close reading, and the reading starts with the wording. 'May have been exposed' is a hedge. 'Downloaded' is a verb. Both appear in the same disclosure, and only one of them describes something that happened. The 'up to' figure is a ceiling set by what the system contains, not a count of people whose records were taken. A university keeps students, staff and a long tail of people who touched the place once and never left the list, so the ceiling is high by design.

What the disclosure leaves out matters as much. It does not say how the attackers got in, how long they were inside, or which fields were copied. Those three answers decide whether 200,000 people face a routine password reset or something worse. Tanner also notes that a bulk read of an entire user directory is an unusual event on any network, and an alert on that single action is the check that turns a download into an interruption. Whether the university had one is not in the disclosure.

Elsewhere, Rachel Lam's Finance and Risk Desk has sorted out three record figures for Hong Kong fundraising that cannot all be the same count. Cheung Kwok-keung's HK Desk has Kai Tak Hospital opening in phases, and Dev Chatterjee's Sports Desk has the Rays holding the Yankees to one hit in the ALDS opener.

So here is what the Danish case establishes. A directory breach is large because a directory is large, and the harm is whatever sat in its fields. Until the university says what was taken, the number 200,000 describes the size of the directory and not the size of the damage.
