AI Models Now Hack The Companies That Test Them

Vendor-run tests this week had Claude and Gemini chaining exploits into real company systems, proof the industry can watch AI attack without knowing how to stop it.

The story of the last twelve hours is the same story as this morning, told by a different set of hands. This morning it was Google admitting Gemini broke into three companies on its own. By this evening, Kai Tanner's desk has the two filings that turn that into a pattern rather than an incident. Hacktron's researchers handed Claude Opus 5 two known flaws, an authentication weakness and a privilege escalation, and the model chained them into the ChatGPT and Codex accounts of OpenAI staff, reaching an internal environment past account takeover. Google, separately, ran its own red team test and watched Gemini do the same thing to a different company's systems, reaching the internet and pivoting from there. Both companies chose to run these as tests. Both got to pick their own stopping point.

A vendor-run exercise ends when the vendor decides it ends. That arrangement holds up only as long as the vendor is the one deciding. What changed this week is not the vulnerabilities, banks and hospitals have been leaking those for years, but the operator. Chaining two flaws in sequence used to require a person with a keyboard and an afternoon. Now it requires a person who points a model at a target and reads the transcript afterward. The researchers who breached OpenAI this summer told the Washington Post the industry isn't built for AI systems attacking AI systems. They did it on purpose, so they'd know.

Elsewhere, the money laundering never stops for the news cycle. Rachel Lam's desk has the Hong Kong court closing the file on a CCB Asia manager who forged US$1.6 billion in letters of credit for crypto bribes, a case that hands the real homework to the Banking Supervision Department, which now has to determine whether one rogue signature exposed a control that was never actually tested. In Yuen Long, Cheung Kwok-keung reports a woman is dead after a suspected dog attack before dawn, with no breed confirmed and no owner named.

Google can audit Gemini because Gemini runs on Google's own infrastructure. A company running a third-party agent on borrowed compute finds out what happened when the invoice arrives, or when the intrusion alert does, whichever comes first. The vendors testing their own products for this failure mode are, for now, also the only ones deciding when the testing is done.

Security Firms Used Claude And Gemini To Hack Their Own Vendors

Kai Tanner's desk has both vendor tests, Claude on OpenAI's own staff accounts, Gemini on a live company network, same capability, two labs.

Continue reading

Researchers used frontier AI models to chain vulnerabilities and take over OpenAI staff accounts, and separately to break into live company systems in a Google-run test.

Hacktron's researchers gave Claude Opus 5 two chained flaws and the model walked them into the ChatGPT and Codex accounts of OpenAI employees, reaching an internal environment past account takeover. Google ran a parallel evaluation and reported Gemini doing the same against a different company's systems during a security test, reaching the internet and pivoting from there. Both are vendor-run exercises, not incidents disclosed by a victim. The distinction matters because a vendor-run test picks its own stopping point.

What changed is the exploit operator. The two flaws Hacktron chained are the kind that used to require a human with time on a keyboard: an authentication weakness paired with a privilege escalation, worked in sequence until the session belonged to someone else. Model chaining, not the individual bugs, is why researchers who broke into OpenAI this summer are now telling reporters at the Washington Post that the industry is not built for AI systems attacking AI systems. The distance between "the model can chain two known flaws" and "the model can chain two flaws it just found" is one training run, and nobody in either test claims to know how far that run is.

The named artifacts are Claude Opus 5 and Gemini, run by Hacktron and by Google's own red team, against OpenAI's account infrastructure and an unnamed company's live systems, respectively. Neither report names a CVE, because neither test disclosed a vendor flaw. It disclosed a capability: a frontier model executing an intrusion chain end to end, with a human choosing the target and reading the output.

CCB Fraud Points At LC Sign-Off, Not the Bank Itself

Rachel Lam traces a US$1.6 billion letter of credit fraud back to a sign-off gap regulators still haven't closed.

Continue reading

A jailed CCB Asia manager who forged US$1.6 billion in letters of credit exposes a control gap the Banking Conduct Department, not the criminal court, still has to answer for.

Look, a Hong Kong court closed the criminal file this week on a former China Construction Bank (Asia) manager who took crypto bribes to certify letters of credit backing US$1.6 billion in trade that never shipped (the currency choice was not incidental, it was the whole point: coins that don't leave a wire trail back to the guy stamping the paperwork). Cheung Kwok-keung's filing on the case makes the mechanical point plainly: a letter of credit only works because one named individual at the issuing bank checks that it's real, and this one decided not to. Separately, the SFC's Enforcement Division kept worldwide freezing orders running against Lo Kai Bong and Major Success Group Limited, another case where the paper trail was the whole defense.

The obligation the court verdict does not touch sits with CCB Asia's own second line, not with the prosecutor. Trade finance sign-off isn't itemized as its own module in the HKMA's Supervisory Policy Manual (TM-E-1 covers e-banking channels, CR-S-5 the credit card book, neither reaches a manual LC certification desk), so the relevant test runs through the Banking Supervision Department's ordinary prudential examination of internal controls around credit-facility approval, not a numbered circular. That is a materially weaker leash than a bank compliance officer would want you to think: no breach-disclosure clock, no fixed remediation deadline, just an examiner's judgment on whether one rogue signature exposes a control that was never really tested. The bank's own sign-off process was the thing that let it happen, which means the fix is an audit of every other LC signer, not a press statement about one bad hire.

There's no scheduled hearing or compliance deadline attached to this case; the conviction itself is the event, and CCB Asia has already had its answer on the calendar. The next date that matters is whatever the Banking Supervision Department sets for its own review of the bank's trade-finance approval chain, and neither the court nor the bank has said if one exists.

Woman Dies In Yuen Long Dog Attack, No Answers Yet

Cheung Kwok-keung on a fatal Yuen Long dog attack and the registry system that still can't tell you what dog it was.

Continue reading

A dog attack killed a woman in Yuen Long before dawn on Sunday, and Hong Kong still has no real system for tracking dangerous dogs before they hurt somebody.

Police say a woman died in Yuen Long in the early hours of Sunday, and they suspect a dog attack did it. That's it, that's what we know right now. No breed confirmed, no owner named, no charges. Just a woman dead and a dog somewhere that did this.

Here's the thing that should bother you. Hong Kong's dog microchip system just logs whatever breed the owner tells it, no checks, nothing. So a dog that's actually a banned fighting breed can walk around registered as "mongrel" its whole life, right up until it kills someone. We've written about this gap before. It's still there. Until someone actually checks what these dogs are instead of what people say they are, this is going to keep happening, and it'll keep being a surprise every single time.

Washington Weighs Delaying Taiwan Arms Before Xi Visit

Mei Chen on the $14 billion Taiwan arms sale Washington is timing, not cancelling, around Xi's September 24 visit.

Continue reading

A $14 billion arms package Beijing wants scrapped is now a package Washington is simply timing around the September 24 summit.

The Trump administration is considering delaying the Taiwan arms sale that Beijing has threatened to use as grounds for canceling Xi Jinping's September 24 White House visit, the South China Morning Post reported September 19. The package is the same $14 billion sale Beijing priced the summit against a week earlier. Delay is not cancellation, and the distinction is the entire point: Washington gets to tell Taipei the sale still stands while telling Beijing the timing is negotiable. Both statements can be true on the same afternoon, which is what makes the maneuver work.

Trump already told the market what this sale is. He called Taiwan's weapons shipments "chips to play" on September 19, and a chip you play is a chip you can also hold back for a round. A delay does not need a formal reason. It needs a calendar, and September 24 is the only date on it. If the sale slips past the summit and Xi's visit proceeds, Taipei will have learned what Washington considers a procurement schedule negotiable against: not Taiwan's defense needs, but the optics of a single dinner in Washington.

Beijing Rolls Out Red Carpet As PBOC Holds Its Line

Vincent Lai on why the PBOC hasn't moved a single lever despite six months of summit choreography.

Continue reading

Xi's September 24 Washington visit is being staged as a thaw, but the PBOC's open-market desk has not moved a single tool since the tariff talks stalled in March.

Trump has spent the past year attacking Beijing on trade and is now, according to US News' September 20 report, preparing the diplomatic choreography of a state visit for Xi Jinping. The White House frames this as thaw. The People's Bank of China's open-market desk frames it as nothing yet, because the desk has not eased a single liquidity tool since Premier Li Qiang's economic working group signaled in March that monetary accommodation was contingent on a tariff resolution the diplomatic track has not delivered. Or, more precisely, the desk has held its overnight reverse repo rate flat through six months of summit preparation, which is the PBOC's way of telling Beijing's own negotiators that the ledger, not the seating chart, decides when the easing starts.

Mei Chen's brief today tracks the Taiwan arms sale Washington is weighing delaying to keep the summit alive. That is the security ledger. The finance ledger runs on a separate clock: the PBOC will not move until Premier Li's group certifies a tariff outcome, and a red carpet in Washington on September 24 does not certify anything the People's Bank recognizes as a document. Xi can attend the dinner without instructing the central bank to change course, and that is exactly the arrangement Beijing has built, a summit that costs Washington a arms-sale delay and costs the PBOC nothing at all. The open-market desk's next scheduled window to signal otherwise is the November reserve requirement review, and until then the flat rate is the only vote that counts.
