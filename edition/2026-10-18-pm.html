Li Qiang Seeks Deeper Economic Ties With Uzbekistan

Beijing's push for minerals and artificial intelligence cooperation puts economic relationships at the centre of its competition with Washington in Central Asia.

Chinese Premier Li Qiang has called for deeper minerals and artificial intelligence cooperation with Uzbekistan, the South China Morning Post reports, giving Beijing's pursuit of Central Asian influence a concrete economic agenda. The approach brings two subjects into the same diplomatic conversation: resources and the technology that governments increasingly want a part in developing. China's offer is to deepen the relationship through both. For Uzbekistan, that makes the contest for its attention about the economic opportunities attached to a partnership, alongside the political significance of receiving the approach.

Washington is making its own approach. Marco Rubio is travelling to Uzbekistan and India, according to AP, putting the American secretary of state in a country Beijing is actively courting. These are separate diplomatic initiatives involving the same Central Asian partner. The supplied reporting establishes China's proposed areas of cooperation and America's senior diplomatic attention. It does not establish an Uzbek commitment to either side. A visit gets a government into the conversation. It does not give it ownership of the answer, an occasionally inconvenient feature of diplomacy.

The substance of Li's appeal matters because minerals and artificial intelligence offer different reasons to sustain a relationship beyond the visit itself. Minerals cooperation concerns access to resources; AI cooperation concerns participation in a developing technological industry. Beijing is asking Uzbekistan to deepen ties across both. That is a more substantial proposition than a general declaration of friendship, although a call for cooperation is still a proposal. The account supplied here reports no completed agreement, investment amount or delivery timetable. Those limits separate the diplomatic initiative from an economic result.

The consequence is a competition in which Uzbekistan has something both powers want: a closer relationship. Beijing has named economic fields through which to pursue it, and Washington is sending Rubio. Neither action establishes exclusive influence. Central Asia's importance is evident in the attention it receives; the terms its governments accept will determine what that attention is worth.
