New World Development Pays $430 Million to Exit Airport Mall 11 Skies

A developer is paying to leave a shopping mall and booking a $3.6 billion loss, and the Airport Authority is taking the project back.

New World Development is paying $430 million to get out of 11 Skies, the airport mall, and has posted a $3.6 billion loss after a run of impairment charges, according to the Financial Times. Cheung Kwok-keung of the HK Desk has the detail. The SCMP adds that the Airport Authority is taking the project back, and the experts it spoke to say that will spur development of the city's aviation hub.

The direction of the payment is the story. Ordinarily the tenant begs the landlord for a break. Here the developer, controlled by the Cheng family, writes the cheque to leave. An impairment charge is a company admitting that an asset is worth less than it said last year, and the loss is that admission turned into arithmetic. The FT report in hand does not say what the Authority gives up in return, and I will not guess.

Before the other desks, Kai Tanner's Cyber desk has Warlock, a year-old Chinese group that Dark Reading says is running ransomware against organizations in Spain and Portugal while behaving like a state-associated espionage group. The evidence supports 'Chinese' and 'a year old', and not much else. On the money side, Rachel Lam's Finance & Risk desk has the SFC suspending trading in Silver Grant International over a suspected diversion of more than 1 billion dollars in loan proceeds. That is a suspicion, and no finding has been made. Elsewhere, Vincent Lai's ledger prices the 9,000 US troops sailing for the Middle East, Mei Chen reports the first F-16V jets landing in Taiwan seven years after approval, and Dev Chatterjee has the Dodgers' NLDS schedule, which Hong Kong will watch in the small hours.

What it means is simple enough. A mall built for people who are passing through has turned out to be worth less to its builder than the cost of leaving it. When a developer pays to exit, the price of exit is its own valuation of the project, and it sits below zero. The site goes back to the airport's owner, and the loss stays with New World.
