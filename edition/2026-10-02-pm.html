  • 17:59 HKT Warlock ransomware gang, a year-old Chinese group, hits organizations in Spain and Portugal Kai Tanner on Warlock, a Chinese ransomware gang that behaves like a state group, and why the label matters more than the victims. read more

    Kai Tanner, CYBER INTEL

    A year-old Chinese threat actor called Warlock is running ransomware against organizations in Spain and Portugal, according to Dark Reading, and its behaviour fits two categories that attribution normally keeps apart. It looks like a cybercrime gang. It acts like a state-associated advanced persistent threat (APT) group. Dark Reading's own summary stops short of choosing.

    The categories matter because they decide who reads the report. A criminal gang goes to the fraud and extortion desk. A state-associated group goes to the national security desk, and to the board paper that mentions geopolitics. An actor that sits across both can be filed in whichever drawer is less awkward.

    Spain and Portugal are not where a Chinese ransomware group is usually expected. Dark Reading calls these "unexpected places", which is the polite way to say the victim list does not match the model most defenders carry.

    That model is the exposure. Many risk registers treat ransomware as a payment problem and espionage as a secrecy problem, each with its own playbook and its own owner. An intruder who is both gets handled by whichever owner calls first.

    Separately, The Record reported two separate cybersecurity company reports on China-linked operations, one of them a phishing campaign impersonating Western experts and aimed at Asian governments. Nothing in the material available ties Warlock to that campaign. The only thing the stories share is the adjective.

    On attribution, the evidence in hand supports "Chinese" and "a year old" and not much else. Whether the money or the access is the point is not established by anything in the reporting. My read is that the hybrid label is doing the work an analyst's missing evidence would otherwise do.

    The control worth checking today is the incident-response runbook. If it has one branch for ransomware and a different one for suspected state activity, find out which branch fires when the ransom note arrives and the intrusion looks like espionage. Written down, that decision costs nothing. Made at 3am by two teams who each assume the other owns it, it costs the weekend.

  • 18:00 HKT New World Development pays $430 million to exit 11 Skies airport mall, posts $3.6 billion loss Cheung Kwok-keung on the strange sight of a developer paying to leave a mall, with the impairment accounting translated. read more

    Cheung Kwok-keung, HK DESK

    Here's a sentence I never thought I'd write. A developer is paying $430 million to leave a shopping mall.

    Normally it goes the other way. The shop pays the landlord. The tenant begs for a break on the rent. Here the big guy is writing the cheque to walk away.

    The mall is 11 Skies, the airport project. New World Development is the company, and the Cheng family controls it. The FT says the company posted a $3.6 billion loss, after a run of what accountants call impairment charges.

    Let me translate that, because nobody at a bank will.

    An impairment charge is the company admitting a thing is worth less than it said last year. You bought a wok for a hundred. Now you say it's worth forty. The sixty has to go somewhere, and it goes into the loss. Do that on a whole mall and the number gets big fast.

    Now think about that from the shopper's side. Most of us have never needed a reason to go to the airport mall. You go if you're flying. You go if you're early. You wander round for twenty minutes and then you find your gate.

    That is a hard crowd to build a mall around. Somebody in a meeting room looked at a big plan and a lot of planes and saw a lot of shoppers. The shoppers had a flight to catch.

    I'm not saying I called it. I run a noodle stall. But when you build somewhere people only pass through, you should ask whether anyone will stay.

    The company is a big developer with a lot of other projects, so it will be fine. The people with the smaller stakes in all this should read the accounts closely.

  • 18:00 HKT Nine Thousand US Troops Sail for the Middle East, and the Bill Goes to the Navy's Operating Accounts Vincent Lai follows the 9,000 troops to the Navy's operating accounts, where the real cost of a deployment sits. read more

    Vincent Lai, GEOPOLITICAL

    A US official said on Thursday that the American military is sending roughly 9,000 troops aboard a group of ships to the Middle East. The announcement gave a headcount and a destination. It gave no price, and the price is the part that decides how long the deployment can last.

    Troops embarked on ships draw on the Navy's operations and maintenance accounts, or, more precisely, on the readiness lines inside them: fuel, spare parts, steaming days and the maintenance windows that follow a long deployment. Those lines are fixed by Congress in advance. A deployment that was not in the budget is paid for either by a supplemental request or by deferring something else, and the Pentagon comptroller's office keeps the ledger on which that choice is recorded.

    The same week carries a second reading. US officials now assess that a Chinese invasion of Taiwan before 2028 is increasingly unlikely, despite Xi Jinping's 2027 readiness order, citing delays from military corruption purges and Beijing's preferences. A ship group sent to the Middle East is a ship group not in the western Pacific, and that assessment is what makes the allocation affordable. The two decisions sit on the same balance sheet.

    The two timelines do not align. A deployment accrues cost every week it stays. The 2028 assessment depends on purges and preferences in Beijing that Washington does not control and cannot audit. One of them moves, and the fuel and maintenance lines show it first.

    The Pentagon's next budget submission and any supplemental request will show whether these 9,000 troops were a planned line or an unplanned one.

  • 17:58 HKT First New F-16V Jets Reach Taiwan Seven Years After Washington Approved the Sale Mei Chen on Taiwan's first F-16V jets, and what seven years from approval to arrival says about any sale still to come. read more

    Mei Chen, GEOPOLITICAL

    Taiwan's first new F-16V fighters have arrived, and the delivery tells you how long Washington takes to turn an approved sale into an airframe on Taiwanese soil. Reuters reported the landing this week.

    The order dates to 2019, when Washington approved the sale of 66 of the jets. Seven years separate the approval from the first arrival.

    That is the real delivery time, and it is the number to hold against any package still to come. If an approved sale takes this long to land, an unapproved one starts its clock later still.

    Beijing keeps its own clock. Taiwan's Ministry of National Defense tracked 23 Chinese military aircraft, seven naval vessels and seven official ships around the island between 6 a.m. Wednesday and 6 a.m. Thursday. The official ships are the part of that count that does not appear in a fighter inventory.

    The jets will be counted by Taipei as capability. Beijing will count them as a fact it has already priced. China watched the sale move through Washington for seven years and has had that time to plan around it.

    US officials now assess that an invasion before 2028 is increasingly unlikely, citing delays from military corruption purges and Beijing's preferences. That assessment lets Washington treat further arms decisions as something that can wait. The F-16V delivery shows what waiting costs.

    If the read is right, any capability Taipei needs by 2027 that Washington has not yet approved will not arrive in time.

  • 18:01 HKT Dodgers Open Their Three-Peat Bid Saturday At 1 P.M., And The NLDS Schedule Has Fans Grumbling Dev Chatterjee on the Dodgers' three-peat bid and a schedule that asks Hong Kong to choose between the pitch and the pillow. read more

    Dev Chatterjee, SPORTS

    A third straight World Series title is a rarity, and it is exactly what the people now complaining about the kickoff time are waiting to see. The Dodgers are chasing that, and The Athletic's NLDS primer files it under "historic," which is fair.

    The schedule is what the New York Post says fans will not be thrilled about. Game 1 is Saturday at 1 p.m. at Dodger Stadium on Fox. Game 2 is Sunday at 5 p.m. at Dodger Stadium on FS1. Game 3 is Tuesday at 3 p.m., in either Atlanta or Philadelphia, depending on who survived. The Braves answered that question Thursday, beating the Phillies in a decisive Game 3 on a Harris three-run homer, so the Dodgers know their opponent.

    Look at that middle line again. The defending champions play a division series game on FS1. Trace the capital: the broadcast partners buy the series, then the league decides which night of it gets the big network and which gets the channel your cable package may or may not include. A three-peat chase is not enough to buy Game 2 a bigger screen.

    From Hong Kong it is worse. A 1 p.m. Pacific first pitch lands at 4 a.m. Sunday on this reader's clock, and Sunday's 5 p.m. game arrives at 8 a.m. Monday. The Dodgers are chasing history on a timetable that asks half the world to choose between the pitch and the pillow.

    My read is that the schedule is a rounding error. The Dodgers will play it on short notice, in whichever time zone it falls, and if they win it, the grumbling will turn into a parade route.

New World Development Pays $430 Million to Exit Airport Mall 11 Skies

A developer is paying to leave a shopping mall and booking a $3.6 billion loss, and the Airport Authority is taking the project back.

New World Development is paying $430 million to get out of 11 Skies, the airport mall, and has posted a $3.6 billion loss after a run of impairment charges, according to the Financial Times. Cheung Kwok-keung of the HK Desk has the detail. The SCMP adds that the Airport Authority is taking the project back, and the experts it spoke to say that will spur development of the city's aviation hub.

The direction of the payment is the story. Ordinarily the tenant begs the landlord for a break. Here the developer, controlled by the Cheng family, writes the cheque to leave. An impairment charge is a company admitting that an asset is worth less than it said last year, and the loss is that admission turned into arithmetic. The FT report in hand does not say what the Authority gives up in return, and I will not guess.

Before the other desks, Kai Tanner's Cyber desk has Warlock, a year-old Chinese group that Dark Reading says is running ransomware against organizations in Spain and Portugal while behaving like a state-associated espionage group. The evidence supports 'Chinese' and 'a year old', and not much else. On the money side, Rachel Lam's Finance & Risk desk has the SFC suspending trading in Silver Grant International over a suspected diversion of more than 1 billion dollars in loan proceeds. That is a suspicion, and no finding has been made. Elsewhere, Vincent Lai's ledger prices the 9,000 US troops sailing for the Middle East, Mei Chen reports the first F-16V jets landing in Taiwan seven years after approval, and Dev Chatterjee has the Dodgers' NLDS schedule, which Hong Kong will watch in the small hours.

What it means is simple enough. A mall built for people who are passing through has turned out to be worth less to its builder than the cost of leaving it. When a developer pays to exit, the price of exit is its own valuation of the project, and it sits below zero. The site goes back to the airport's owner, and the loss stays with New World.
