ShinyHunters Claim FBI And Cl0p Both Breached

One extortion crew says it hit a federal agency and a rival ransomware gang's infrastructure in the same week, and neither victim has confirmed a thing.

The crew calling itself ShinyHunters says it walked into the FBI's own human resources systems through an unpatched flaw in Oracle PeopleSoft and came out with data on every employee: names, addresses, job assignments. The Bureau has not confirmed it. Oracle has not named the flaw. Kai Tanner's desk has the full sourcing on that gap, and on the second half of the same week's work: ShinyHunters also defaced Cl0p's dark web site and claims to hold Cl0p's own victim data, meaning companies that already paid Cl0p a ransom now face a second extortion attempt from whoever holds that data next.

What makes it one story rather than two is the crew, not the target. The same operators claim a federal law enforcement agency and a fellow ransomware gang as marks inside a single cycle. Identity and access controls that both a US agency and a criminal enterprise relied on turned out not to hold, and PeopleSoft's own customer base spans exactly the kind of institution that assumes itself hardened.

Elsewhere, Aya Nakamura's desk reports that researchers used Anthropic's Claude, not a rival's model, to penetrate OpenAI's own infrastructure in a disclosed red team exercise, days after Anthropic tightened Claude Opus 5.5's safeguards specifically against this kind of use. Rachel Lam's desk flags that Standard Chartered's chief executive admitted this week, in a media interview rather than a regulatory filing, that the bank had closed accounts tied to a 6.9 billion dollar Russian laundering network. No Hong Kong regulator has made it a supervisory matter. In Washington, Mei Chen and Vincent Lai both file on Xi Jinping arriving Thursday for his summit with Donald Trump with a thinned Central Military Commission behind him and a more aggressive South China Sea patrol posture in front of him.

Across the cyber and AI filings this week, the pattern is not which vendor's software failed or which lab's model was used. A federal agency, a systemic bank, and a frontier AI lab each learned of a breach or made an admission before their own regulators or auditors caught it. The disclosure came from the attacker, or from the CEO's own mouth, ahead of the system built to catch it.

ShinyHunters Say They Have Every FBI Employee's Data

Kai Tanner has the sourcing gap that matters: ShinyHunters' claim against the FBI and Cl0p, and what neither Oracle nor the Bureau has confirmed.

Continue reading

A single Oracle PeopleSoft zero-day allegedly gave one extortion crew simultaneous access to the FBI's internal HR systems and Cl0p's dark web infrastructure in the same week.

ShinyHunters says it broke into FBI systems through a previously unknown flaw in Oracle PeopleSoft, the human-resources platform the Bureau uses to manage personnel records, and walked out with data on every employee. 404 Media's report cites the group's own claims and a sample of the stolen files, not an FBI confirmation. Oracle has not published an advisory naming the flaw. That gap between claim and confirmation is itself the operational reality here: a federal law enforcement agency's employee roster, home addresses and job assignments may be sitting on a criminal market with no public acknowledgment from the vendor whose software let it happen.

The same week, the same crew defaced Cl0p's dark web site and claimed to have stolen Cl0p's own victim data, meaning organizations that already paid Cl0p ransoms now face a second extortion attempt from whoever holds that data next. One group breaching both a federal agency and a ransomware operator's infrastructure in the same cycle says something about the state of identity and access controls across sectors that consider themselves hardened targets. PeopleSoft deployments sit behind enterprise authentication layers precisely because HR systems hold the data an intelligence service would want: names, roles, reporting lines, personal details for every person who works there.

Oracle PeopleSoft is the named product; Cl0p and ShinyHunters are the named actors on the extortion side. Cl0p's own victims, the companies that paid to keep their stolen data private, are now the exposed party a second time, through no new breach of their own. The FBI has not confirmed the intrusion, and Oracle has not shipped a patch tied to a CVE. Until one of those two things happens, the only verified fact is that ShinyHunters is now naming both a federal agency and a fellow extortion gang as marks in the same week.

Researchers Hacked OpenAI With Anthropic's Own Model

Aya Nakamura on researchers turning Claude against OpenAI, and why the direction of that arrow matters more than the headline.

Continue reading

A red-team exercise turned Claude into the attacker against OpenAI's infrastructure, and the result says more about audit access than about either model.

Researchers used Anthropic's Claude to penetrate OpenAI systems in a disclosed red-team exercise, according to Ars Technica's September 22 report. The mechanics matter more than the headline: this was not Claude "going rogue," it was operators pointing a capable model at a target and letting it chain reconnaissance, exploit-writing and execution the way a human penetration tester would, just faster and cheaper. That capability is not new to September. What is new is which model did it, and to whom, days after Anthropic shipped Claude Opus 5.5 with what The Verge (September 22) described as tightened cybersecurity safeguards specifically aimed at this kind of misuse.

The part worth sitting with is the direction of the arrow. Security researchers have spent two years warning that any sufficiently capable model can be pointed at a target, OpenAI's own GPT models included. This exercise proves the concern is symmetric: it was not a GPT model attacking Anthropic, it was Claude attacking OpenAI, which means the industry's threat model can no longer treat "our model is the safe one" as a marketing position. Add Google's disclosure the same week (Ars Technica, September 21) that Gemini models were used to hack three companies in May 2026, and the pattern across all three US labs, OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, is the same: the safeguard that matters is not which lab trained the model, it is whether the lab operating the target has agent-level audit logging in place before the attack, not after. Enterprises buying any of these three vendors' API access should read Opus 5.5's tightened controls as a response to this exact scenario, not a routine version bump.

StanChart's $6.9B Admission Lands on No One's Desk

Rachel Lam on StanChart's 6.9 billion dollar admission and why it still isn't a Hong Kong regulatory event.

Continue reading

StanChart's CEO admitted the bank closed accounts tied to a $6.9 billion Russian laundering network, but the disclosure came through a media interview, not a regulator filing.

Look, StanChart's own chief executive told reporters this week that the bank had closed accounts connected to A7, the Russian fintech that regulators say moved billions in sanctioned payments by exploiting gaps in correspondent banking controls (an admission volunteered on the record, which is not how a bank usually chooses to discuss a $6.9 billion laundering network it was part of, however briefly). Separately, UBS settled with Dutch prosecutors over tax crimes inherited from Credit Suisse, closing out yet another line item from the 2023 merger's contingent-liability pile. Neither story landed a filing on Hong Kong's regulators this week: the HKMA's only press release since Monday was a notice that the PBoC will issue renminbi bills through the CMU, a plumbing announcement, not a supervisory one.

That absence is the story for a compliance head here. StanChart's account closures and the A7 sanctions-evasion allegations are exactly the fact pattern the HKMA's Enforcement and AML Department examines under the AMLO and its AML/CFT guideline, customer due diligence, sanctions screening, suspicious transaction reporting to the JFIU, and StanChart Hong Kong sits inside that department's supervisory perimeter as an authorized institution. But nothing published this week ties the two together: no HKMA circular referencing correspondent-banking sanctions exposure, no SFC enforcement notice naming a Hong Kong-licensed counterparty. A compliance officer at a StanChart correspondent bank in Hong Kong has one job out of this: check whether the closed accounts touched any HKD or CNH correspondent relationship booked here, because if they did, the JFIU filing obligation runs from when the institution knew, not from when the HKMA gets around to asking.

The renminbi bills through the CMU take effect on the date specified in HKMA's September 21 notice; watch the settlement mechanics there, not the StanChart story, for what actually changes a Hong Kong desk's workflow this week. The StanChart admission is a fact on the record. It is not yet a Hong Kong regulatory event, and until the HKMA or the SFC files something naming a Hong Kong entity, that is the whole of what a compliance desk here can act on.

China Expels Two Top Generals Before Xi-Trump Summit

Mei Chen on why Xi is walking into Thursday's summit having just gutted his own command structure twice in a month.

Continue reading

The purge of Zhang Youxia's peers lands three days before Xi walks into the White House, and the timing is the message.

The Chinese Communist Party expelled two senior military officials this week over alleged corruption and disloyalty, according to AP reporting, thinning Xi Jinping's Central Military Commission to two members ahead of his September 24 meeting with Donald Trump. Xi has now removed rivals from his own command structure twice inside a month, once in late August and again days before boarding the plane to Washington. A leader who purges his own general staff before a summit is not projecting strength to the room he is about to enter. He is clearing the room behind him first.

The sequencing matters because Xi arrives at the White House this week to press Trump on the frozen 14 billion dollar Taiwan arms sale, a threat Beijing raised September 19 and has not withdrawn. A commander who trusted his military bench would not need to gut it on the eve of his highest-stakes meeting with an American president in years. Xi is walking into that room with a smaller, more loyal, less experienced command tier than he had a month ago. If the purge reflects genuine threat rather than manufactured discipline, Xi negotiates from a domestic position weaker than his Taiwan rhetoric this week suggests.

China Moves Coast Guard From South China Sea To Pre-Summit Cover

Vincent Lai checks the purge against the Coast Guard's own patrol cycle, not the press conference.

Continue reading

A ramming incident and a general's expulsion look unrelated until you check which ministry's books absorb each one.

The China Coast Guard rammed a Philippine government vessel in the South China Sea on September 20, four days before Xi Jinping lands in Washington for Thursday's meeting with Donald Trump. The incident does not sit on the Ministry of Foreign Affairs ledger, or, more precisely, it sits there only for the press conference. Operationally it belongs to the China Coast Guard's own command structure, which answers through the Central Military Commission, the same body that expelled two senior generals this week and now carries just two members heading into Xi's highest-profile diplomatic trip since taking office.

That sequencing is the story Mei Chen's brief on the CMC purge does not carry: a military commission stripped to two members did not also stand down its South China Sea patrol posture. It escalated it. A thinner command tier ordering a more aggressive maritime encounter four days before a Trump summit reads less like weakness and more like a decision to hand Manila and Washington a fresh grievance before the Taiwan arms sale even reaches the table, forcing Trump's negotiators to choose which flashpoint gets airtime on September 24. The China Coast Guard's next patrol cycle, not the CMC's roster, is the thing to check against Thursday's outcome.

18,000 Taxi Plates, Zero New Cars, One Crackdown Coming

Cheung Kwok-keung on who actually benefits when Hong Kong cracks down on illegal ride-hailing drivers.

Continue reading

Hong Kong's promised enforcement push on illegal ride-hailing drivers arrives on top of a taxi fleet that hasn't grown since the medallions were capped.

Ask a taxi driver in Sham Shui Po how business is and he'll laugh at you first, then tell you the real answer. There are about 18,000 taxi licences in this city and there have been about that many for years. Meanwhile everyone's phone has three ride-hailing apps on it, half the drivers on those apps aren't supposed to be driving for hire at all, and the government has now said, officially, it's going after them. Chief Executive John Lee's five-year plan, out this week, leans hard on "regulation" and "order." Translate that from policy-speak: they're finally going to check who's actually driving you home.

Here's the bit nobody says out loud. Those 18,000 licences aren't just permission slips, they're assets, and some of them changed hands for over HK$5 million each back when the market was hot. Somebody paid real money for a fixed number of plates. Now the government wants to clean up the drivers working around that fixed number, which sounds fair, until you ask who benefits when the alternative gets squeezed out. A crackdown on the black market Uber drivers isn't just tidiness. It's also a pretty good deal for whoever's still holding a medallion.

A Compact AI Model Just Beat Every Frontier System At Aging

Magnus Honeyfield on the small model that just beat every frontier system at understanding aging.

Continue reading

A small, specialized model outperformed the largest general-purpose AI systems on a benchmark built to test understanding of aging biology, not scale.

A new benchmark built specifically to test how well AI models understand aging biology found that a compact, purpose-built model beat every frontier system thrown at it, including the large general-purpose models that usually top every leaderboard. The test wasn't about writing fluent sentences about telomeres. It scored models on tasks like predicting how a cell's molecular markers shift as it ages and matching interventions to the biological pathway they actually affect, the kind of structured reasoning where a model has to know the mechanism, not just describe it. The frontier systems, trained on a slice of the entire internet, did worse than a model trained narrowly on biological data.

The reason is not mysterious once you separate what "big" buys you from what a benchmark like this actually tests. A frontier model's size helps it predict plausible-sounding text across every domain at once; it does not force the model to hold a consistent internal model of, say, how NAD+ depletion cascades into mitochondrial dysfunction. A compact model trained only on aging biology datasets can afford to get that one cascade right, because it never has to also be good at writing poetry or debugging code. This is the same lesson protein-folding models taught the field years ago: a narrow model with the right training data beats a general one on the task it was built for, every time. The next gate is whether this compact model's edge holds up against real experimental validation, meaning wet-lab tests of the interventions it flags, not just its score on a written benchmark.

Kawhi Leonard Signs $115 Million Raptors Extension

Dev Chatterjee on Kawhi Leonard's 115 million dollar extension, and who actually holds the exit clause.

Continue reading

Toronto locked its franchise cornerstone into a two-year deal with a player option, betting on his knees one more time.

Kawhi Leonard agreed to a two-year, $115 million extension with the Raptors, plus a player option for 2028-29, his agent Harrison Gaines of Slash Sports confirmed Tuesday. This is a max-level bet on a body that has cost two franchises a combined half-decade of load management, and Toronto just signed up for the third tour. Meanwhile in Dallas, Kyrie Irving's future is generating trade buzz before camp even opens, which is the Mavericks admitting out loud that their timeline and his no longer match.

The number that matters here isn't the $115 million, it's the structure: a player option in year two means Leonard, not Toronto, holds the exit. Franchises love to talk about "control" when they hand out these deals, then hand the actual lever to the guy with the load-management history. The Raptors just bet nine figures that the guy who plays 52 games a year is worth building around anyway. If he stays healthy, genius. If not, Masai Ujiri gets to explain the option clause to season-ticket holders in Scotiabank Arena's upper bowl, where they've already sat through one rebuild pretending it wasn't one.
