China Begins Live-Fire Drills Putting Taiwan On Alert

Beijing's exercises place an immediate demand on Taiwan's serving personnel, exposing the distance between authorising a stronger military and having one ready.

China has begun live-fire drills, prompting Taiwan to closely monitor the exercises, according to Hong Kong Free Press and the report carried by MSN. The development puts an immediate military demand behind the defence spending President Lai Ching-te raised on October 10. Taipei has committed more resources to deterring war. Beijing has now supplied a practical test of readiness, one that must be answered by the military Taiwan has today.

The sequence matters. A government can approve money before it has recruited and trained the people who will turn that money into military capacity. Equipment purchases and personnel training proceed on different schedules. China's drills impose another schedule altogether: the exercise begins, and Taiwan's existing personnel must respond. That does not establish that the spending decision has failed. It establishes why a decision intended to strengthen future readiness cannot, by itself, relieve an immediate demand. Budget approval is fast. Becoming qualified to operate what it buys is rather less impressed by paperwork.

Mei Chen's Geopolitical desk carries the reporting on that gap, drawing on The Korea Times account of a manpower shortage undermining Taiwan's ability to fight. Her filing establishes the constraint beneath today's alert: the people monitoring the drills are already serving, while the additional capacity Taipei wants takes time to build. The supplied reports do not quantify how many personnel the exercises require or how much training they displace. Those limits matter. An understaffed force facing a new demand is established here; a measured loss of combat capability from these particular drills is not.

The consequence is a mismatch between political commitment and military availability. Taiwan's higher spending expresses what its government intends to provide. China's exercises require a response before that undertaking can be completed. Deterrence depends on trained people being available when they are needed, as well as on the equipment a government can afford. Today's drills make that distinction concrete: Taiwan's promise of a stronger force still rests on the personnel carrying its present obligations.
