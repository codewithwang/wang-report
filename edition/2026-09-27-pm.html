  • 17:46 HKT Google warns ShinyHunters bypass WAF rules on PeopleSoft flaw Kai Tanner explains exactly how ShinyHunters slipped an encoded payload past the WAF rule banks thought had them covered. read more

    Kai Tanner, CYBER INTEL

    CVE-2026-35273 sits in Oracle PeopleSoft's web tier and was patched, then wrapped in web application firewall rules to catch exploit attempts against it. Google's Threat Intelligence Group says ShinyHunters found that encoding the malicious URL a different way slides past those WAF signatures unchanged, and the group has resumed dropping web shells on internet-facing PeopleSoft instances it had already compromised once. The rule caught the string it was written for. It did not catch the string doing the same thing.

    The mechanism matters more than the label. A WAF rule written against one URL-encoding of a payload is a rule against that encoding, not against the underlying request the application will still parse and execute identically. Any bank running PeopleSoft HR or financials modules on an internet-facing endpoint, patched or not, is exposed if it treated the WAF rule as the fix rather than a stopgap around the actual patch. ShinyHunters has spent 2026 running exactly this playbook against Oracle E-Business Suite and now PeopleSoft: find the widely-deployed enterprise app, exploit before patch parity, then out-encode whatever gets bolted on after.

    Google names the campaign as targeting "multiple sectors globally" through web shells planted via the same PeopleSoft weakness, which puts the ownership of the fix back on Oracle's patch and on whoever operates the exposed PeopleSoft server, not on the firewall vendor whose rule got walked around. Oracle's original advisory for CVE-2026-35273 is the artifact that matters now. The WAF rule was never it.

  • 17:47 HKT FWD Life's HK$19.5 million AML fine details four control failures Rachel Lam pulls the number that matters out of FWD's fine: four separate control failures, not one lapse. read more

    Rachel Lam, FINANCE & RISK

    Look, the number everyone quoted was HK$19.5 million (Insurance Authority, 25 September). The more useful number is four: the Insurance Authority's order lists four separate anti-money laundering and counter-terrorist financing control failures at FWD Life, not one lapse repeated. Same week, the Securities and Futures Commission suspended trading in Silver Grant International Holdings shares over suspected diversion of more than HK$1 billion in loan proceeds to a former major shareholder (SFC, 25 September), and HKCERT flagged an F5 BIG-IP remote code execution flaw, CVE-2026-94127, being exploited in the wild (HKCERT, 23 September). Three different desks, three different failure modes: a distribution channel that didn't screen, a lending relationship that got redirected, and an edge appliance that got popped.

    For a Hong Kong-authorized insurer, the FWD order is the one that travels. The Insurance Authority's enforcement sits under GL3, the Guideline on Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorist Financing, which covers customer due diligence and screening obligations for insurers and intermediaries under the AMLO (the guideline doesn't distinguish between four small gaps and one big one; it just counts them). A four-count order out of one insurer reads less like an isolated lapse and more like a control framework that was tested in pieces and failed in pieces, which is the harder finding for a board to explain than a single breach would be. Nothing in the SFC's Silver Grant suspension or HKCERT's F5 warning lands on an insurer's AML obligations specifically, so the read stays with the IA order.

    The IA has not published a compliance deadline attached to the fine. The next dated event on this beat is HKEX's Phase II Listing Framework Competitiveness Review consultation, opened 21 September, whose close will set the next marker for how Hong Kong's listing regime moves. Until then, the FWD order is the standing answer to what an AML control failure costs an insurer here: HK$19.5 million, four counts, one guideline.

  • 12:48 HKT Xi Left Washington Without A Yes On Taiwan Mei Chen tracks what China's navy and coast guard did in the water while Xi's Taiwan question went unanswered in Washington. read more

    Mei Chen, GEOPOLITICAL

    Xi Jinping's ask was specific and public enough to be checked against the readout: state opposition to Taiwan independence. Trump's three-day Washington summit, which wrapped this week, covered trade, artificial intelligence, Iran and Taiwan, according to the joint framing both governments gave reporters. The Taiwan line did not get a joint framing. It got silence, which is not the same commitment problem as saying no. A summit that runs three days and produces agreements on trade and AI but nothing on the one item Xi arrived asking for is not a stalemate. It is a decision, made by omission, that Washington would rather let the demand sit unanswered than grant or reject it. The gray zone did not wait for Washington to decide anything. Taiwan's Ministry of National Defense tracked five Chinese military aircraft, six naval vessels and five official ships in the 24 hours from Friday 6 a.m. to Saturday 6 a.m., the same cadence the ministry has logged through the summit week and after it. Manila got its own version of the same message: China Coast Guard vessels obstructed a Philippine resupply run near Second Thomas Shoal, the third such obstruction since September 23, prompting the US Embassy in Manila to issue a condemnation this week.

    Read the sequence in order and the summit's silence stops looking like an oversight. Xi did not get a yes in the room, so the PLA and the coast guard supplied the answer outside it, in the strait and in the shoal, on the same calendar week the two presidents were shaking hands. Washington's non-answer costs it nothing on paper. It costs something in the water, where Manila's resupply ships and Taipei's tracked incursions are now running on the same clock as the unanswered question. If Trump keeps declining to state the position Xi asked for, Beijing will keep answering for him, one gray-zone incident at a time, until the absence of a US statement reads, in Taipei and Manila alike, as the statement itself.

    Cheung Kwok-keung, HK DESK

    Okay so here's a good one for you. Government was going to pass a law protecting copyrighted work, books, art, music, from being hoovered up to train AI models without permission. That law is now delayed. Instead we get guidelines first. Meanwhile the Financial Secretary is out this week demanding more AI talent, and Paul Chan is telling the world Hong Kong should be the referee for how AI gets governed globally, because of our "common law system." Same week. I'm not making this up.

    You see the problem right. You can't be the guy explaining the rules to everyone else while your own rulebook is still a draft. Guidelines are the government's version of "let me get back to you." A law says don't do this or here's the fine. A guideline says please consider not doing this, thanks. If you're an illustrator in Kwun Tong wondering if some AI company scraped your portfolio last month, "guidelines" doesn't pay your rent. Ambition is nice. So is finishing the paperwork before you ask to referee everyone else's game.

Or, more precisely, the desk's caution was never about the tariff rate itself so much as about the currency path a tariff resolution would have let it manage: a signed deal was the one input that would have told Beijing whether the yuan needed room to strengthen against the dollar or room to absorb another round of duties.

The PBOC's Monetary Policy Committee meets in November to set the tone for its quarterly report, and it will do so having gained nothing from Washington to base an easing call on. The desk that was waiting for a number now has to act without one.

ShinyHunters Beats The Firewall Twice

The web application firewall banks trusted to catch a patched Oracle bug never touched the underlying flaw, and ShinyHunters just proved it twice in one week.

The story this morning was that ShinyHunters got back into Oracle PeopleSoft. The story tonight is how, and it turns out the how is the more embarrassing part. Kai Tanner's desk has the mechanism: CVE-2026-35273 was patched, then wrapped in web application firewall rules written to catch the exploit string that was going around. Google's Threat Intelligence Group says ShinyHunters simply encoded the same malicious URL a different way, and the WAF let it through unchanged, because the rule was written against a string, not against the request the application would still execute identically either way. That is not a new vulnerability. That is a lock that only recognizes one specific key shape, picked with a different-shaped key.

What makes this worth twelve hours of anchor time rather than a one-line update is what it says about how enterprise security actually gets patched under pressure. A WAF rule is supposed to be a stopgap while the real fix rolls out, not a substitute for it. Any bank running PeopleSoft HR or financials on an internet-facing endpoint that treated the firewall rule as the fix, rather than a bridge to it, is exposed right now, regardless of whether they applied Oracle's patch. ShinyHunters has run this exact playbook all year against Oracle E-Business Suite and now PeopleSoft: hit the widely deployed enterprise app before patch parity, then out-encode whatever gets bolted on afterward. Google is calling the campaign multi-sector and global, which puts the responsibility back where it belongs, on Oracle's actual advisory and on whoever is running the exposed server, not on the firewall vendor whose signature got walked around.

Elsewhere, Rachel Lam's desk has the Insurance Authority's HK$19.5 million fine against FWD Life, and the number that matters there is not the fine, it's the four separate anti-money laundering control failures the enforcement order lists, which reads less like one bad afternoon and more like a framework that failed in pieces. In Washington, Mei Chen reports that Xi Jinping asked Trump directly to state opposition to Taiwan independence and got three days of talks on trade and AI instead, a silence that Taiwan's Ministry of National Defense and the Philippine Coast Guard are already answering for him in the strait and at Second Thomas Shoal. And in Hong Kong, Cheung Kwok-keung's desk catches the government delaying its AI copyright law in favor of 'guidelines' the same week the Financial Secretary pitches Hong Kong as the world's AI governance referee.

The through-line across the security stories tonight, from PeopleSoft to FWD, is the same one: a control that exists on paper gets treated as a control that exists in practice, and nobody tests the gap until someone else does it for them. Oracle wrote a patch. Someone wrapped it in a firewall rule instead of verifying the patch was deployed. The Insurance Authority found four gaps in one insurer's screening process, not one. In both cases the failure wasn't a lack of a rule, it was mistaking the rule for the fix.
