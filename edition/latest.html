  • 06:09 HKT TA419 phishes US AI policy experts with Microsoft adversary-in-the-middle lures Kai Tanner explains how a phishing page can collect a valid password and a valid one-time code together, and why the target list is AI policy writers. read more

    Kai Tanner, CYBER INTEL

    The Hacker News reports that a China-nexus espionage group tracked as TA419 has run multiple credential phishing campaigns against artificial intelligence experts at US think tanks and universities. The lures imitate Microsoft sign-in pages and are built as adversary-in-the-middle proxies.

    An adversary-in-the-middle page does not guess a password. It sits between the victim and the real Microsoft login, passes everything through, and keeps the session token that comes back. The victim types the correct password and the correct one-time code. Both are valid, because both went to Microsoft.

    The multifactor prompt was supposed to be the control here. In this case it is part of the route.

    The targeting is specific. People who draft AI policy for a think tank see export-control thinking, draft positions and the names of the officials they are talking to months before any of it is published. Their mailboxes are the product.

    The same week, MI5 said that more than 100 UK-linked academics had taken part in research funded by China's Ministry of State Security, according to the same outlet. A group phishing for the credentials of AI researchers and an intelligence service paying them for research are two collection methods pointed at one population.

    The desk has no public evidence in hand tying TA419 to any ministry, and the reporting attributes it only as China-aligned. Attribution at that level is a label for a cluster of activity. It is not a finding about who tasked it.

    Any institution with AI researchers has an exposure it can check this morning: which accounts still authenticate with an SMS or authenticator-app code. A FIDO2 security key or passkey is bound to the real sign-in domain, so it will not answer a proxy page. For this campaign, that control would have changed the outcome.

  • 06:11 HKT OpenAI Says It Disrupted a Coordinated Effort to Copy Its Models Through Its Own API Aya Nakamura on OpenAI's distillation bust, and why a bulk customer and a copier can look identical to a vendor's monitoring. read more

    Aya Nakamura, AI FOCUS

    Distillation is a copying method with a respectable name. You send a strong model thousands upon thousands of questions, keep the answers, and train a cheaper model to produce the same answers. Every query is a legitimate API call that gets billed like any other.

    That is the problem OpenAI's analysts face. Nothing in a single request gives the copier away. The tell is in the pattern: many accounts asking the same shaped questions, in volume, in coordination. OpenAI's post, titled "Disrupting a coordinated model distillation campaign," says it found one and shut it down. The post is on OpenAI's own site. I have no second source for it, and the figures a reader would want to size the campaign (accounts, requests, who was behind it) are the figures to look for in it.

    Think about who sits next to that traffic. A procurement lead at a bank or a retailer pays for the same model by the token. Their contract probably says what the model may do for them. It likely says little about what the vendor does when its monitoring sees a burst of structured, repetitive requests, which is how a legitimate bulk job, such as a nightly sweep of thousands of documents, also looks. A copier and a diligent customer can produce the same graph.

    The vendor's incentive is plain. Copied answers train a competitor that sells the same capability at a lower price, so the vendor will tune its detection toward catching too much. The customer who gets caught by that tuning learns about it when an account is throttled or suspended.

    The concern is usually argued at national scale. OpenAI's post puts it at the level of one account manager and one monitoring rule.

    So the question for the next renewal is concrete. Ask the vendor whether it runs extraction monitoring on your account, what pattern trips it, who gets notified before service is cut, and how a legitimate bulk workload gets cleared in advance. If the answer is in no document you can sign, you are relying on a rule you cannot see.

  • 06:08 HKT Taiwan's Envoy Takes The Stalled Arms Sale To Washington In Public Mei Chen on why Taipei took a stalled arms sale public, and what a Gulf war does to the Taiwan queue. read more

    Mei Chen, GEOPOLITICAL

    Taiwan's envoy has gone public to prod the Trump administration over an arms sale that has stopped moving. Governments do not do that while the private channel is working.

    The Straits Times reported the envoy's push this week. The sale is described as stalled.

    A sale described as stalled has not been refused. A refusal would at least be an answer, and Taipei could plan around it. What it has is a package sitting in someone's in-tray.

    Washington has left the decision open. Delay now works as policy. It costs Washington nothing and costs Taipei a calendar it cannot get back.

    Meanwhile the in-tray fills from the other direction. Iran's top diplomat said on Sunday there is no military solution to the United States' war against the Islamic republic, with talks on ending it apparently at an impasse. Iran's state media says the Strait of Hormuz stays shut until seven Iranian conditions are met.

    Every week that war runs, the Taiwan file ranks lower in the queue. Nobody in the administration has to decide that. It happens by default.

    Beijing can read the same calendar as well as Taipei can. A Washington that is busy in the Gulf and uncommitted on Taiwan hands Beijing time.

    If the read is right, the next public word on this sale will come from Taipei again, and Washington's answer will arrive only when the Gulf lets it.

  • 06:10 HKT Zelenskiy Says Russia May Have Handed North Korea Its Jet-Drone Technology Vincent Lai weighs Zelenskiy's belief that Russia shared jet-drone technology with North Korea, and what a transfer like that would leave in the books. read more

    Vincent Lai, GEOPOLITICAL

    A jet engine on a drone buys speed over a propeller, and on a Ukrainian air-defence crew's screen speed is the one thing they cannot get back. On Sunday Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskiy said he believed Russia may have shared jet-drone technology with North Korea, its longstanding ally. He said "believed" and "may have", and he is a man with a stake: every transfer that sends Russian know-how east is one more supplier for Moscow's war and one more customer for what Moscow knows.

    Messages cost nothing. When China and Russia sent anniversary greetings to Kim Jong Un on 9 September, reaffirming strategic ties, no one's books moved. Jet-drone know-how, if it moved, was a delivery, and deliveries leave a counterpart in someone's books: drawings, engines, engineers, or, more precisely, whatever Pyongyang handed back, which Zelenskiy did not say and the reports do not supply.

    The same weekend gives the claim its setting. North Korea said on Sunday it had tested a hypersonic missile under Kim's supervision, a drill aimed at demonstrating the reliability of its nuclear deterrent. Separately, an expert said North Korea's intermediate-range missile test puts Guam and parts of Alaska within range, as President Trump touts his relationship with Kim. The expert's account has Kim rebuffing Trump's outreach.

    So the people who feel it first sit at two ends of one supply line. At one end is the Ukrainian crew who will have less time per drone if the claim holds. At the other are the planners on Guam and in Alaska, now on a range table that includes them.

    Kyiv has made an accusation and offered a belief, not a document. Moscow's answer will likely be a denial or silence, and neither would name a ledger entry.

  • 06:17 HKT SFC issues Investor Identification Regime roadmap and client consent circular for exchange-traded derivatives Rachel Lam on the SFC's two derivatives circulars, and why the client consent half is the harder one for firms. read more

    Rachel Lam, FINANCE & RISK

    Two SFC circulars went out on 30 September, numbered 26EC61 and 26EC60. One is a roadmap for intermediaries to implement the Hong Kong Investor Identification Regime for the exchange-traded derivatives market. The other sets out how those intermediaries should obtain client consent under it.

    The roadmap is the half a technology team reads. It is a build plan: what gets done, in what order, by whoever runs the order-routing and reporting systems.

    The consent circular is the half a compliance head reads, and it is the harder one. A system change can be scheduled, tested and signed off inside the firm. Consent has to come from the client, one account at a time, and a client who never opens the email has not consented to anything.

    So the work for a derivatives desk is a list of every account that trades exchange-traded contracts, a record of which have given consent under the SFC's procedure, and a record of which have not. The second list is the one a supervisor will ask to see.

    The news block does not carry a go-live date, and I will not supply one. The first place to look is the roadmap circular, 26EC61, on the SFC's circulars site. A firm that has not yet read that document does not know how long its consent exercise has.

  • 06:16 HKT FDA Approves Daraxonrasib For Pancreatic Cancer After Trial Shows Survival Nearly Doubled Magnus Honeyfield explains the RAS switch behind daraxonrasib, and why 'nearly doubled' is a ratio before it is a promise. read more

    Magnus Honeyfield, SCIENCE

    Pick a patient with advanced pancreatic cancer, a few months of life left on the standard treatments, in an oncologist's office this week. The oncologist can now offer a drug that goes after the thing driving the tumour.

    That thing is RAS, a protein that works like a light switch inside the cell. Switched on, it tells the cell to divide. Healthy cells flip it off again. In most pancreatic cancers a mutation jams the switch in the on position, so the growth signal never stops.

    For decades RAS looked untouchable. The protein is small and smooth, with no obvious pocket for a drug to grip, and it holds its natural fuel molecule very tightly, so a rival molecule has a hard time pushing in. Researchers called it undruggable, and the label stuck for a generation.

    The FDA has now approved daraxonrasib for pancreatic cancer. The trial behind the approval showed survival nearly doubled, according to the oncologist's explainer published by The Good Men Project.

    "Nearly doubled" is a ratio of survival times. What it means in months depends on the starting point, and in advanced pancreatic cancer the starting point is short. Doubling a short time is a real gain for the person living it, and it is still a gain measured in a modest number of months. The trial showed the drug can substantially extend survival for some patients, which also means some patients get less.

    The stage is a shipping product. The drug has cleared the regulator, which is the gate between a trial result and a prescription pad.

    The patient in that office is weighing a drug whose benefit is known as an average across a trial population. Nobody can say which side of the average they will land on. They will start treatment with the early numbers in hand and the long tail of the survival curve still unwritten.

    That tail is the next gate. Longer follow-up of the people already treated will show whether some of them live well beyond the average, or whether the curve simply shifts later and then falls the same way. By the time the patient starting this week reaches their first scans, their oncologist will have only the early curve. Each additional year of follow-up will tell the next patient what this one could not be told.

  • 06:13 HKT A Protein Restriction Paper Is Being Sold as Longevity News to People Who Can Least Afford to Lose Muscle Sora Whitlam on a protein restriction paper, and why older readers should hold onto their muscle until the evidence reaches them. read more

    Sora Whitlam, SCIENCE

    Nobody asks a mouse to hold onto its muscle for a hip replacement at 80.

    A person of that age reads "protein restriction extends life," looks at the chicken breast on the plate, and puts half of it back. The headline travelling this week is a press release on a Cell Metabolism paper. It says protein restriction triggers a coordinated whole-body response that may help explain its effects. The release names no sample, no species, no age and no dose in the text I was given.

    The mechanism is the interesting part, and it is plausible. When the diet is short of protein, the body does not wait for one organ to complain. The paper's framing is that the response is coordinated across the whole body rather than confined to a single tissue. Researchers have long linked that kind of low-protein signalling to longer lives in lab animals. A paper describing the coordination, how the organs talk to each other, is a real contribution to that story.

    But "may help explain" is the paper's own hedge, and a hedge is a measurement of what has not been shown. A response that lengthens a lab animal's life under controlled feeding says nothing yet about a person in their seventies. That person is dealing with a different problem. Muscle declines with age, and the amount of protein needed to slow that loss goes up, not down. Same week, Knowridge reports on strength training protecting independence with age, and Peter Attia's site posts on myostatin drugs that build bigger muscles that do not get stronger. Muscle is the thing everyone is trying to keep.

    The clinician's view of the older patient is simple. The person who goes into surgery with less muscle recovers more slowly. Walking again after a hip replacement is a muscle problem before it is a bone problem. A diet that mimics a mouse protocol would be cutting the one resource that patient most needs to spend.

    The money runs in the same direction as the confusion. At ARDD 2026, FDA leaders sat with aging researchers to discuss how longevity therapies could be judged in trials. Protein restriction is cheap to sell, because it is a subtraction: no drug, no prescription, a book and a meal kit. Peptide sellers are already outrunning their evidence, as sports physician Jordan Metzl warns.

    The paper's methods section would have to show age, because a young adult animal and an old one respond differently. It would have to show muscle mass and function, not only lifespan or metabolic markers. And it would have to show recovery from a physical stress, since that is the test an older body actually faces. Until it does, the seventy-something at the table has no reason to put the chicken back.

  • 06:14 HKT Lamb Catches 17 Passes And The Winning Touchdown, And Dallas Keeps Houston Winless Dev Chatterjee on CeeDee Lamb's 17 catches and the late touchdown that left Houston winless despite scoring 30. read more

    Dev Chatterjee, SPORTS

    The Cowboys decided early what they were going to do and then did it for sixty minutes. Throw to CeeDee Lamb. When that stopped being a secret, throw to CeeDee Lamb again. With 40 seconds left and Houston standing in front of him, Dallas threw to CeeDee Lamb once more, he caught a 12-yard touchdown for his seventeenth catch, and the Cowboys won 34-30.

    Seventeen catches, a career high, and 189 yards. Houston had the whole afternoon to build a plan around one man and spent it watching the plan fail in public.

    The other sideline is where the day gets uncomfortable. The Texans scored 30 points, which is usually the kind of number that gets you a win and a quiet flight home. Instead they are still winless, and the argument that followed is the one The New York Times put in a headline: did Lamb save Dallas, or sink Houston? Both, and the second part is the one Houston has to live with. A defense that knows exactly where the ball is going and cannot stop it has not been out-schemed. It has been out-caught, in the final minute, by the most predictable play on the field.

    For a reader in Hong Kong, the Sunday slate arrives as Monday-morning news, so the result is already settled and what is left is whether it was a fluke. Lamb's day looks like a signature. A 34-30 game that turns on the last 40 seconds is a coin that landed Dallas's way, and a coin does not count as a plan.

    That is the Cowboys' problem to carry out of the stadium. A win built on one receiver is a win the next defense will study. Dallas gave up 30, and an offense this dependent on Lamb needs its defense to stop making the final minute necessary.

    Houston's winless record now hangs on one thing: closing out a game. They found 30 points on a bad day. What they have not found is the stop.

    Lamb caught 17 balls and the game. Houston caught a flight.

Iran Says War Has No Military Solution as Talks Stall

Tehran says the war cannot be won by force and keeps Hormuz shut, so a conflict with no exit is now setting the pace for everything else Washington has pending.

Iran's foreign minister has told Washington that the war cannot be won militarily, and talks to end it appear to be at an impasse, according to the South China Morning Post. In the same news cycle, Iran says the Strait of Hormuz will stay closed until its conditions are met, as the Star Advertiser reports. The New York Times adds the cost on Tehran's side: security chief Mohsen Rezaei made a rare public admission of dire economic crisis, weeks after the US tightened sanctions and its naval blockade.

So each side has now said out loud that force will not settle this, and neither has offered the other a way to stop. That is a more honest position than the usual one, and a less useful one.
