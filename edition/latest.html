    Mei Chen, GEOPOLITICAL

    More imagery still has to earn its place in a warning.

    Taiwan's Ministry of National Defense, responsible for tracking Chinese military activity, recorded nine naval vessels and seven official ships between Thursday morning and Friday morning. Its tally kept the categories separate.

    For the Taiwanese staff receiving that report, the distinction matters before another image arrives. The official ships cannot simply be counted as additional warships. They cannot be dropped from the assessment either. The work is deciding what their presence adds to the naval deployment.

    The published tally establishes presence. It supplies no account of coordination between those ships. Adding the columns would produce a bigger number and a poorer description.

    Taiwan now plans AI-powered satellite analysis to spot Chinese ships and missile activity. That proposal enters an existing assessment problem: deciding which observations belong together.

    In September, this paper examined Chinese aircraft and vessel movements alongside military talks involving Washington. The sequence carried meaning that the count alone could not establish.

    For the Taiwan Strait, the proposed analysis earns its place when it helps defence staff decide whether a deployment warrants a warning before the next daily tally arrives.

Washington Proposes More Russian Gas Sales To Europe

A proposed Ukraine peace deal would expand Russia's European gas business, asking skeptical European governments to accept an economic benefit for Moscow.

The Trump administration is proposing more Russian gas sales to Europe as part of a Ukraine peace deal, The New York Times reports. Following President Donald Trump's waiver of sanctions on Russia, Washington is putting a further economic benefit for Moscow into the discussion. The proposal has drawn European skepticism. Europe is being asked to consider buying more from the country whose war the agreement would seek to end.

The sanctions decision supplies the immediate context. According to the Associated Press, lawmakers angered by Trump's waiver see no quick way to reverse it. Their objection therefore comes with a practical limit: Congress can protest a decision it cannot readily undo. The gas proposal takes the argument beyond Washington. Selling more Russian gas to Europe requires European acceptance, and skepticism is the response reported so far. A proposal from the White House does not itself create a European customer. Even diplomacy needs a buyer.

That distinction matters for Ukraine. Volodymyr Zelensky has asked European Union leaders for another $79 billion next year to sustain the war effort, as the South China Morning Post reported in the previous edition. Europe is already being asked to commit more money to Ukraine. Washington's gas proposal would also have it consider an arrangement expanding Russian sales. Those are different decisions, but they belong to the same peace negotiation: how economic commitments might change the positions of the countries fighting, and of the governments supporting them.

The reported development is a proposal, with European skepticism attached, rather than an agreed settlement. Its significance is nevertheless clear. Washington is offering Russia the prospect of more European business as part of ending the war. European governments would have to accept that bargain for the gas element to work. Trump's sanctions waiver demonstrates his ability to change American policy; it does not establish Europe's willingness to finance the terms he proposes.
