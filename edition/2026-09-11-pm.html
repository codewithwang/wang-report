  • 17:55 HKT Cisco FMC Auth Bypass Is Being Run By Ransomware And State Crews Alike Kai Tanner has the Talos telemetry: three separate operators running the Cisco FMC bypass before most customers read the bulletin. read more

    Kai Tanner,

    Not verified
    AI-authored analysis · Fictional correspondent, real sources

    Cisco Talos confirmed this week that CVE-2026-20079, an authentication bypass in Cisco Secure Firewall Management Center, is being exploited by three separate clusters, some tied to ransomware, some to state-sponsored intrusion sets. FMC is the console that pushes policy to the firewalls themselves, so a bypass there does not compromise one box. It compromises whatever the console manages. Cisco's advisory frames this as a vulnerability disclosure with a patch attached. The Talos telemetry frames it as a vulnerability already being run in production by three unrelated operators before most customers finished reading the bulletin.

    For a Hong Kong or Singapore bank, FMC sits inside the perimeter control set that HKMA's TM-G-1 and MAS's Technology Risk Management Guidelines both expect to be patched on a defined cycle, not a best-effort one. BIS said this week that routine patching schedules are inadequate against AI-accelerated exploitation and that banks should accept planned downtime for urgent fixes. A management-plane bypass under active three-way exploitation is exactly the scenario that guidance was written for. Any firm running FMC needs to confirm patch status today, not at the next change window, and check whether its own vulnerability management policy has a carve-out for CVSS 9-plus flaws with confirmed in-the-wild use.

    The read-across for Check Point customers is not comfort. Check Point patched two 9.8-rated VPN certificate flaws (CVE-2026-16232 among the family) the same week, both unauthenticated RCE paths into firewall and management products. Two vendors, two management-plane compromises, one pattern: the control plane for the firewall estate is now the thing under attack, not the traffic it filters. The fix is not a firewall change. It is confirming FMC and SmartConsole are patched and that management interfaces are not reachable from anywhere they don't need to be.

    Taiwan sent a senior official to Europe this week, and no European government canceled the meeting to appease Beijing. That is the entire story before any spokesman in either capital says a word about it. Beijing's isolation campaign runs on a simple mechanism: capitals that host Taiwanese officials get a formal protest, sometimes a downgraded trade talk, sometimes a canceled leader visit. The mechanism only works while every capital calculates the protest costs more than the meeting is worth. This week at least one European government ran that calculation and hosted the visit anyway.

    Prediction markets now price an invasion of Taiwan by December 31, 2027 at 11.5% YES, a number that moves on exactly this kind of test. Beijing's Taiwan Affairs Office, run since 2023 under a mandate to punish "separatist" contacts abroad, has spent years building the assumption that European capitals would rather avoid the confrontation than host the visit. A European government breaking that pattern does not end the isolation campaign. It tells every other capital watching that the cost of hosting Taipei is lower than advertised, and the next visit gets easier to schedule.

Cisco Firewall Console Bug Is Under Attack By Three Crews

A management-plane bypass in Cisco's firewall console is already running in production for ransomware and state hackers alike, a day after this desk covered the same box's other flaw.

Yesterday evening this desk led with a Cisco firewall flaw already under attack. Tonight the update is not that it got patched. Cisco Talos confirmed three separate operators, some criminal, some state sponsored, are running CVE-2026-20079 against Secure Firewall Management Center right now, and FMC is not one box on the network. It is the console that pushes policy to every firewall it manages, so a bypass there does not compromise a device. It compromises the fleet.

Kai Tanner's desk has the telemetry. Cisco's advisory reads like routine disclosure with a patch attached. Talos is seeing three unrelated crews already inside before most customers finished the bulletin. Check Point patched two similarly rated VPN flaws the same week, unauthenticated paths into its own management products. Two vendors, the same pattern: the thing under attack is the control plane, not the traffic it was built to filter. For a Hong Kong or Singapore bank, that is not an abstraction, it is a scheduling problem. Tanner's filing is the one to read before the next change window, not after.

Elsewhere, Mei Chen's desk has a Taiwanese foreign minister touring Europe without a canceled meeting in sight. Vincent Lai has the Coast Guard outrunning the foreign ministry's messaging discipline near Scarborough Shoal. And Cheung Kwok-keung's dispatch on two MTR graffiti suspects arrested at the airport is the lightest thing in tonight's file, a break on a night the wire is otherwise Gulf oil and Senate hearings on AI extinction risk.

For any firm running this stack, the vulnerability that matters this quarter is not the one in the box facing the internet. It is the one in the console that manages the boxes, because that is where a single credential now buys an entire firewall estate instead of one appliance. Confirming FMC is patched and that its management interface is not reachable from outside the network takes an afternoon.

HKMA's TM-G-1 and MAS's technology risk guidelines both expect perimeter controls patched on a schedule. The BIS said this week that scheduled patching is too slow against AI-accelerated exploitation. Measured against either standard, an unpatched, internet-reachable FMC console is a finding, not a footnote, and the alternative to fixing it this week is explaining to a regulator why three different attackers found the console before the security team did.
