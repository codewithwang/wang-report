North Korea Steals $351.6 Million From Bitget

North Korean operators drained Bitget's hot and warm wallets in the year's largest single crypto theft, and Hong Kong regulators filed two more reminders that patch cycles are not optional.

Bitget disclosed today that suspected North Korean operators pulled $351.6 million out of its hot and warm wallets, the ones an exchange keeps connected so funds can move fast. That connectivity is also what made them reachable, and the amount alone puts this above nearly every other reported theft attributed to North Korean state operators this year. Kai Tanner's desk has the full trail: this filing lands six days after four governments jointly named WaterPlum, a separate North Korean recruiting scheme that had already pulled $10.71 million from 7,000 wallets over eight months before anyone put a name to it. Bitget has not detailed how the intruders got in. North Korean operators have targeted hot wallet infrastructure at exchanges repeatedly this year, and regulators in Bitget's operating jurisdictions now have a nine figure number to reconcile against whatever custody standard they thought they had.

Hong Kong's Insurance Authority fined FWD Life HK$19.5 million this week for four separate anti money laundering control failures, roughly HK$4.9 million per failure, under the customer due diligence and monitoring rules that never show up in an embedded value presentation until they do. Rachel Lam's desk has that filing. HKCERT separately warned on CVE-2026-94127, a remote code execution flaw in F5 BIG-IP already being exploited in the wild, with no fixed remediation date for firms still running it unpatched.

Cheung Kwok-keung's desk has a third scene: an underground pipe outside Woodland Garden on MacDonnell Road blew at 1am, sending a two metre column of saltwater into the lobby of one of the pricier addresses in the Mid-Levels. Nobody was hurt. The marble floor was not so lucky. The Chief Executive spent this week on the radio describing his five year vision for the city; the pipes under one of its most established streets are old enough to erupt without warning.

Bitget chose liquidity over custody and paid $351.6 million for it. FWD chose growth metrics over AML rigor and paid HK$19.5 million. Hong Kong's Water Supplies Department left an ageing main unreplaced, and paid in soaked marble instead of money. A hot wallet, a compliance programme, and a hundred year old pipe all failed the same way: nobody monitored them until they broke.

IA's HK$19.5 Million Fine Sets FWD's AML Bar

Rachel Lam breaks down what HK$19.5 million per AML failure actually buys the Insurance Authority in deterrence.

Continue reading

The Insurance Authority fined FWD Life HK$19.5 million for four anti-money laundering control failures, setting the compliance floor every HK-licensed insurer now has to clear.

Look, the Insurance Authority fined FWD Life Insurance HK$19.5 million this week for four separate anti-money laundering control failures, and HKCERT flagged a remote code execution flaw in F5 BIG-IP, CVE-2026-94127, already being exploited in the wild. Neither story is really about the headline number. FWD's fine is the IA telling every authorized insurer in Hong Kong what "four failures" costs when it's your AML programme, not your investment performance, that gets graded. HKCERT's warning is the kind of notice that sits in a bulletin until it sits in an incident report (the gap between the two is usually measured in patch cycles nobody tracked).

The FWD fine lands squarely on IA, GL3, the Guideline on Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorist Financing: customer due diligence, ongoing monitoring, suspicious transaction reporting under the AMLO, the stuff that doesn't show up in an embedded-value presentation but shows up in an enforcement notice. HK$19.5 million against four control failures works out to roughly HK$4.9 million per failure (the IA didn't itemize which control cost what, which is its own tell about how these calculations get built). For the F5 exposure, there's no insurer-specific module that covers a network appliance vulnerability directly, so this sits with whatever authorized institution or insurer runs BIG-IP in its infrastructure stack, and the obligation is the general one: patch a known-exploited CVE before the regulator asks why you didn't.

The IA doesn't publish a compliance deadline for firms watching the FWD enforcement action, only the fact of the fine dated September 24. HKCERT's advisory carries no fixed remediation date either, just the standing expectation that a flaw already being exploited gets patched now, not on the next quarterly cycle. FWD's HK$19.5 million is this week's number; the F5 vulnerability is next week's incident report if nobody moved on it.

Woodland Garden Salt Water Main Bursts, Floods Lobby

Cheung Kwok-keung on a Mid-Levels lobby flooded by a pipe older than the five-year plan meant to fix it.

Continue reading

A ruptured underground main on MacDonnell Road sent a saltwater column into a Mid-Levels lobby at 1am, the kind of infrastructure failure that never makes the five-year plan speeches.

At about 1am Friday, an underground salt water main outside Woodland Garden on 10 MacDonnell Road burst. The pipe sent a column of water roughly two metres high straight into the building's lobby. Security guards and residents got soaked cleaning it up before the morning rush. Nobody was hurt, but the marble floor took a beating.

Here's the thing about Mid-Levels. Rent there is not cheap, and MacDonnell Road is about as established as HK addresses get. Old pipes don't care how much your flat costs. The Chief Executive was on the radio this week talking up his five-year vision for the city. Sounds great. Meanwhile the actual pipes under one of the priciest streets in town are old enough to blow a two-metre geyser into someone's lobby at 1am. Fix the boring stuff first. Nobody claps for a new water main, but everybody notices when the old one fails.

Falcons End Packers' Home-Opener Streak

Dev Chatterjee on how Atlanta ended Green Bay's 13-year home-opener streak while October's real seeding quietly locked in.

Continue reading

Atlanta's win over Green Bay landed the same week the Dodgers and Brewers clinched their way into a postseason built to make sure the biggest media windows fall in October, not September.

Atlanta beat Green Bay on Thursday night, and the number that matters is thirteen: consecutive home openers the Packers had won before Bijan Robinson and Drake London walked into Lambeau and ended it. Robinson and London did the damage on the ground and through the air. Jordan Love and a Green Bay offense that looked settled in August looked lost by the fourth quarter. Thirteen years is a long time to hold a streak nobody outside Wisconsin was tracking closely, which is exactly why losing it stings more than the box score suggests.

Meanwhile the actual pennant races quietly finished themselves. The Dodgers clinched a top-two National League seed and a first-round bye before they even played Thursday, courtesy of the Braves losing to the Reds. The Brewers hit 100 wins the same night, beating Philadelphia and slowing the Phillies' own charge toward October. Cleveland clinched a playoff spot for the third straight year. None of that is an accident of scheduling. Major League Baseball's postseason format rewards the teams that lock up seeding early with rest, and rest is the one asset money can't buy back once the games start counting. Atlanta gets a signature win in Week 3. The Dodgers get something better: a bye week nobody has to explain to the fans.
