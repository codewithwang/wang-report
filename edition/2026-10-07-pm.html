Here is today's evening report. Wed, Oct 7, 18:11 HKT

  • 13:04 HKT Dozens evacuated from Mong Kok building after rooftop fire, no cause or injuries reported Cheung Kwok-keung has the Mong Kok rooftop fire and the one part of every building that everybody agrees is for emergencies and nobody keeps clear. read more

    Cheung Kwok-keung, HK DESK

    Every Hong Kong stairwell I know has a pile of boxes in it, and the landlord always says it is only temporary. Think about that when you read that dozens of people fled a Mong Kok rooftop blaze on Tuesday.

    RTHK reported the fire and the evacuation. We have no cause and no injury details. So what we know is simple: a fire on the roof, and a lot of people who had to get down and out.

    Now picture it. Smoke above you, a neighbour in front of you, and one narrow stairwell. You are not thinking about fire safety. You are thinking about the person with the shopping trolley.

    That stairwell is the one part of the building everyone agrees is for emergencies. It is also the part everyone uses as a cupboard. Old fans. Flattened cardboard. A rice cooker nobody will admit to owning. Each pile looks harmless on its own. Together they are the reason inspectors keep writing the same notice.

    Nobody plans to block an exit. It just happens one box at a time, and the exit shrinks with it.

    The Observatory has a fire danger warning in force today, 7 October. It is 27°C with 56% humidity (our city strip). Nobody will tell you to stay home, but it is dry, and dry is when roofs and rubbish catch.

    So go and look at your own stairwell tonight. If there is a pile of boxes in it, you now know who left it there. The boxes will probably outlast the fire warning.

  • 13:06 HKT Atlanta spent Sale's October on Philadelphia, and Yamamoto's Dodgers lead 2-1 Dev Chatterjee on how Atlanta spent its ace on Philadelphia and then met Yamamoto, with the Dodgers one win from the NLCS. read more

    Dev Chatterjee, SPORTS

    The heroic kind of ace usage gets applauded on the day and invoiced later. Chris Sale closed out Philadelphia in a four-out relief job on one day of rest, and on Tuesday the bill arrived in the form of Yoshinobu Yamamoto.

    That is the matchup everyone circled: Sale against the Dodgers' right-hander, Game 3 of a series that stood level at 1-1. Yamamoto dominated it. Kiké Hernández hit a two-run homer, Freddie Freeman was in the middle of the Dodgers' night, and Los Angeles won 3-1 to take a 2-1 series lead. One run is what Atlanta had to show for its ace's start. Our scoreboard has it final, Dodgers 3, Braves 1.

    Sale is not the reason Atlanta lost. The point is that Atlanta spent its best pitcher's October on a series it needed to win, won it, and arrived at the next one with the lineup that had to beat Yamamoto and did not. Sale's four outs against Philadelphia read as a gift to the bracket. Against the Dodgers they read as a loan.

    The Dodgers bet big on a Japanese right-hander and a Japanese designated hitter. Yamamoto is the half of the bet that showed up on the night it counted, against the other team's best.

    For anyone following from Hong Kong, this was a Wednesday-morning result: the game finished while the city slept, and the 3-1 was waiting on the phone like a bill slipped under the door.

    Game 4 is Thursday in Atlanta. The Dodgers need one win to reach the NLCS, and Atlanta needs a pitcher who has already thrown his October.

Trump's Remarks on Iran Striking Los Angeles Draw Criticism From Both Parties

The president's talk of Iranian strikes on American cities landed in a week when the war was already reaching allied soil, and objections came from his own side too.

President Trump's comments about Iran striking Los Angeles and San Diego have drawn criticism from both parties, The New York Times reports, while a White House official says he was quoted out of context. That is the usual defence, and it may even be accurate. It does not change what the sentence did once it left the room. A president naming American cities as targets of a country the United States is at war with is not a briefing. It is a headline, and the cities in it are home to millions of people.

The timing explains the volume. This week the Pentagon moved B-1 bombers out of RAF Fairford over what officials later described, through the Times, as a possible Iranian drone plot, and this morning's edition reported British police had arrested a seventh man over the incident. Washington has spent days showing that the war's reach extends well past the Gulf, one cautious statement at a time. Against that, a loose remark about California reads less like colour and more like an admission nobody in the building wanted on the record.

Separately, the Associated Press reports that six US soldiers were killed in the war's deadliest strike, and that most of their families did not attend a White House reception honouring Gold Star families. Families do not owe anyone an explanation for staying away.

Elsewhere, Cheung Kwok-keung's Hong Kong desk has the Mong Kok rooftop fire, where dozens were evacuated and the Observatory's fire danger warning remains in force today. Dev Chatterjee's sports desk has the Dodgers' 3-1 win over the Braves, which puts Los Angeles ahead 2-1 in the NLDS.

The point of the day is plain. A war that began as a matter of ships, bases and bombers is now being argued in terms of American cities and American families, and when members of both parties object to the same sentence, it means how the war is described has become as politically consequential as how it is fought.
