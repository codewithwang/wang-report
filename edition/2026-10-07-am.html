  • 06:14 HKT FBI blames a missed patch for ShinyHunters theft of staff data, names no system or flaw Kai Tanner explains why the FBI's answer to a stolen staff database was to remove one contractor, and what the contract should have said instead. read more

    Kai Tanner, CYBER INTEL

    The Hacker News reports that the FBI has removed an Accenture contractor for an alleged role in a ShinyHunters breach. The breach led to the theft of personal details of thousands of bureau employees. The stated cause is a patch failure.

    Read that sequence again. A criminal extortion group took the personnel data of the agency that investigates criminal extortion groups, and the cause on the record is an update that was available and was not applied.

    The account we have identifies the contractor's employer and does not name the system, the vulnerability or the number of days it sat unpatched. Those are the three facts a defender needs to test their own estate, and none of them is in what has been published. What is published is a personnel action.

    (A personnel action is the cheapest remediation on the menu. It closes the ticket without touching the process that assigned the ticket to someone.)

    The people on the receiving end are the employees. Their personal details, and whatever else the bureau held on them, are now in the hands of a group whose business is selling or leaking what it takes. They cannot rotate a home address.

    The same week, our CVE Intel feed shows six flaws from our tracked list added to CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue in the seven days to 6 October. Each one is a patch that someone, somewhere, is responsible for applying. In most organisations that someone is a contractor operating under a service level written before the flaw existed.

    The control that would have changed this outcome sits in the contract, not in the scanner. A patch deadline with a named owner, a weekly report of overdue critical fixes that goes to the client's own security team rather than back through the outsourcer, and a right to read the evidence. The FBI had a contractor to remove. Most organisations have only the contractor.

  • 06:13 HKT Ukraine fires its first FP-7 ballistic missile as Kyiv says Russia holds 600 Mei Chen on Ukraine's first FP-7 ballistic missile, and why Kyiv's figure of 600 Russian missiles reads as an interceptor shopping list. read more

    Mei Chen, GEOPOLITICAL

    Ukraine has joined the ballistic missile club in the same week it told the world how many ballistic missiles Russia has left to throw back. Euromaidan Press reports Kyiv fired its first FP-7 on Tuesday, day 1,685 of the war.

    The reply is already priced. Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha, in office as Ukraine's top diplomat, said last week that Kyiv estimates Russia is holding 600 ballistic missiles in reserve and can produce up to 140 a month.

    That is a stockpile with a replenishment rate attached.

    Ukraine's own strikes are not small. Russia says Ukraine launched its biggest strike of the war, 1,750 drones and 17 Flamingo missiles.

    A drone attack on the Moscow region killed two people and injured nine, according to emergency services cited by the Sunday Guardian.

    Kyiv also claims half of Russia's oil refining capacity is destroyed. That is Ukraine's own claim and the Guardian carries it as one. Boston Herald reporting says Kyiv is bracing for a large Russian reprisal.

    Taiwan's air defence planners do not need to guess what a ballistic reserve of 600 does to a magazine. Kyiv has just published the arithmetic for them.

    Every salvo Russia spends is an interceptor Ukraine's backers must find, and every interceptor found is one that is not available elsewhere, Taiwan included.

  • 06:17 HKT Nobody Tells You What to Eat on Day Three of the Antibiotics Sora Whitlam on a gut study the pharmacy counter never mentions, and why nobody has a reason to fund a trial of the plate. read more

    Sora Whitlam, SCIENCE

    The instructions at the pharmacy counter cover timing, whether to take it with food, and finishing the course. They say nothing about what goes on the plate. A new study suggests that silence may matter.

    The study, reported by CIDRAP, found that sugar-rich foods amplify the disruption antibiotics cause in the gut. Every patient in it was on antibiotics at some point during the study, and many received broad-spectrum drugs, the kind known to significantly disrupt the balance of good and bad bacteria. The study's sample size has not been published, and the work is described as suggesting a link, not proving one.

    A broad-spectrum antibiotic is a blunt instrument. It cannot tell the bacteria causing your infection from the thousands of species that were quietly digesting your lunch. The community thins out, and what survives inherits whatever food arrives next. Sugar is an easy food for gut bacteria to use, and the reading consistent with the study is that a depleted community fed sugar reorganises worse than one fed something slower. That is a hypothesis about mechanism. The coverage reports the association.

    Here is the part the pharmacy counter will not mention. An instruction to eat less sugar for the length of a course costs nothing, which may mean few have a reason to fund a trial of it. Drug makers pay for trials of pills. Probiotic sellers pay for trials of probiotics. A rule about the plate is unlikely to come with a sponsor or a price tag.

    The supplement aisle is not waiting for a sponsor. A survey of nearly 63,000 Americans, which we covered on 27 September, found probiotic and fiber supplement use rising while fermented-food and fiber intake stayed flat. People are already buying the capsule instead of changing the meal.

    No trial of the plate is attached to this finding. The patient on day three of a course will make the call alone, standing in front of the same shelf.

  • 06:15 HKT Yankees must win three straight after four errors give Tampa Bay a 5-2 Game 2 win Dev Chatterjee on four Yankees errors, a 5-2 loss, and a season now resting on Max Fried's left arm. read more

    Dev Chatterjee, SPORTS

    The Yankees lost Game 1 of the Division Series. They lost Game 2 by being unable to catch. Tampa Bay won 5-2 on Tuesday night, and the Rays' main contribution to the evening was standing there while it happened.

    Three errors in the first inning. Four for the game. ESPN called it a shocking display of postseason baseball, which is a polite phrase for a ballclub handing out gifts to a team that was already ahead in the series. A first inning like that has a sound. It is a stadium full of people going quiet one fielding mistake at a time.

    Consider the arithmetic of the series. In Game 2 the Yankees scored twice and defended like people meeting the infield for the first time. The Rays have had to be good for one of those nights. For the other they had to show up.

    Now the Yankees are 0-2 in a best-of-five, which means every remaining game is a funeral if they lose it. The Rays come to New York for Game 3 on Thursday, and Yahoo's headline hands Max Fried the job of saving the season. That is a lot to put on one left arm.

    Fried can throw a clean game. Whether the man behind him can field one is the part nobody has been able to promise since Tuesday.

British Police Arrest Seventh Man Over Iran-Linked RAF Fairford Security Incident

The Fairford case has moved from a military withdrawal to a criminal investigation on British soil, with arrests now giving the threat a human face.

British police have arrested a seventh man in connection with the security incident at RAF Fairford. He is 22, from London, and was detained on suspicion of terrorism offences, according to the South China Morning Post, which reports that authorities have linked the incident to Iran.

That is the part that moved overnight. The Pentagon has confirmed the bombers left, and the New York Times has reported that the Revolutionary Guard ordered a possible drone attack. Neither of those put anyone in custody. This does. Seven arrests make Fairford a criminal case run by British police, with suspects, charges to come and a court calendar, and not only a decision about where American aircraft should be parked. For a threat that Washington first described only as unspecified, it has acquired a remarkable number of addresses.

Elsewhere, the desks have filed. Kai Tanner's Cyber desk has the FBI's account of a ShinyHunters breach of its own employees' personal data. The bureau blames a missed patch and has removed an Accenture contractor, while naming no system and no flaw. Mei Chen reports from the Geopolitical desk that Ukraine has fired its first FP-7 ballistic missile, in the same week Kyiv put the size of Russia's ballistic reserve at 600. Sora Whitlam's Health desk asks what a patient should eat on day three of antibiotics, after a study reported by CIDRAP linked sugar-rich food to deeper gut disruption. And Dev Chatterjee's Sports desk has the Yankees, who committed four errors in Game 2 against Tampa Bay and now need three straight wins. Four errors in one game is generous, even by the standards of postseason hospitality.

What it means is simple enough. The Iran war has reached Britain in the form of police work. Washington has already decided its bombers are safer elsewhere, and British investigators are now establishing who was close enough to make that decision necessary.
