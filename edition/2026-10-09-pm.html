Here is today's evening report. Fri, Oct 9, 18:06 HKT

  • 13:01 HKT FBI seizes seven Flax Typhoon domains behind MicroScan and FishHub hacking tools Kai Tanner on why seizing seven domains cuts the scanner's reporting line but leaves every intrusion, and every exposed gateway, exactly where it was. read more

    Kai Tanner, CYBER INTEL

    The FBI has seized seven domains that Flax Typhoon, a Chinese state-sponsored group, used to run two tools called MicroScan and FishHub. Both were used in attacks that breached critical infrastructure, according to BleepingComputer's account of the seizure.

    The Record reports that the US and other nations also took down infrastructure belonging to Integrity Tech, a Beijing-based company. The infrastructure allowed what the coalition described as "widespread vulnerability scanning and, in some cases, intrusions."

    Read that sentence again. Vulnerability scanning is a product category. The company behind the tools, according to the coalition, ran it against other people's networks.

    A separate FBI notice, reported by The Hacker News, concerns hackers tied to a Chinese cybersecurity company. It says they stole email from government organisations, law enforcement agencies, healthcare systems and religious institutions in Southeast Asia, and ran a portal selling access to the stolen mail. A mailbox stops being a breach and becomes inventory. The people in it did not consent to being listed.

    CISA published its own advisory, aa26-281a, titled "Chinese State Hackers Blend Automated and Hands-On Tools to Steal Data." The title describes the working method. Scanners find the door. A person walks through it.

    That ordering is visible in our own feed. Four flaws on our tracked list entered CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue in the seven days to 9 October, among them the FortiMail path traversal (CVE-2026-104286) and the Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN Manager bypass (CVE-2026-76504). Both are internet-facing management or mail systems, the kind of box a scanner reaches before anyone has read the advisory.

    Seizing seven domains cuts the scanner's reporting path. It does not patch the Cisco device or the FortiMail gateway. Whoever already holds scan results can still read them.

    The control that changes the outcome sits on the defender's side of the glass. An internet-facing management interface, restricted to an allow-listed address range, returns nothing to a scanner that arrives from outside it. A patch window is optional for the scanner. An access list is not.

  • 13:00 HKT Huanggang border crossing opens Monday, and the first morning rush is the test Cheung Kwok-keung on Huanggang, which both governments and Immigration call ready for Monday, and why the first morning rush is the real inspection. read more

    Cheung Kwok-keung, HK DESK

    Both sides have now said the revamped Huanggang border crossing is ready. It opens Monday.

    Immigration said the same thing on Friday. So that is Hong Kong, Shenzhen and the officers at the counter, all saying "ready" before anyone has walked through.

    Look, I believe them. But "ready" is what a restaurant says at 5pm. The test is 7pm, when the table of twelve turns up without booking.

    For a border, that table is Monday morning. Everyone who has been waiting for this crossing will turn up together. They will all find out at the same moment whether the new layout makes sense.

    The reports we have do not say what changed inside. So I will not guess at it. What I can tell you is how it works at a crossing.

    The first week tells you more than any press release. Signs get argued over. Queues find the one gate nobody planned for. Someone's auntie, with two bags and a trolley, will find the bit nobody tested.

    If you cross on Monday, leave extra time and don't schedule anything tight. Day-one queues at a new crossing run long.

    The Observatory has no warnings in force today, 29°C. Use the weekend to check your travel documents.

FBI and Allies Seize Flax Typhoon Domains Tied to Beijing Firm

The takedown removes the scanning tools and names the company behind them, but it cannot recall the intrusions already made or the scan results already collected.

The FBI has seized seven domains that Flax Typhoon, a Chinese state-sponsored group, used to run two tools called MicroScan and FishHub, according to BleepingComputer. Both tools were used in attacks that breached critical infrastructure. The Record reports that the United States and other nations also took down infrastructure belonging to Integrity Tech, a Beijing-based company, which the coalition says enabled widespread vulnerability scanning and, in some cases, intrusions.

Kai Tanner's Cyber Intel Desk has the full account, and the detail worth noticing is the name. Governments usually attribute attacks to a group with a codename. Here the coalition has pointed at a company, and the thing it is said to have run is vulnerability scanning, which in most settings is a product category sold to defenders. The CISA advisory, aa26-281a, describes the method in its own title: automated and hands-on tools, blended. The scanner finds the door, and a person walks through it. Tanner also covers a separate FBI notice, reported by The Hacker News, about hackers tied to a Chinese cybersecurity company who stole email from government bodies, law enforcement, healthcare systems and religious institutions in Southeast Asia, and ran a portal selling access to it.

The seizure matters for what it cuts and what it leaves. Taking seven domains severs the scanner's reporting path. It does not patch anything. Four flaws on the desk's tracked list entered CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue in the seven days to 9 October, among them the FortiMail path traversal and the Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN Manager bypass, both internet-facing systems of exactly the kind a scanner reaches first. Whoever already holds scan results can still read them.

So the takedown is a real act of attribution and a real loss of infrastructure for the operator, but it is not a repair. The exposure it targeted lives on the defender's side: a management interface that answers any stranger on the internet will keep answering, whoever owns the scanner. Naming Integrity Tech tells the public who built the tools. It does not tell any organisation whether its own front door was already mapped.
