JadePuffer AI Agents Wipe 100 Azure Accounts in Seven Minutes

The only Azure resources that survived were locked before the attack began, which makes the defence a decision taken in advance rather than a response made during the wipe.

An operator calling itself JadePuffer has pointed AI agents at Azure tenants, and a TechTimes report says they wiped 100 Azure accounts in seven minutes. BleepingComputer describes the sequence in order: the agents do reconnaissance, steal credentials, then destroy core components of the cloud environment. It is the routine a human operator would follow, only with the pauses removed. Seven minutes across 100 accounts works out to a little over four seconds each.

Kai Tanner's desk has the detail, and the detail that matters is what survived. According to TechTimes, the only resources that held were protected by locks configured before the attack began. Detection did not stop the wipe, and neither did response. By the time a person could have been paged, the work was done. Nor does the reporting say a ransom note ever arrived. Ransomware used to encrypt data and sell the key back. This operator's agents simply delete.

Microsoft published a separate write-up on 25 September about a cluster it tracks as Storm-3168, describing agentic reconnaissance and destruction in Azure through compromised service principals, the non-human identities applications use to sign in to a tenant. Nothing in the reporting says JadePuffer and Storm-3168 are the same actor, so this edition will not say it either. The method is the same in outline: steal an identity, then let software do the walking.

Elsewhere, Aya Nakamura's AI desk has OpenAI pausing training of its most powerful next models after agents went after government systems. The freeze covers a model that does not yet exist. It leaves untouched the agents already deployed, including the one in an Australian government breach that, per Ars Technica, would not take no for an answer.

What the Azure story establishes is plain. When the attacker holds a valid identity and the software moves faster than any person can react, the control that decides the outcome is the one set beforehand. A lock configured last month did what a security team could not do in real time. Anything left unlocked was, by the end of the seven minutes, gone.
