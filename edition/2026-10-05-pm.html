Here is today's evening report. Mon, Oct 5, 18:10 HKT

  • 12:07 HKT HK Express Hefei flights from 3 December, HK$77 sale closed 4 October Elsewhere, Cheung Kwok-keung's desk explains why the HK$77 Hefei fare is gone, what the flight really costs, and when it starts. read more

    Cheung Kwok-keung, HK DESK

    Say you searched "hkexpress" this morning because someone sent you a HK$77 fare to Hefei. You click through and the number is gone. The sale ran from 10:00 on 30 September to 23:45 on 4 October. You missed it by a few hours.

    The announcement does not list post-sale fares. The HK$77 was a round trip, "Ultra Lite", with one small personal item. Anything bigger costs extra.

    The flight itself is real. Four a week to Hefei, the capital of Anhui, on Tuesdays, Thursdays, Saturdays and Sundays. About two and a half hours on an A320neo. First flight is 3 December, and the airline says the route is subject to regulatory approval.

    Wego priced an early December sample after the fact: US$128 return from Hong Kong. China Eastern's nonstop, which flies Mondays, Wednesdays, Fridays and Sundays, was US$178. So the budget carrier still wins by US$50, just not by the margin on the poster.

    Between the two airlines there is now a nonstop to Hefei every day of the week. Pack small, check the price on the day, and wait for 3 December to see if the approval comes through.

US Air Force Pulls Bombers From RAF Fairford Over Iran Plot Threat

A base central to the Iran campaign was judged too exposed to hold aircraft, so a security scare has become a limit on how the war is flown.

The US Air Force has pulled all of its bombers out of RAF Fairford, and The New York Times reports it did so at unusual speed after officials cited threats of an Iran-based plot against the base. Fairford is the English airfield used for strikes on Iran, so an evacuation there is a decision about how the war is run, and not a footnote about security.

The Times has the lead-up. A mysterious call near the base last Sunday set off a huge counterterrorism response across Britain and days of uncertainty over a site central to American operations. A call costs nothing to make. What followed was expensive: a national police response, then a judgement by the Air Force that aircraft were safer somewhere else. The reporting available here does not say what the plot was or who stood behind it, and the officials speak of threats, not arrests. That gap matters, and it should be stated plainly.

The move lands in a war with no diplomatic exit in sight. Iran's foreign minister said on Sunday there is no military solution, with talks apparently at an impasse, which was the story this morning. As Mei Chen reports from the Geopolitical desk, Iranian state media says the Strait of Hormuz stays shut until seven Iranian conditions are met. The pressure runs both ways. The Times also reports that Mohsen Rezaei, Iran's security chief, has made a rare public admission of severe economic trouble, weeks after Washington tightened sanctions and its naval blockade.

What this means is simple to state. A war fought from an ally's soil depends on the host country's ability to protect the runway as much as on the aircraft that use it. By moving the bombers, Washington has judged that protection insufficient, whether or not any plot is ever proven. That trades proximity for safety, and it shows that the war's reach now extends to the English countryside where its aircraft were parked.
