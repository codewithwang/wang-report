  • 13:17 HKT Beijing and Manila Each Accuse the Other of Breaking the Rules of the Same Piece of Sky Vincent Lai has Beijing and Manila each filing a charge over one South China Sea encounter, in vocabulary built to be quoted back. read more

    Vincent Lai, GEOPOLITICAL

    Somebody in Manila is now drafting a reply to a Chinese charge that the Philippine aircraft was the intruder, while the Philippine account describes a PLA aircraft that came too close. Both documents concern one encounter over the South China Sea, and each is written to be quoted back at the other.

    The vocabulary is chosen with care. Manila says "unsafe," a word that belongs to aviation practice and places the fault in how the PLA pilot flew. Beijing says "airspace," a word that belongs to sovereignty and places the fault in where the Philippine aircraft was. Neither word can be answered with the other's. A safety complaint does not concede the airspace, and a sovereignty claim does not address the separation distance, so the two ministries can file past each other indefinitely, or, more precisely, for as long as the paper is useful to both.

    It has been useful before. On 10 September we wrote that a leaked diplomatic note had moved the dispute from patrol boats to paper, and that paper is what carries into any future arbitration. Nine days later a ramming incident put the Philippine bond book on notice. Our flashpoint map has logged 25 moves in the South China Sea since 6 September, the latest on 4 October, and the aircraft encounter adds another dated entry for each side.

    The encounter itself is less consequential than the calendar around it. The Australian reports the marathon ASEAN talks on the sea are nearing their end, and whatever text emerges will be read against two freshly filed accounts of the same sky. A drafting officer in any ASEAN capital now has Manila's word, "unsafe," and Beijing's word, "airspace," on the desk beside the draft.

    Regional bond desks price Manila off exactly this paper. The next entry will be whichever government answers the other's note first, and the talks give that reply a deadline.

  • 13:15 HKT Twelve People In A Chicago Trial May No Longer Have To Be Their Own Pancreas Magnus Honeyfield explains what 'potentially cured' means for twelve people with type 1 diabetes, and why the follow-up matters more than the headline. read more

    Magnus Honeyfield, SCIENCE

    With type 1 diabetes, the immune system destroys the cells that make insulin, and from then on the patient is the factory. They measure blood sugar before a meal, work out a dose, inject it, and do the arithmetic again at the next meal. At night they wake to check, because a dose that was slightly too large can drop blood sugar while they sleep.

    A trial at the University of Chicago reports that all 12 of its participants have potentially been cured, according to NBC News. The treatment transplants islet cells. Islets are the small clusters of cells in the pancreas that sense blood sugar and release insulin in proportion, which is the job the patient has been doing by hand.

    A cure for this disease has one practical meaning. The body measures its own sugar and doses itself, and the person stops injecting. That is what the transplant is built to restore. It puts working cells back in the loop, so the sensing and the dosing happen in the same place again, with no arithmetic.

    The word "potentially" matters here, and so does the number. Twelve people is a trial, not a treatment, and the reporting available today does not say how long each participant has been followed. Nor does it spell out what drugs, if any, they take to keep the immune system from attacking the new cells. That second question carries weight because the immune system that destroyed the original islets is the same one that would see the transplanted ones. Islet transplants have conventionally relied on drugs that suppress it, and a person who swaps insulin for those drugs has traded one daily burden for another.

    So the result to read for is the follow-up. Cells that work at the first check-up and still work after years are a cure. Cells that work for a few months are a long holiday from injections. The trial's later reports will say which of those the 12 have.

    Until they do, anyone with type 1 diabetes still sets the alarm for 3 a.m. and reaches for the meter.

NYT Reports Iran's Guard Ordered Possible Drone Attack on Fairford Bombers

The unnamed threat behind the B-1 withdrawal now has a described form, a drone strike on a British base, which turns a vague precaution into a stated warning about Iran's reach.

The threat that sent American bombers away from RAF Fairford now has a shape. The New York Times reports that twelve B-1 bombers left the base after officials warned that the Revolutionary Guard had ordered a possible drone attack. The Telegraph's headline puts it more bluntly, saying Iran 'planned drone attack on RAF Fairford'.

The story has moved in three steps. On Sunday the Times reported that the Air Force had pulled its bombers out at unusual speed, citing an Iran-based plot. This morning the Pentagon confirmed the withdrawal, and President Trump attributed it to a threat without saying what the threat was. The new reporting supplies the missing piece: the weapon was a drone, and the order, as officials describe it, came from the Guard.

The wording deserves care. The Times says officials warned of a possible attack. That is an intelligence assessment, not an event, and nothing in the reporting says a drone reached the base. What the reporting does establish is that Washington judged the warning serious enough to move twelve of its most prominent aircraft within days, and that it did so on British soil.

The choice of weapon explains the speed. A bomber is large, expensive and, on a ramp, hard to hide. A drone is small, cheap and hard to stop. Defending a fixed base against that kind of weapon is a different problem from defending against a missile, and a quick departure costs far less than discovering whether the defences hold.

So the withdrawal tells us something about the war itself. The Iran conflict was supposed to be fought in the Gulf, and American planners are now treating an English airfield as inside Iran's range of options. Washington has decided that bombers are better placed out of reach than within reach, and it has now said, through officials, what it was afraid of.
