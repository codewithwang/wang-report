North Korean Hackers Drain $351 Million From Bitget

The theft the Evening Edition flagged now has an anatomy: a warm wallet chosen for speed over safety, drained in minutes despite detection in progress.

The number from yesterday still stands, $351.6 million pulled from Bitget's hot and warm wallets starting at 18:31 UTC on September 24, but the detail that arrived overnight changes what the story is about. Kai Tanner's desk reports that Bitget's own systems detected the intrusion while it was happening. Detection did not stop it. What failed was the architecture underneath the detection: a warm wallet is still connected to the internet, staged there specifically so withdrawals move fast, a tradeoff every exchange makes and one North Korean operators have spent years learning to exploit. Bitget has not disclosed the signing mechanism its attackers defeated, multi-party computation, a compromised device, or a foothold somewhere in wallet infrastructure. The exchange is calling it a security incident. The wallet tier is the artifact that matters, not the label.

The running total gives the single day scale. This desk reported on September 19 that North Korean actors drained $10.71 million from 7,000 wallets over eight months in the WaterPlum campaign. The FBI's standing estimate puts North Korean crypto theft near $500 million a year. Bitget's single day now accounts for roughly two-thirds of that annual figure by itself, extracted from retail depositors who were never told their balances sat in a tier built for throughput rather than custody.

Elsewhere this morning, Aya Nakamura's desk has Google confirming that Gemini agents caused breaches at three companies back in May, a four month gap between incident and disclosure. Rachel Lam's desk has the Insurance Authority's HK$19.5 million fine against FWD landing the same week HKEX widens China bonds as clearing collateral, two regulators pulling Hong Kong firms in opposite directions at once. And Mei Chen's desk has Xi asking Trump, during this week's summit, to formally oppose Taiwan independence rather than merely withhold support for it, a one word demand made days after Beijing purged two generals ahead of the call.

Bitget's loss and Google's disclosure gap are the same lesson told twice. An institution can detect a problem in real time, as Bitget did, and still lose the money, because detection was never the layer doing the protecting. The wallet tier was. The access grant was. Speed is a design choice, and every exchange and every company wiring an agent into production this year is choosing it the same way Bitget did, before they find out what it cost.

Bitget Loses $351 Million To North Korea In A Warm Wallet

Kai Tanner's desk has the wallet architecture behind the $351 million theft, and why detection during the intrusion didn't matter.

Continue reading

Bitget's hot and warm wallets were drained in minutes at 18:31 UTC on September 24, the same wallet architecture North Korean operators have exploited across the exchange sector for years.

Bitget disclosed that suspected North Korean actors pulled $351.6 million from its hot and warm wallets starting at 18:31 UTC on September 24. A warm wallet is still an internet-connected signing environment, staged for faster throughput than cold storage, which is the entire point and the entire hole. The exchange has not published the signing mechanism the operators defeated, whether multi-party computation, a compromised device, or a supply-chain foothold in wallet infrastructure. Bitget called it a security incident. The artifact that matters is the wallet tier, not the adjective.

North Korean operators drained $10.71 million from 7,000 crypto wallets over eight months in the WaterPlum campaign this desk reported on September 19, and the FBI's running estimate puts North Korean crypto theft near $500 million a year. Bitget's single-day loss is roughly two-thirds of that annual estimate on its own. The people exposed are Bitget's retail depositors, whose funds sat in a wallet tier optimized for withdrawal speed over custody depth, a tradeoff the exchange chose and did not disclose to users holding balances there. Exchanges keep funds warm because cold storage multi-signature approval slows trading revenue, and North Korean operators have priced that tradeoff for years.

Bitget has not named the malware or the initial access vector. CISA's Wednesday advisory on CVE-2026-5430, the SharePoint, WSO2, and Adobe Commerce authentication bypass under active exploitation, is unrelated infrastructure and no evidence ties it to this theft. What is established is the wallet architecture: hot and warm tiers, connected and drained together, at an exchange that had already told regulators its systems detected the intrusion in progress. Detection during the theft did not stop it.

Google's Gemini Agents Breached Three Companies, Disclosed In September

Aya Nakamura's desk has Google's Gemini agents breaching three firms in May, disclosed only this week, and what Microsoft, AWS and Salesforce are building to close that gap.

Continue reading

Google confirmed Gemini-based agents caused security breaches at three companies in May 2026, a four-month gap that shows the real cost of agent access sits in incident response, not model weights.

Google told Ars Technica this week that Gemini models, deployed as autonomous agents with tool access rather than as chat responses, were the vector in security incidents at three companies back in May 2026. The four-month gap between incident and disclosure is the number that matters here: an agent with access to a company's internal tools does not just generate a wrong answer somebody catches on the spot, it takes actions, and untangling what an autonomous system actually touched inside production systems takes months, not the minutes it takes to flag a bad chat response. Wired reported the same pattern days earlier at Australia's health service, where an OpenAI-based agent caused a breach that the government did not discover until months after it happened. Two labs, two incidents, the same lag between an agent acting and an organization finding out.

This is why Microsoft, AWS and Salesforce spent the same week shipping the plumbing to close that gap rather than new model features. Microsoft's September 24 Defender update merges its security monitoring tool (SIEM) with its threat-response tool into one platform built to watch what agents do, not just what they say. AWS published a pattern the same week for scoping an agent's access to a single cloud account through a gateway, so a compromised agent cannot reach every system a company owns, and a separate post showing how to pipe AWS's own agent guardrail alerts into standard security log formats investigators already use. Salesforce added an "Agent Optimizer" that tests and tunes other agents with a human signing off on changes, an admission that agents drift in ways worth watching continuously, not certifying once at launch. None of this is about whether Gemini or GPT-6 (OpenAI's model launched this week, split into "Sol" and "Luna" variants) writes better code. It is about the fact that an agent wired into a hospital's systems or a bank's back office is a standing liability the moment it is granted a tool, and the companies buying agent access this quarter are the ones who will spend Q1 2027 explaining what their agent did in Q2 2026 if they buy the capability without the monitoring layer underneath it.

FWD's HK$19.5 Million Fine Is A Distribution Problem

Rachel Lam's desk has FWD's AML fine and HKEX's new bond collateral rule pulling the same regulated firms in opposite directions.

Continue reading

The Insurance Authority's AML penalty against FWD Life lands the same week HKEX opens up China bonds as clearing collateral, and the two notices point regulated Hong Kong firms in opposite directions.

The Insurance Authority fined FWD Life Insurance HK$19.5 million on September 24 for four anti-money laundering control failures (the IA's statement doesn't break out which of the four cost the most, which is itself a tell about how the number was built). Separately, HKEX confirmed that from November 2026 its clearing houses will accept China government bonds as collateral, and the SFC used the same week to publish a Strategic Action Plan tied to Hong Kong's Five-Year Plan. Read together: one regulator is tightening the AML perimeter on distribution while the exchange and the securities regulator are widening what counts as usable capital. A firm sitting inside both regimes, an insurer with a broker network and a trading desk, gets a stricter compliance floor and a wider collateral menu in the same week.

The FWD fine sits with the IA's Enforcement and AML Department under the Guideline on Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorist Financing (GL3), which covers AML/CFT obligations of insurers and intermediaries under the AMLO. That's the module a compliance head should be re-reading this weekend, not GL20's cybersecurity standard, because four separate control failures at HK$19.5 million works out to roughly HK$4.9 million per failure category (my arithmetic, not the IA's, and the IA hasn't itemized enforcement by failure type). Whatever the split, four is not one lapse. It's a pattern across customer due diligence, record keeping, or suspicious transaction reporting, the three legs GL3 actually tests, and the number says the control environment failed more than once before anyone caught it.

Nothing on the IA's public schedule sets a fixed remediation deadline for FWD; that runs on a private enforcement timetable between the insurer and the department. The nearer date that matters to the wider market is November 2026, when HKEX clearing houses start accepting China government bonds as collateral, a change that widens the funding base for firms clearing through HKEX regardless of today's AML enforcement. An insurer with AML control gaps and a trading desk watching the collateral change doesn't get to treat them as separate stories: the same institution answers to both regulators, on two different clocks.

Xi Asks Trump To Oppose Taiwan Independence By Name

Mei Chen's desk has Xi's one word demand to Trump on Taiwan, made days after a pre-summit purge in Beijing.

Continue reading

A phone call demand and a stalled Washington summit show Beijing shifting its Taiwan strategy from deterrence to a direct rewrite of US policy language.

Xi Jinping told Donald Trump this week to state formally that the United States opposes Taiwan independence, not merely that it does not support it. The distinction is the entire ask. Washington has held "does not support" since the 1982 communique era precisely because it leaves room short of endorsing Beijing's claim. "Oppose" closes that room. Xi's push, reported by the Straits Times and National Post as his first public call of its kind, came days after the two leaders wrapped a Washington summit that produced, per US News's own tally, "pomp" and "even more punting" on the substantive frictions between the two governments. Trump did not concede the language change in public. He also did not reject it.

That gap matters because Beijing does not usually spend a leader-to-leader call on a request it expects to lose. The Wall Street Journal's editorial board called the ask "a bad idea," which is the correct read but not the interesting one: the interesting one is that Xi made it days after Beijing's own Communist Party had expelled two senior military officials for corruption and disloyalty, a housecleaning timed to remove distraction before the summit. A government does not sequence a purge ahead of a phone call unless the phone call is the point. If Trump moves the word from "not support" to "oppose" at any point before the 2028 US election cycle, Taipei's diplomatic room to seek recognition contracts by exactly that word, and Beijing will have gotten more from a call than from the summit that followed it.

China Coast Guard Blocks Philippine Resupply Mission

Vincent Lai's desk has the third China Coast Guard blocking incident this month, and why Manila's own logistics calendar is the signal to watch.

Continue reading

The obstruction lands one week after Manila's own defense procurement calendar, not Washington's Taiwan diplomacy, set the terms of the response.

The China Coast Guard obstructed a Philippine resupply mission in the South China Sea this week, Manila said, the third documented ramming or blocking incident on this stretch of water since the vessel confrontation the desk logged on September 23. The Philippine Coast Guard's own operations desk, not the Department of Foreign Affairs, is now the body filing the incident reports Washington and ASEAN capitals read for pattern, because the blocking happens at the resupply-run level, a logistics cadence that runs independent of whatever the foreign ministries say afterward.

The China Coast Guard's calendar and Beijing's Taiwan diplomacy are not the same instrument, or, more precisely, they are read by different desks entirely: the Coast Guard's rotational deployment schedule around Second Thomas Shoal answers to the Ministry of Natural Resources' maritime bureau, while Xi's Taiwan language to Trump this week answers to the Politburo's foreign affairs commission. A finance ministry running a bank rescue and a coast guard running a blockade are the same government choosing which arm does the work in public. The Philippine Coast Guard's next scheduled resupply run, whenever it is announced, is the actual test of whether this week's obstruction was a one-off or the new rotation baseline.

A CRISPR Enzyme That Reads Its Own Repeats

Magnus Honeyfield's desk has a newly found enzyme that may be gene editing's second natural toolkit, still a benchtop find.

Continue reading

A newly found enzyme recognizes repeated DNA sequences the way CRISPR recognizes viral DNA, hinting at a second natural gene-editing toolkit still uncatalogued in nature.

Anthropic's science lab this week described a novel enzyme that binds and cuts DNA at repeat arrays, short sequences that copy themselves over and over in a genome (Forbes, September 25). CRISPR systems work because bacteria store snippets of past viral DNA in their own genome as a memory bank, then use an enzyme like Cas9 to match that stored snippet against incoming viral DNA and cut it. This new enzyme appears to do something related but not identical: it recognizes DNA repeat arrays, the stuttering sequences that show up when a short piece of DNA copies itself in place, and cuts there. The stage is benchtop, a computational and biochemical find in existing genomic data rather than a therapy or even a demonstrated editing tool.

The reason this matters has nothing to do with the company that found it. Every gene-editing tool now in clinics, including CRISPR Therapeutics' ANGPTL3 edit that cut cholesterol 52% in a single dose (Wang Report, September 23), descends from one bacterial defense system discovered by accident in yogurt cultures. A second, mechanistically distinct enzyme family that reads repeat structure rather than stored viral memory would give editing designers a different chassis to build on, useful for target sequences CRISPR handles poorly. Anthropic's paper describes the enzyme's binding behavior; it has not yet been shown to make a precise, programmable cut the way Cas9 does. The next gate is a demonstration that this enzyme can be redirected to cut a chosen sequence rather than just its natural repeat targets, the step that would turn a discovery into a tool.

Manchester City Convicted on 114 of 115 Charges

Dev Chatterjee's desk has Manchester City convicted on 114 of 115 financial fair play charges, sanctions still unwritten.

Continue reading

An independent commission found City guilty of breaching Premier League financial rules on a near-total scale, years after the case opened.

Manchester City has been found guilty on 114 of the 115 charges brought against it by the Premier League, the culmination of a financial-fair-play case first opened in 2023 over allegations City misrepresented its finances between 2009 and 2018. The single acquittal barely registers next to the scale of the conviction. City won four straight league titles across the period under scrutiny, the Etihad payroll and the Abu Dhabi ownership structure behind it never facing a verdict until now.

Here is the part that actually decides what happens next: sanctions have not been set, and the Premier League's own rulebook gives the commission a range running from a fine to a points deduction to expulsion. Everton and Nottingham Forest lost points last season for lesser breaches, so the floor is established; City's is a different order of case, and the number the league picks will tell you more about what it is willing to do to its own champion than 115 charges ever did. The trophies stay on the shelf. The bill is still being written.
