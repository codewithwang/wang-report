  • 06:02 HKT Bitget Loses $351 Million To North Korea In A Warm Wallet Kai Tanner's desk has the wallet architecture behind the $351 million theft, and why detection during the intrusion didn't matter. read more

    Kai Tanner, CYBER INTEL

    Bitget disclosed that suspected North Korean actors pulled $351.6 million from its hot and warm wallets starting at 18:31 UTC on September 24. A warm wallet is still an internet-connected signing environment, staged for faster throughput than cold storage, which is the entire point and the entire hole. The exchange has not published the signing mechanism the operators defeated, whether multi-party computation, a compromised device, or a supply-chain foothold in wallet infrastructure. Bitget called it a security incident. The artifact that matters is the wallet tier, not the adjective.

    North Korean operators drained $10.71 million from 7,000 crypto wallets over eight months in the WaterPlum campaign this desk reported on September 19, and the FBI's running estimate puts North Korean crypto theft near $500 million a year. Bitget's single-day loss is roughly two-thirds of that annual estimate on its own. The people exposed are Bitget's retail depositors, whose funds sat in a wallet tier optimized for withdrawal speed over custody depth, a tradeoff the exchange chose and did not disclose to users holding balances there. Exchanges keep funds warm because cold storage multi-signature approval slows trading revenue, and North Korean operators have priced that tradeoff for years.

    Bitget has not named the malware or the initial access vector. CISA's Wednesday advisory on CVE-2026-5430, the SharePoint, WSO2, and Adobe Commerce authentication bypass under active exploitation, is unrelated infrastructure and no evidence ties it to this theft. What is established is the wallet architecture: hot and warm tiers, connected and drained together, at an exchange that had already told regulators its systems detected the intrusion in progress. Detection during the theft did not stop it.

    Mei Chen, GEOPOLITICAL

    Xi Jinping told Donald Trump this week to state formally that the United States opposes Taiwan independence, not merely that it does not support it. The distinction is the entire ask. Washington has held "does not support" since the 1982 communique era precisely because it leaves room short of endorsing Beijing's claim. "Oppose" closes that room. Xi's push, reported by the Straits Times and National Post as his first public call of its kind, came days after the two leaders wrapped a Washington summit that produced, per US News's own tally, "pomp" and "even more punting" on the substantive frictions between the two governments. Trump did not concede the language change in public. He also did not reject it.

    That gap matters because Beijing does not usually spend a leader-to-leader call on a request it expects to lose. The Wall Street Journal's editorial board called the ask "a bad idea," which is the correct read but not the interesting one: the interesting one is that Xi made it days after Beijing's own Communist Party had expelled two senior military officials for corruption and disloyalty, a housecleaning timed to remove distraction before the summit. A government does not sequence a purge ahead of a phone call unless the phone call is the point. If Trump moves the word from "not support" to "oppose" at any point before the 2028 US election cycle, Taipei's diplomatic room to seek recognition contracts by exactly that word, and Beijing will have gotten more from a call than from the summit that followed it.

North Korean Hackers Drain $351 Million From Bitget

The theft the Evening Edition flagged now has an anatomy: a warm wallet chosen for speed over safety, drained in minutes despite detection in progress.

The number from yesterday still stands, $351.6 million pulled from Bitget's hot and warm wallets starting at 18:31 UTC on September 24, but the detail that arrived overnight changes what the story is about. Kai Tanner's desk reports that Bitget's own systems detected the intrusion while it was happening. Detection did not stop it. What failed was the architecture underneath the detection: a warm wallet is still connected to the internet, staged there specifically so withdrawals move fast, a tradeoff every exchange makes and one North Korean operators have spent years learning to exploit. Bitget has not disclosed the signing mechanism its attackers defeated, multi-party computation, a compromised device, or a foothold somewhere in wallet infrastructure. The exchange is calling it a security incident. The wallet tier is the artifact that matters, not the label.

The running total gives the single day scale. This desk reported on September 19 that North Korean actors drained $10.71 million from 7,000 wallets over eight months in the WaterPlum campaign. The FBI's standing estimate puts North Korean crypto theft near $500 million a year. Bitget's single day now accounts for roughly two-thirds of that annual figure by itself, extracted from retail depositors who were never told their balances sat in a tier built for throughput rather than custody.

Elsewhere this morning, Aya Nakamura's desk has Google confirming that Gemini agents caused breaches at three companies back in May, a four month gap between incident and disclosure. Rachel Lam's desk has the Insurance Authority's HK$19.5 million fine against FWD landing the same week HKEX widens China bonds as clearing collateral, two regulators pulling Hong Kong firms in opposite directions at once. And Mei Chen's desk has Xi asking Trump, during this week's summit, to formally oppose Taiwan independence rather than merely withhold support for it, a one word demand made days after Beijing purged two generals ahead of the call.

Bitget's loss and Google's disclosure gap are the same lesson told twice. An institution can detect a problem in real time, as Bitget did, and still lose the money, because detection was never the layer doing the protecting. The wallet tier was. The access grant was. Speed is a design choice, and every exchange and every company wiring an agent into production this year is choosing it the same way Bitget did, before they find out what it cost.
