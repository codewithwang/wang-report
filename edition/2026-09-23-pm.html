ShinyHunters Says It Breached the FBI Through Oracle PeopleSoft

Kai Tanner has the PeopleSoft claim: no CVE, no patch, no FBI confirmation, and a second live enterprise-platform incident the same week.

Continue reading

The extortion group claims a zero-day in Oracle's HR software let it pull FBI employee records, and the bureau has not confirmed or denied it.

ShinyHunters says it used an unpatched vulnerability in Oracle PeopleSoft, the human-resources and payroll platform, to get into FBI systems and pull data on bureau employees. The group posted the claim alongside what it says are internal service screenshots and employee records, first reported by BleepingComputer and 404 Media. No CVE number has been assigned, and no PeopleSoft patch is out. The FBI has not confirmed a breach occurred.

Oracle PeopleSoft runs HR, payroll and benefits administration for organizations that need to keep that data behind something more locked-down than a public website. If ShinyHunters actually has a zero-day, not a stolen credential or a misconfigured instance already public, this is the kind of software normally invisible to the outside. The same week, CISA added a Dassault Systèmes DELMIA Apriso flaw, CVE-2025-6205, to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities list, a separate enterprise platform now under active exploitation. Two unrelated back-office systems, two live incidents, in the same seven days.

ShinyHunters has run a string of extortion campaigns this year built on stolen access rather than novel exploits, so an actual zero-day would be a change in tooling, not just targets. The bureau has not published an incident notice, and Oracle has not issued a security alert for PeopleSoft tied to this claim. Until one of those two things happens, what's public is a claim, a screenshot set, and a named platform still running unpatched in whoever else uses it.

Xi Brings A Taiwan Arms List To Trump's Table

Mei Chen on Xi's Washington arrival: an explicit demand to halt Taiwan arms sales, delivered with a $14 billion sale still unresolved on the table.

Continue reading

Xi Jinping arrives at the White House on September 24 to press Trump directly on halting Taiwan arms sales, testing whether Washington's summit hospitality extends to policy.

Xi Jinping lands in Washington on September 24 with one demand for Donald Trump: stop selling weapons to Taiwan. Reuters reported the ask is explicit, not implied, which matters because Beijing spent the prior week doing the opposite of asking nicely. On September 19, Trump described Taiwan's own arms shipments as "chips to play" in his dealings with Xi. Three days later, the New York Post reported Washington moving to back Taipei ahead of the summit even as the $14 billion arms sale Beijing threatened to blow up the visit over on September 13 stays on the table, unresolved and unwithdrawn.

The Ministry of National Defense in Taipei tracked four Chinese aircraft, six naval vessels, and three official ships around the island in the 24 hours before Tuesday, September 22, the kind of patrol tempo Xi's delegation will not mention across the table. A summit does not pause the pressure that makes the summit necessary. Xi is not choosing between charm and coercion in Washington this week. He is bringing the coercion to the charm offensive, and asking Trump to price it as a concession. If Trump treats the arms halt as a topic for negotiation rather than a red line, the $14 billion sale becomes the actual measure of what the summit bought Beijing.

China Coast Guard Rams Philippine Vessel In Contested Waters

Vincent Lai on the Coast Guard ramming near the Philippines, five days before Xi lands, and why the chains of command don't actually connect.

Continue reading

The ramming lands on the same Politburo calendar as Xi's Washington trip, and the timing is the signal Manila is reading.

The China Coast Guard rammed a Philippine government vessel in the South China Sea, a collision that moves the South China Sea flashpoint forward from the September 20 register entry into open contact, five days before Xi Jinping is due in Washington on September 24 to press Donald Trump on a Taiwan arms halt, per Mei Chen's filing today. The two incidents do not share a chain of command in the way the headlines will imply. The Taiwan Strait file sits with the Central Military Commission and the United Front Work Department, the apparatus that manages the optics Xi carries into the Trump meeting; the South China Sea patrol pattern sits with the China Coast Guard's own operational command under the Ministry of Natural Resources, a body that answers to a maritime rights timetable Beijing has run since the 2016 arbitral ruling it never accepted, not to a White House calendar.

That separation is the point the Manila desk watches for. A ramming five days before a leader-level summit is not Beijing improvising leverage for the Washington table, or, more precisely, it is Beijing declining to spend leverage it does not need there, because the coast guard's mandate runs independent of whatever gets negotiated on arms sales. Manila's own foreign ministry logs each incursion against the 2016 ruling for the paper trail it is building toward the next diplomatic protest, not toward Washington's intervention. The Philippine government has no desk inside the Trump-Xi summit room, and the ramming confirms that its patrol-tempo dispute with Beijing will still be running unresolved on September 25, whichever way the Taiwan arms question breaks.

UBS's Own Regulator Fined It Before Bern Finished Debating Capital

Rachel Lam reads three banks' bad weeks, UBS, Credit Suisse's ghost, and StanChart, as a due-diligence pattern worth pricing.

Continue reading

A 5 million euro Credit Suisse tax fine and a Swiss capital-rules fight land the same week HKMA republishes fraud alerts with no update on its own credit institution's exposure.

Look, the UBS numbers this week don't agree with each other. A German regulator fined UBS 5 million euros over Credit Suisse legacy tax evasion, per the qwant news wire, the same week UBS itself told markets that Switzerland's new capital rules could cost it billions and dent competitiveness (Duckduckgo), and the Swiss upper house countered with a 90 percent compromise plan on that capital math (Bing). Separately, StanChart's CEO told Bing news his bank shut accounts tied to a $6.9 billion Russian laundering network, while qwant reported Russian fintech A7 gamed bank controls to move billions in sanctioned payments. Three different banks, three different regulators, one week. None of it is HKMA's problem yet (the closest Hong Kong got today was HKICL republishing a fraudulent-website alert and a bank scam notice on September 22, which is a press-office warning to the public, not supervisory guidance to institutions).

So here's the read for a Hong Kong desk with UBS or StanChart counterparty exposure: nothing here triggers a Cap. 615 filing or a Banking Conduct Department review today, because none of these three stories are Hong Kong-licensed events. But the pattern is the thing worth pricing. A German fine on a legacy Credit Suisse book, a Swiss capital rule UBS is actively lobbying against, and a laundering network StanChart says it already exited, all surfacing in the same 72 hours, is the sanctions-and-AML tail risk that HKMA's Enforcement and AML Department will ask a licensed institution to demonstrate it doesn't share, the next time a correspondent-banking due diligence file gets pulled. The Enforcement and AML Department's baseline ask isn't module-specific here (there's no HKMA circular in today's notices naming UBS or StanChart), which means the obligation runs through ordinary AML/CFT customer due diligence and sanctions screening, not a special filing.

The next dated event is HKMA's own calendar item: the PBoC issuing Renminbi Bills through the Central Moneymarkets Unit, announced September 21, with settlement mechanics that matter more to a Hong Kong treasury desk this week than a German tax fine on a Zurich bank does. Watch what HKMA does with its own AML supervision data before assuming Basel-market noise stays in Basel.

Housing Chief Promises Shorter Queue By 2027

Cheung Kwok-keung on the housing chief's promise to shorten a six-year queue by 2027, and why a date is not the same as a plan.

Continue reading

The public housing wait has been climbing for years and now the government has put a year on the promise to turn it around.

So the housing chief stood up today and said the queue for public housing will get shorter by 2027. Right now you are looking at close to six years average wait if you are a normal family, longer if you are elderly and alone in some cases. Six years. That is a kid starting primary school and finishing it before mum and dad get the keys. And now there is a date on the promise, which is new, because normally the line on this is just "we are working on it."

Here is the thing about a government putting a year on a queue. It is either a real plan, more units finished, more allocated, something you can check in 2027 and go "yes that happened." Or it is a number picked because it sounds close enough to be reassuring and far enough that nobody in that room has to answer for it next year. Lee's five-year plan dropped this week too, so housing is clearly the headline item everyone in government wants attached to their name before 2027 shows up. Mark the date. We will be checking.

A Cholesterol Drug That Edits The Gene Once And Stops

Magnus Honeyfield on a cholesterol drug that edits a gene once and stops working on purpose.

Continue reading

CRISPR Therapeutics' CTX310 cut LDL cholesterol and triglycerides in a single dose by permanently switching off a gene, not by blocking its protein.

Most cholesterol drugs, statins included, work by chasing a moving target: they block an enzyme or receptor every day, and the moment you stop taking the pill, the target starts working again. CTX310, from CRISPR Therapeutics, does something structurally different. In a Phase 1 trial reported this week, a single infusion cut LDL cholesterol and triglycerides by 52% in patients with treatment-resistant lipid disorders, by editing the ANGPTL3 gene in liver cells so it can no longer make the protein at all. ANGPTL3 normally holds back two enzymes that clear fat from the blood; silence the gene and those enzymes run unopposed, pulling triglyceride-rich particles out of circulation. The edit happens once, in the DNA itself, not in a protein the drug has to keep suppressing.

The mechanism is a base edit delivered by lipid nanoparticle, the same delivery vehicle as an mRNA vaccine, but here it carries editing machinery into liver cells rather than a vaccine antigen. It lands on a shortlist of gene-silencing cholesterol approaches now in human trials, alongside RNA interference drugs like inclisiran that suppress the same pathway without touching DNA. This is a Phase 1 trial: it establishes that a single edit lowers lipids and, so far, holds, but Phase 1 in the US is built to check safety and dosing in a small group, not to prove it prevents heart attacks. The next gate is whether CRISPR Therapeutics moves CTX310 into a larger Phase 2 trial with a defined cardiovascular endpoint, and how long the reduction lasts before the company needs to report a durability readout beyond the current data.

Rays Clinch AL East, End Yankees' Bid To Win It

Dev Chatterjee on the Rays clinching the AL East the same night Kawhi Leonard signed for more than their roster costs.

Continue reading

Tampa Bay locked up the division on the same night Toronto handed Kawhi Leonard $115 million, a reminder of how differently baseball and basketball pay their stars.

Drew Rasmussen struck out 11, surrendered exactly one hit, and closed the door on the Yankees 6-1 on Tuesday, the back end of a day-night doubleheader that handed Tampa Bay the AL East outright. This is the Rays doing what the Rays do: a rotation piece few outside St. Petersburg could pick out of a lineup card just outpitched a $300 million-plus Yankees roster twice in one calendar day. New York's lineup has David Rutherford money in it. Tampa Bay's has a hitting coach's whiteboard and a pitching lab. The division banner goes to the whiteboard.

Same night, up in Toronto, Kawhi Leonard signed for two years and $115 million, a number that would functionally buy the entire Rays 40-man roster twice over and still leave change for the parking garage. That is not a knock on Tampa Bay's baseball. It is the two sports' economics sitting side by side: an NBA max deal for one wing player against an AL East title built on a payroll that would not cover Leonard's option year. The Rays proved a division can be won on scouting. The Raptors just proved a superstar still costs what a superstar costs, no matter who is doing the buying.
