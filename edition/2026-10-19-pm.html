Here is today's evening report. Mon, Oct 19, 18:44 HKT

  • 18:30 HKT Broadcom Wi-Fi flaw leaves routers exposed even when passwords stay secret Kai Tanner explains how an unpatched Broadcom flaw lets a nearby attacker take control without ever learning the Wi-Fi password. read more

    Kai Tanner, CYBER INTEL

    Broadcom's Wi-Fi setup software lets an attacker within radio range run code as root without a network password or a pairing step. The disclosed flaw remains unpatched. An affected router can therefore keep providing connectivity while someone nearby has a route to administrative control.

    The owner has no update to install. Changing the network password does not block the access described in the report. The attacker does not need to join.

    That makes patch availability consequential before anyone reaches a maintenance window. Microsoft has separately confirmed that this month's Windows Server security updates cause Exchange sign-in and mail delivery problems on some systems. There, an administrator has an update whose installation can disrupt service. For the affected Broadcom router, the reported fix is still missing.

    Radio range limits who can attempt this attack. It also puts the relevant boundary outside the list of devices authorised to use the network. A strong password can remain entirely secret throughout the compromise.

    Withdrawing an affected router from service removes that route to root access. Until a patch arrives or a replacement takes over, it also removes the connectivity the owner bought it to provide.

    Dev Chatterjee, SPORTS

    Dallas brought a high-powered offense to Green Bay and spent Sunday night demonstrating the importance of the adjective “theoretical.”

    The Packers won 23-13, containing the Cowboys while Jordan Love threw a touchdown pass and scored on a one-yard keeper. In a game cluttered with penalties, Green Bay’s quarterback found two ways into the end zone. Dallas found considerably less room than its reputation required.

    The NFL’s national television money is shared among its teams. Competence remains separately administered. That arrangement helps explain the league’s enduring commercial trick: every fan helps sustain the weekly spectacle, including the ones spending Monday wishing they had slept through it.

    Arizona supplied the weekend’s most economical job description. Chad Ryland kicked six field goals, accounting for all its points in an 18-16 win over the Rams. The last, from 43 yards as time expired, settled the game. Six invoices. Payment in full.

Philippines Summons Chinese Envoy Over Threat Of Force

Manila's diplomatic response makes Beijing answer for threatening language over disputed waters, without resolving the disagreement that gave those words their force.

The Philippines has summoned China's envoy over remarks about using force, Bloomberg reports, turning threatening language into a formal diplomatic confrontation over disputed claims in the South China Sea. Beijing's representative now faces a complaint from the government those remarks concerned. The summons does not settle a claim or change control of the waters. It establishes that Manila considers the language serious enough to demand an answer through diplomatic channels. An ambassador's appointment book is an unusually polite place to register an objection to force.

The sequence matters. Remarks about using force came first; the Philippine summons followed. Manila's reported response is diplomatic. That distinction limits what can responsibly be concluded from the news: the supplied account establishes a threat and an official protest, rather than a new military encounter. But language has consequences before anyone acts on it. Introducing force into a disagreement over territory changes what the other government must respond to. Manila is now contesting both the underlying claims and the terms in which Beijing discusses them.

For a Hong Kong reader, the significance lies in that change of terms. Governments can maintain incompatible claims while continuing to conduct diplomacy. A threat of force adds the prospect of coercion to that exchange, making the handling of the dispute consequential alongside its eventual resolution. By summoning the envoy, the Philippines has chosen an established diplomatic means of registering its objection. That gives Beijing an opportunity to respond officially. Nothing in the supplied report establishes that the response has arrived, or that either side has changed its position.

The result is a sharper diplomatic dispute, with its territorial substance unresolved. Manila has made clear that threatening language will itself draw an official challenge. Beijing's claims do not become more legitimate because force is mentioned; the Philippine objection does not resolve them either. What changed today is the diplomatic relationship around those claims: the threat has become something the two governments must address directly.
