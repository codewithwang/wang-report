Cisco Root Flaw Exploited, No Patch Timeline Set

CISA confirmed active exploitation of a critical Cisco email gateway flaw, and Hong Kong bank compliance desks are now running on the KEV clock, not Cisco's.

Cisco's Secure Email Gateway has a SQL injection flaw, CVE-2026-76461, that needs no password and hands an attacker root. CISA added it to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on September 14, confirming active exploitation rather than a theoretical lab finding. Cisco's own advisory tells customers to patch "as soon as possible," language that survives a lawyer's edit but gives nobody an actual number of hours.

Kai Tanner's desk has the mechanics: email gateways exist to parse untrusted internet traffic, so this bug needs no phishing lure and no stolen password to reach the box, and root access on a mail relay means the attacker reads everything the appliance touches. Rachel Lam's desk has the part that matters for Hong Kong specifically. HKMA pushed two scam alerts this week, September 14 and 15, warning of fraudulent sites impersonating HKICL and the banks themselves. That means the phishing traffic this bug could wave through is already being generated, not hypothetical. The compliance obligation sits under HKMA's general IT control framework, not the narrower e-banking module, and with no patch date published, the operative clock is the 21-day remediation window US federal agencies use for KEV listings. Cisco has not published a deadline of its own, so that 21-day window is the one Hong Kong bank compliance desks should be reading off now.

Elsewhere, Mei Chen's desk pairs China's total war mobilization law with the Pentagon's Taiwan Strait drone doctrine, two documents drafted by governments that are not talking to each other but are provisioning for the same fight. And in Hong Kong, Cheung Kwok-keung's desk covers John Lee's first five-year plan: a HK$30,000 cheque for a second child, paired with a public housing wait cut from six years to four.

Vendors write urgency into adjectives; a government catalog turns urgency into an enforceable number. For a Hong Kong bank running this gateway as its inbound filter, the fraudulent websites HKMA is already warning about are the exact payload this flaw would wave through. Patch against the calendar CISA already published, not against how alarmed Cisco eventually sounds.

Cisco Patches Email Gateway Bug It Rates Critical

Kai Tanner breaks down the mechanism: an unauthenticated SQL injection that reaches root on a box that faces the open internet by design.

Continue reading

A SQL injection in Cisco's Secure Email Gateway lets an unauthenticated attacker reach root, and the exploit code for it is already public.

CVE-2026-76461 is a SQL injection in Cisco Secure Email Gateway, CVSS 9.8, added to the CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on September 14. Cisco's own advisory language is "urges urgent patch," which is the kind of phrase that survives a legal review and tells a defender nothing about the clock. The EPSS score on this one sits at 2%, low predicted near-term exploitation volume, but that number describes mass scanning behavior, not what a single motivated operator does with a root-level injection into a mail gateway that already sits on the internet by design.

The mechanism is unglamorous, which is the point. Email gateways parse untrusted input from the open internet as their entire job description, so a SQL injection there does not need a phishing lure or a stolen credential to reach the box. Root access on the gateway means the attacker sees every message the appliance relays, which for a bank branch or a claims processor is the mail server most likely to be sitting between "unauthenticated" and "everyone's inbox." The seven-second SSH bastion compromise Sysdig published this week and this bug share the same lesson: the perimeter appliance is now the fastest path in, not the slowest.

Cisco Secure Email Gateway is the named product, and CISA's KEV listing is the only artifact that matters here, since it confirms exploitation is not theoretical. The patch exists. The gap is between "urgent" as adjective and "urgent" as a number of hours, and Cisco has supplied the former.

Cisco's Root-Access Flaw Sets the Real Patch Clock

Rachel Lam maps the Cisco flaw against HKMA's TM-G-1 framework and the scam alerts already landing in Hong Kong inboxes.

Continue reading

CISA's KEV listing for a critical Cisco email gateway bug does what the vendor's advisory language couldn't, put a number of days on the remediation.

Cisco's advisory for CVE-2026-76461 describes unauthenticated SQL injection in AsyncOS for Secure Email Gateway, CVSS 9.8, sitting on the mail relay that every gateway appliance places with network reach into internal systems (Kai Tanner's filing today). CISA added it to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog September 14. Separately, HKMA's press office pushed two scam alerts this week, on September 14 and 15, flagging fraudulent websites impersonating HKICL and the banks themselves.

Look, Cisco's own advisory tells customers to patch "as soon as possible," which is the sentence every vendor writes the week the actual deadline gets set somewhere else. For a Hong Kong bank running Secure Email Gateway as its inbound filter, CVE-2026-76461 isn't scoped to HKMA's e-banking module, TM-E-1 covers Internet banking, contactless payments and card-not-present transactions, not the mail relay a phishing kit rides in on. The obligation here sits with the Banking Supervision Department's technology risk oversight under TM-G-1, the general IT control framework, and it isn't module-specific: fix the exploited component before the scam emails HKMA is already publicly warning about start arriving through it. The 21-day remediation window US federal civilian agencies operate under on KEV entries is the de facto clock every bank compliance desk should be reading off now, not Cisco's open-ended "as soon as possible."

CISA's KEV catalog updates continuously, so the next observable event is whenever Cisco issues a fix, there's no patch timeline in the advisory as of September 14. Until Cisco publishes one, the KEV listing date, not a vendor deadline, is the only clock running.

Beijing Rehearses Total War While Washington Counts Drones

Mei Chen reads China's mobilization law against the Pentagon's Taiwan drone plan as two sides provisioning for the same war.

Continue reading

China's State Council mobilization law and the Pentagon's Taiwan Strait drone plan describe the same conflict from opposite sides of the timeline.

The Pentagon's plan for a Taiwan Strait war, detailed by defense officials this week, assumes Chinese forces jam allied communications on day one and floods the strait with cheap aerial, sea-surface and underwater drones to slow a PLA crossing anyway. That is a plan for degraded conditions, built by planners who expect to lose the opening exchange of information and win the attrition that follows. It sits next to a different document. China's State Council and Central Military Commission advanced a total war mobilization law this month, sourced on this desk September 15, letting local governments requisition civilian shipping for the People's Liberation Army. One government is drafting the legal authority to convert its merchant fleet into a war economy. The other is designing a minefield of drones to sink whatever that fleet becomes.

Sequencing tells the story neither government states outright. A mobilization law is not a contingency, it is an inventory: China is counting the ships it can commandeer before it needs them, the same posture as a firm doing a pre-acquisition audit. A drone hellscape plan is not a deterrent speech, it is a targeting assumption: the Pentagon is not asking whether jamming happens, it is asking how many drones survive it. Neither side is signaling. Both are provisioning. Defence Minister Dong Jun told the Xiangshan Forum this week that nations should avoid "provocative actions," the same week his own government's cabinet was drafting the legal mechanism to seize civilian ships for a fight. If the mobilization law passes before Washington finalizes the drone doctrine, Taipei's shipping registry becomes the next number Beijing prices into Xi's calendar, the same way it priced the September 24 summit against the $14 billion arms sale on September 14.

Government Pays You HK$30,000 To Have A Second Baby

Cheung Kwok-keung on John Lee's HK$30,000 baby bonus and the housing wait cut that actually moves the needle.

Continue reading

John Lee's first five-year plan puts a HK$30,000 cheque behind the second child, on top of cutting the public housing wait to four years.

Here's the pitch from Government House this week. Have a second kid, get HK$30,000. That's the headline number out of John Lee's first ever five-year plan, dropped Wednesday alongside a promise to cut the public housing wait from six years to four. Two announcements, one target: convince Hong Kong people this city is still worth building a life in.

Now, HK$30,000 sounds nice until you actually think about raising a kid here. That's maybe two months of decent childcare, if you're lucky. Nobody's turning down free money, but let's be honest about the math: this isn't the reason a couple has a second baby, it's a nice bonus after they've already decided. The four-year housing wait is the bigger deal, because a flat you can actually move into changes what feels possible. The cheque is the government showing it cares. The queue getting shorter is the government actually doing something about why people weren't having kids in the first place.

Adam Silver Says He Missed the Red Flags in $12.5 Billion Lakers Sale

Dev Chatterjee on Adam Silver admitting the league's vetting missed an FBI probe before the $12.5 billion Lakers sale closed.

Continue reading

The NBA commissioner told owners an FBI-probed buyer cleared vetting, a admission that reveals how thin due diligence gets when a check this size lands.

Adam Silver stood in front of the NBA's Board of Governors in New York this week and said, essentially, that the league's background check missed the part where the FBI was already looking at the guy. Mark Walter, the insurance billionaire fronting the $12.5 billion purchase of the Los Angeles Lakers, cleared the league's vetting process before reports surfaced tying him to a federal probe. Silver called it a surprise. For a league that fingerprints backup point guards for gambling ties, that is not a small admission. It is the commissioner of a $150 billion industry saying the metal detector went off after the plane had already boarded.

Here is what the surprise actually protects: the largest franchise sale in North American sports history, one that resets the valuation ceiling for every other team in the league. The Lakers sale is not just a transaction, it is the new floor under every Board of Governors conversation about what a marquee franchise is worth, and Silver has too much riding on that number to slow it down over a red flag he says he never saw. The NBA did not find the problem. The league found the price it wanted first, and the vetting came after.
