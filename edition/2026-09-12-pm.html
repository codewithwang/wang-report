OpenAI Freezes Astra Pro Signups On Launch Week

OpenAI's new work-focused model shipped strong enough to overwhelm its own billing pipeline, a distribution failure no benchmark captures.

OpenAI launched GPT-6 Astra on September 10 as a coding and agent model aimed squarely at engineering and scientific work, and by the same day the model's success had become its own problem. TechCrunch reported OpenAI froze new Pro subscription signups because demand outran the infrastructure that turns a signup into a billed account. An OpenAI-run benchmark had Astra solving a Navier-Stokes fluid-dynamics problem in 88 hours of compute, a result meant to signal this is a serious tool, not a chatbot. Aya Nakamura's AI Desk makes the point that matters here: the eval score was never the story. The story is that the most resourced lab in the business could not provision enough paid seats on its own infrastructure to meet a week-one rush.

That infrastructure is Microsoft Azure, and the freeze is a live reading of how much load one lab can absorb on a single cloud platform before the front door has to close. It says nothing about the enterprise contracts a Hong Kong or Singapore firm might already be running inside a regulated workflow; those agreements sit untouched. But it says a great deal about what's actually scarce in this market right now. Moonshot AI's separate push toward two billion dollars in annual revenue, reported the same week, is the other half of that picture. When the frontier lab's own consumer tier can't onboard fast enough, capacity, not capability, is the thing being rationed.

Elsewhere, the story about what AI can be made to do without anyone's permission continues to file from multiple desks. Kai Tanner's Cyber Intel desk has the fuller account of Anthropic's disclosure that state-linked hackers used Claude to rebuild malware faster than defenders could re-detect it, a detail that matters more to a bank's SecOps team than the topline distillation numbers. Mei Chen reports that Beijing reinforced artillery along the Taiwan Strait on September 11, a full day before it filed a formal protest over the Taiwanese vice president's routine stopover in Europe. That order tells Taipei and Washington the deterrence math runs through military posture, not diplomatic behavior. Vincent Lai's ledger adds that Beijing is fighting the narrative of this week's flare incident near a contested reef harder than it's fighting over the reef itself.

For a reader in this city, the throughline is capacity, not capability. Astra's benchmark score was never in doubt; what broke was the plumbing behind it, and that plumbing runs through exactly one cloud platform absorbing demand from every lab at once. The firms making procurement decisions this quarter, on AI vendors or on the cyber defenses meant to catch what those vendors' tools now enable, are the ones actually weighing that concentration risk against their contracts. The ones still reading the benchmark score are answering yesterday's question.

Beijing Protests Taiwan VP's Trip While Reinforcing the Strait

Mei Chen on the Strait artillery move Beijing made a day before it even filed its protest over Taiwan's VP trip.

Continue reading

Taipei sent its vice president to Europe on a routine transit, and Beijing answered with artillery, not just a diplomatic note.

Taiwan Vice President's stopover en route to an EU visit drew a formal protest from Beijing this week, the kind of statement China's foreign ministry issues after nearly every senior Taiwanese official's trip abroad. What the protest omitted is the part that matters: China had already reinforced artillery positions along the Taiwan Strait on September 11, a day before the objection was even filed. The sequencing inverts the official story. A protest note is supposed to be the whole of Beijing's response to a provocation. Here it is the follow-up paragraph to a decision made before the trip's optics were even in the news cycle.

Read the artillery move as the actual position and the protest as the press release, and the picture is a Beijing that treats Taiwanese diplomatic travel as a pretext already priced into its posture, not a trigger for it. That matters because it tells Taipei and Washington the deterrence calculus does not run through diplomatic behavior. Toning down VP travel will not soften what is a standing military posture along the Strait; it only removes Beijing's rhetorical cover for moves it had already made.

China Fires Flares at Philippine Aircraft Over Reef

Vincent Lai on why Beijing is fighting the story of the reef flare incident harder than the incident itself, and what that tells Manila's bond desk.

Continue reading

Manila's account of a flare incident near a contested reef lands on a desk that has spent weeks trying to keep South China Sea friction off the ledger it actually cares about, Manila's dollar debt.

China fired flares at a Philippine military aircraft this week, according to Manila's account carried by the South China Morning Post, the latest escalation in a dispute the Chinese foreign ministry has, since September 11, been recasting as a Philippine information operation rather than a maritime one, having accused Manila's defense secretary of leaking a diplomatic note and "grandstanding." The China Coast Guard has not disputed the flare account itself. It has disputed who gets to narrate it.

The distinction matters to a reader who prices Philippine risk, because the Bureau of the Treasury in Manila is mid-cycle on its 2026 external borrowing programme, and the flare incident does not move that calculus the way a blockade or a collision would, or, more precisely, it moves the *narrative* risk premium without moving the physical one, since no vessel was damaged and no crew was injured. Beijing's choice to fight the story rather than the reef, the same posture its foreign ministry took toward the Taiwan VP's EU trip this week, tells the Treasury's external funding desk that the coast guard budget is running the confrontation and the foreign ministry is running the cleanup. The two offices are not yet reading from the same page, and until they are, Manila's sovereign spread absorbs the noise, not the risk.

Anthropic Says It Disrupted Claude-Assisted Hacking Campaigns

Kai Tanner's standing filing on Anthropic's malware-iteration disclosure, the one with an actual patch-cadence lesson for APAC banks.

Continue reading

Anthropic disclosed that state-linked and criminal groups used Claude for malware iteration and mass credential harvesting, handing defenders a rare look at how the abuse actually worked.

Anthropic said this week it disrupted a Russian state-sponsored group that built an AI-assisted workflow around Claude specifically to rebuild malware faster than defenders could re-detect it, a technique-development loop rather than a one-off jailbreak. Separately, Anthropic said financially motivated and China-linked espionage actors abused Claude to pull secrets out of 1.8 million Android apps, and that it identified industrial-scale distillation attacks against Claude from seven China-based labs, including Alibaba, Moonshot, DeepSeek and Z.ai. Anthropic's account names the technique and the scale. It does not name which detection signatures the rebuilt malware evaded, which is the artifact a bank's SecOps team would actually want.

For a Hong Kong or Singapore bank, the live regulatory question is not the distillation story, it is the malware-iteration one: HKMA's TM-G-1 and MAS TRM both assume attacker development cycles measured in weeks, and BIS said this month that routine patching schedules are "increasingly inadequate" as AI compresses that cycle to minutes. A defender running detection tuned against last month's malware family, refreshed on a quarterly cycle, is testing controls against a threat that no longer holds still between tests.

No vendor named in Anthropic's disclosure is one an APAC bank runs directly, so no product change follows from this story. The control that matters is internal: detection-signature refresh cadence tied to threat-intel ingestion, not to a patch calendar. A bank that still refreshes signatures on the same schedule it patches Windows is defending against last quarter's malware.
