Windows Malware Lets AI Models Vote On Attacks

Cisco Talos reverse-engineered a Windows implant that puts attack decisions to a vote among up to four AI models instead of a human operator.

Cisco Talos published research on September 22 describing CLOSEDQUORUM, a Windows implant that queries up to four AI models and acts on whatever the majority votes for, rather than waiting on instructions from an operator's command-and-control server. The mechanism is a quorum: the binary submits its situation, what's running, what's been blocked, what's exposed, to each model, collects a vote on the next move, and executes the outcome. Talos had to reverse-engineer the voting protocol to work out what the malware had decided, because there was no operator issuing commands from fixed infrastructure to intercept.

Kai Tanner's desk has the full mechanics: Talos doesn't name which four models CLOSEDQUORUM queries, and BleepingComputer reported the same week that a separate financially motivated actor used open-source AI agent frameworks to run skimmer operations across more than 100 retail sites, lifting over 600,000 card records. The two campaigns are not connected, but both remove the signal defenders have traditionally used to attribute and disrupt an intrusion: a steady operator working from known infrastructure.

The same week, Anthropic disclosed that researchers used Claude itself to compromise OpenAI's systems, and shipped Claude Opus 5.5 with tighter cyber guardrails alongside the disclosure. Aya Nakamura's desk has that story: an AI system capable of finding and chaining vulnerabilities well enough to breach a rival lab is the same system enterprise security teams want running their own penetration tests, and Anthropic tightened guardrails in the same release cycle. Google separately confirmed its own Gemini models had been used to breach three companies back in May.

The decision-making and vulnerability-chaining that used to require a skilled human operator, on either side of the fence, now runs on model infrastructure that doesn't need to be staffed round the clock. For defenders, a live operator's fingerprint is no longer a given. Windows Malware Lets Four AI Models Vote On Its Next Move

Kai Tanner reverse-engineers the malware that lets four AI models vote on its next move, no operator required.

Continue reading

CLOSEDQUORUM hands attack decisions to a quorum of language models instead of a human operator, and Cisco Talos had to reverse-engineer a voting protocol to find out what it decided.

Cisco Talos published its CAIRN research on September 22 describing CLOSEDQUORUM, a Windows implant that queries up to four AI models and acts on whatever the majority votes for, rather than waiting on instructions from an operator's command-and-control server. The mechanism is a quorum: the binary submits its situation (what's running, what's been blocked, what's exposed) to each model, collects a vote on the next move, and executes the outcome. That is a design choice with a specific consequence. It removes the one signal defenders have always relied on to attribute and disrupt a campaign, a steady operator issuing commands from a fixed infrastructure, and replaces it with a decision loop that can vary run to run depending on which models answer and how.

The effect shows up downstream. BleepingComputer reported the same week that a separate financially motivated actor used open-source AI agent frameworks to run skimmer operations across more than 100 retail sites, lifting over 600,000 card records. Talos's binary and the skimmer campaign are not the same operation, but they share a premise: the decision-making that used to require a live operator now runs on infrastructure the attacker doesn't have to staff. Talos's writeup frames CLOSEDQUORUM as a "shift in effort displacement," which is accurate and also undersells it. Effort displacement usually means a human did less typing. Here it means the artifact defenders normally chase, the C2 channel carrying operator intent, doesn't exist in the same form.

What's actually implicated is the model layer itself, not a named vendor's product. Talos doesn't name which four models CLOSEDQUORUM queries, and that gap matters more than the binary's other capabilities: any provider whose API can be reached anonymously and queried for a next-step recommendation is a candidate component in this architecture. The malware's resilience depends entirely on model API access staying open and unattributed, not on any exploit or credential theft. That is the fact that settles how serious this is: the binary itself is unremarkable, and the availability of anonymous, unmonitored model access is what makes the design work.

Claude Broke Into OpenAI, And That's The Selling Point

Aya Nakamura on Claude breaching OpenAI, and why Anthropic's guardrail tightening the same week is the real story.

Continue reading

Anthropic disclosed that researchers used Claude itself to compromise OpenAI's systems, then shipped a model built to be harder to weaponize the same way.

Ars Technica reported researchers used Claude to hack OpenAI, and the same week Google confirmed its Gemini models had been used to breach three companies back in May. Both disclosures land days after Anthropic shipped Claude Opus 5.5 with what The Verge described as tighter cyber guardrails, and TechCrunch reported the release came with lower prices matched to Fable-level performance. The sequence matters because it is not a story about a model getting hacked. It is a story about a model doing the hacking, which is a different capability with a different customer. An AI system that can find and chain vulnerabilities well enough to compromise a rival lab's infrastructure is the same system a bank's security team wants running its own penetration tests. Anthropic's guardrail tightening in the same release cycle is the tell: the company is not just building a better assistant, it is trying to keep the offensive capability it just demonstrated from becoming the reason regulators or enterprise buyers stop trusting the tool.

That reframes what "guardrails" are for in this market. Microsoft's separate disclosure of an AI-assisted platform that compromised 12,000 accounts, and Wired's report of a zero-day shipping in Meta's Muse agent, both point the same direction: the gap between an agent's demonstrated capability and an enterprise's ability to contain it is now the product decision, not a footnote. AWS's own security blog this month is teaching customers how to pipe Bedrock guardrail interventions into standard security telemetry, and how Benchling isolates untrusted agent-generated code across tenants on Bedrock AgentCore, because the containment problem showed up before the governance tooling matured enough to answer it. A bank's compliance team evaluating Claude for internal use now has to price two things at once: what the model can do to their systems, and what it could do to someone else's if the access controls slip. Anthropic's next test is not a benchmark score. It is whether Opus 5.5's guardrails hold the next time someone tries what worked on OpenAI.

Two Officers Fell Before Xi Sat Down With Trump

Vincent Lai on two generals purged from China's military command three days before Xi sits down with Trump.

Continue reading

The Central Military Commission expelled two generals in the same week Beijing's ambassador drew a red line on Taiwan, and the sequencing tells the Pentagon more than the summit will.

The Central Military Commission's disciplinary announcement on September 22, naming two senior officers expelled for corruption and disloyalty, landed three days before Xi Jinping sat down with Donald Trump in Washington on September 24. Reuters reported the same week that the broader purge of military commanders, running since 2023 under the Commission's own inspectorate, has likely cost the People's Liberation Army some war-fighting readiness even as it tightens Xi's control over the body that would have to execute any Taiwan Strait order. China's ambassador to Washington had already told reporters on September 22 that Taiwan remains a red line Beijing will not trade away at the table, or, more precisely, a line the Foreign Ministry can hold publicly precisely because the Commission spent the same week demonstrating internally that the chain of command answers to Xi alone, not to the officer corps whose loyalty just failed two more tests.

Read against the arms sale the Pentagon has been sitting on since Beijing raised it as a threat on September 19, the sequencing matters more than either government's summit statement will. A 14 billion dollar Taiwan arms package delayed by the White House is a signal Xi's negotiators can carry into the room as leverage; two generals expelled by the Commission's own inspectorate is a signal that the room Xi negotiates from has just gotten narrower, not wider, because a purged high command cannot be the guarantor of restraint that Washington's arms-sale calculus assumes it is. The Commission's inspectorate, not the Foreign Ministry's spokesperson, is the desk that will decide whether Thursday's summit produces a deal Xi can actually enforce.

UBS's Swiss Capital Blow Has No Hong Kong Trigger Yet

Rachel Lam on why a Swiss capital vote isn't the compliance deadline Hong Kong desks should actually be tracking.

Continue reading

A Swiss parliamentary vote could force UBS to hold billions more capital, but the read for Hong Kong's regulated firms is HKEX's own rulebook, not Bern.

Look, the number everyone's quoting is Goldman's: the Swiss upper house backed the tougher 90 percent capital plan this week, and Goldman's desk put the potential hit to UBS at $17 billion (parliament still has to reconcile chambers, so that figure is a ceiling, not a bill). Separately, HKEX filed a consultation on Phase II of its Listing Framework Competitiveness Review on September 21, and floated its first change to trading hours in 14 years. Neither is a Hong Kong regulatory action on UBS. The SFC's Intermediaries Division has no filing this week naming UBS's Hong Kong branch or its capital treatment here, and the HKMA's Banking Policy Department, which would own any local Basel add-on, published nothing in the past three days beyond Exchange Fund Bills tender results and two scam alerts.

So for a CRO at a Hong Kong-regulated firm, this week's actual instrument is HKEX's own: the Phase II consultation on listing framework competitiveness, open for comment, sits with the Listing Division, not the SFC (the SFC's Corporate Finance Division only touches this through dual filing of listing applications, and this consultation isn't a listing application). A longer trading day, if it lands, changes market-risk monitoring windows and end-of-day reconciliation cutoffs for anyone running Hong Kong equities systems, which is a real operational question for a compliance head here even though the headline story is Swiss capital math three time zones away.

Mark the HKEX consultation close date once it's published in the paper; that's the deadline a Hong Kong compliance desk actually has to answer, not the Swiss parliamentary calendar.

The Number That Sets Quantum's 2028 Deadline

Magnus Honeyfield on the coherence-time number quietly setting quantum computing's 2028 deadline.

Continue reading

The Department of Energy's $215 million quantum competition names a scoreboard, and this week two hardware teams showed why the scoreboard is currently losing.

The number that matters in quantum computing right now isn't qubit count, it's how long a qubit stays a qubit. Rigetti's 108-qubit Cepheus system, detailed this week, is bottlenecked on coherence time: the window during which a qubit holds its quantum state before random noise scrambles it back into an ordinary bit. Every extra microsecond of coherence buys more time for a calculation to run before it needs correcting, and gate fidelity, the odds that a single logic operation on a qubit does what it was told, falls apart if the qubit decoheres mid-operation. Rigetti's public work this week is aimed at stretching that window on superconducting hardware that already exists in Fremont, California, not at adding qubits. Separately, IonQ reported running real-time quantum error correction, the constant background process of catching and fixing those noise-induced errors before they compound, on a standard laptop chip rather than custom silicon built for the job. That's a software and control-systems result: it says the correction math can ride on off-the-shelf hardware, which matters because every dedicated chip a quantum lab has to build is cost and lead time stacked on top of the qubits themselves.

Both results answer to the same yardstick. The Department of Energy's Quantum Genesis competition, announced last week with $215 million behind it, is betting that money on private teams building fault-tolerant quantum computers, machines that can run indefinitely long calculations because error correction outpaces error accumulation, by 2028. That single benchmark, QPU-BM, already put Google, IBM, and Quantinuum's processors 100,000 times slower than classical simulation on September 15. Rigetti's coherence work and IonQ's laptop-chip error correction are both prototype-stage moves against that same 100,000x gap, not finished machines. The next gate is concrete: whether any team, funded by the DOE program or not, posts a QPU-BM score that closes the gap rather than just explains why it's still open.

What a Fast-Food Burger Actually Costs You in Minutes

Sora Whitlam on what a fast-food burger actually costs you, measured in minutes of statistical lifespan.

Continue reading

An epidemiological model converts processed-meat and red-meat risk data into an estimated toll on lifespan for a single burger.

A 2025 analysis published on NutritionFacts, built on pooled cohort data from studies including the EPIC and NIH-AARP diet cohorts, applies the "microlife" method (a metric developed by Cambridge statistician David Spiegelhalter) to estimate that a serving of processed red meat, the size in a typical fast-food burger patty, is associated with a loss of roughly 20 to 30 minutes of expected lifespan. The number is not measured in any single person. It is a population average derived from relative-risk figures for colorectal cancer and cardiovascular disease in large observational cohorts, spread across the average remaining life expectancy of the group studied.

The method matters as much as the number. A microlife estimate does not track your DNA or your arteries. It multiplies a study's hazard ratio (the increased relative risk of dying in a given period) by the average years of life left in the population sampled, then converts the result to minutes. That is why the same tool spits out a similar 20-to-30-minute estimate for a cigarette. Both figures describe averaged population risk, not a countdown clock inside your body. A single burger does not "cost" you thirty minutes the way a subway fare costs you money. It shifts your odds, in a data set of thousands, by an amount small enough that no individual will ever feel it directly, and large enough that repeated over a population of millions, it shows up as a measurable number of colorectal cancer and heart disease deaths.

Rays Clinch AL East by No-Hitting the Yankees

Dev Chatterjee on Rasmussen's near-no-hitter clinching the Rays a division the Yankees can't seem to buy.

Continue reading

Drew Rasmussen took a no-hitter into the eighth to finish off the division Tampa Bay has now owned for most of the decade, and the Yankees clinched a playoff spot anyway on the same trip.

Drew Rasmussen carried a no-hitter into the eighth inning Tuesday, gave up exactly one hit, and closed it out with 11 strikeouts as the Rays beat the Yankees 6-1 to clinch the AL East. That is not a division title, that is a mugging with a receipt. New York had actually taken the doubleheader opener behind Carlos Rodon's three-hit shutout, so the Yankees flew home from Tampa Bay having won a game and lost a division in the same 24 hours, which is the kind of week that ends with someone throwing a clipboard. They still clinched their third straight postseason berth, Aaron Judge missing more than three months and all, so nobody in the Bronx should be checking out of the hotel yet.

Here is the number that should bother the sport more than the standings: Tampa Bay is doing this with a payroll that ranks near the bottom of the league, in a stadium so decrepit the team has spent a decade angling for a new one in St. Petersburg while the Yankees spend like the AL East is a subscription service. The Rays keep winning the division on player development and pitching depth, not on record checks, and the Yankees keep making the tournament anyway because $300 million buys you a floor even on a bad week. Both things are true. Only one of them is sustainable for the other 28 teams watching.

No Tampering Found In Sports Drinks, Fear Stays Anyway

Cheung Kwok-keung on why the sports drink scare outran the all-clear that followed it.

Continue reading

Health officials tested the sports drinks everyone got spooked about this week and found nothing wrong, but good luck telling that to a parent standing in the supermarket aisle.

Somebody got sick, somebody said "tampering," and by Tuesday half of Hong Kong was reading ingredient labels on sports drinks like they were defusing a bomb. RTHK reported this week that officials checked and found no evidence of tampering. No contamination, no foreign substance, nothing. The bottles are fine.

Here's the thing about a scare like this: the "all clear" never travels as far as the scare did. The rumour got a headline and a weekend of nervous group chats. The clearance gets one line, buried under bigger news, read by maybe a tenth as many people. So the drink stays on the shelf, the tests say it's safe, and somewhere a mum is still checking the seal twice before she lets her kid drink it. Nobody did anything wrong here, not the company, not the regulator who actually went and tested the thing properly. It's just that fear moves at Temple Street pace and facts move at government pace, and by the time the facts show up, everyone's already made up their mind at the fridge.
