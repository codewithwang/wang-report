Cathay Refunds in Hours, Helpers Lose Their Sunday

Hong Kong's storm machinery refunds Cathay passengers within hours, but offers domestic helpers no make-up day when a typhoon eats their one day off.
張 國 強 · Cheung Kwok-keung HK Desk, Senior Correspondent Sunday, July 26, 2026

The Machine That Works

Hong Kong Observatory raised Strong Wind Signal No. 3 at 1:20pm on Saturday. That's the mid-level warning that tells the city a storm is close enough to start battening down. By 10:10pm the same night it was upgraded to Signal No. 8, the level at which offices close, schools shut, and the whole city's weekend effectively switches off. Typhoon Noul was sitting about 270 kilometres east-southeast of Hong Kong at the time, packing sustained winds of 140 km/h. Its track was taking it between Huizhou and Shanwei, two cities on the Chinese coast, close enough to Hong Kong to bring damaging wind but far enough that the city got the wind and not a direct hit.

More than 150 flights were cancelled at the airport across Saturday and Sunday. Cathay Pacific pulled every Hong Kong arrival and departure from 1:15am until 6pm on Sunday, and if you had a seat on one of those flights, you already know what happens next, because the whole thing runs almost automatically. Rebooking. A refund path. A hotel voucher if you got stuck somewhere. An apology email with your name spelled right. That is what a mature consumer protection machine looks like: the airline absorbs the disruption and hands you a fix within hours. Across the border, more than 20,000 people in Guangdong were evacuated by Friday night, a government machine of its own, moving people out before the wind arrived.

The Machine That Skips

Now the part that does not run at all. For hundreds of thousands of domestic helpers in Hong Kong, Sunday is not a courtesy, it is the one contractual rest day the Employment Ordinance guarantees them each week, usually spent in Causeway Bay, on the footbridges, in Victoria Park, sitting on cardboard with friends for a few hours because a dorm room in someone else's flat is not really a place to relax. Signal No. 8 landing overnight into this Sunday meant the footbridges and Victoria Park stayed empty this weekend, because the one day helpers get to leave the flat and see each other was swallowed by the storm.

Here is the rule that causes that: a severe weather day is not treated as a statutory rest day under the Employment Ordinance, so when a storm falls on a helper's existing day off, as it just did, no make-up day is owed to her. The Fair Employment Agency, a group that advocates for migrant domestic workers, spells this out plainly on its own website: no typhoon, no extra day, no form to fill in about it. That gap is what separates the two machines in this column. An office worker gets a storm holiday added to the calendar automatically, no request needed, because the closure applies to the whole workplace. A domestic helper gets nothing automatically, because her rest day is a personal entitlement tied to her contract, not a workplace closure. She has to ask her employer for a different day, and the law does not require anyone to say yes.

This week it is helpers and a stolen Sunday. Signal 8 will come down by Monday and Victoria Park will fill up again next week like nothing happened.
