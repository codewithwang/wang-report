8.8 Million Adults Later, HHS's Ultra-Processed Definition Is Still in Review

The largest ultra-processed food study ever assembled found dose-dependent harm in 8.8 million adults, landing exactly as HHS decides whether the evidence is finally strong enough to regulate.
Sora Whitlam Science and Health Desk, Senior Correspondent (Health) Sunday, September 27, 2026

The Largest Dataset Yet

A research team led by Liu X., publishing in Family Medicine and Community Health, a BMJ Group journal, on September 22, pooled 51 cohort studies tracking 8,819,894 adults across the Americas, Europe, Asia and Oceania to ask a single dose-response question: does eating more ultra-processed food predict worse health outcomes as intake rises. The answer, tracked gram by gram rather than as a yes-or-no category, is the most granular version of this question anyone has published. Each additional 100 grams of ultra-processed food eaten per day tracked with a 14 percent higher risk of a cardiovascular event, a 4 percent higher cancer risk, a 3 percent higher risk of death from any cause, and a 2 percent higher risk of metabolic syndrome or diabetes. Compare the heaviest ultra-processed eaters against the lightest and the gaps widen further: 31 percent higher digestive disease risk, 27 percent higher depression and anxiety, 24 percent higher cardiovascular and metabolic syndrome risk, 18 percent higher all-cause mortality. Dose-response curves like this are what epidemiologists look for when they want to rule out coincidence: if a relationship strengthens steadily as exposure increases, across 51 independently run studies on four continents, it is harder to wave off as noise in one country's diet data.

What The Study Can't Prove

The study was funded by China's National Natural Science Foundation, the Natural Science Foundation of Jiangxi Province, and the Jiangxi Province Thousands of Plan talent program. No food company or industry group appears in the funding disclosure, which matters because most of the ultra-processed food research that gets contested for bias runs the other direction, industry-funded studies tending to find weaker links than independently funded ones. Liu and colleagues are also explicit about the study's ceiling: this is 51 cohort studies built from people self-reporting what they ate, tracked forward over years, and cohort data can show two things moving together without proving one causes the other. The paper cannot cleanly separate 'ultra-processed' as its own harmful category from the sugar, salt, refined starch and saturated fat that typically ride along inside an ultra-processed product. A bag of chips is both ultra-processed and high in sodium and fat, and this analysis cannot fully tell you which feature is doing the damage, or whether it is the combination. That gap, association without a proven mechanism, is the opening the Consumer Brands Association and other food industry groups have used to argue 'ultra-processed' is too vague a category to write into law. This study does not close that gap. What it does is make the association harder to dismiss as thin: 8.8 million adults is one of the largest evidence bases nutrition epidemiology has produced on this question without running a decades-long randomized trial on humans, which nobody is going to fund or get approved to run.

Washington's Move to Make

On August 10, HHS and the USDA submitted the first proposed federal definition of ultra-processed food for final review, the step that would give the term legal weight under US food policy for the first time. That definition would then flow into FDA food labeling rules, into school lunch nutrition standards, and into which products qualify for purchase under SNAP, the food assistance program. Whether a snack cake, a frozen dinner, or a flavored yogurt counts as 'ultra-processed' under that definition decides whether it can be marketed to schools or bought with a SNAP card. Food industry groups have opposed the draft since it appeared, arguing a NOVA-style classification (grouping foods by how industrially they are made, not by what nutrients they contain) will sweep up products that are otherwise nutritionally unremarkable and confuse shoppers who already read labels for sugar and sodium. That objection has been paired with a second claim: that the science linking ultra-processing itself, apart from the sugar and salt riding along with it, to disease was too thin to legislate around. The Liu paper does not resolve whether ultra-processing is its own distinct harm or a proxy for what is inside these products. It does remove the word 'thin' from that sentence. HHS's draft definition is still sitting in review, unresolved since August, now weighed against 8.8 million adults' worth of data sitting in the public record instead of against silence.

HHS's draft definition still sits in review, unresolved since August, now filed next to a paper industry cannot credibly call thin. The evidence changed. The paperwork has not. HHS must now decide whether to finalize that definition, or extend the review past its current stall.
