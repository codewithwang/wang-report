Anthropic's 950-Agent Swarm Finds A New Enzyme In 21 Hours

Anthropic's new life-sciences lab ran 950 AI agents against public DNA for 21 hours and flagged a genuinely new enzyme system, but no regulator yet knows how to audit a machine's own judgment about what counts as a discovery.
Magnus Honeyfield Science and Health Desk, Senior Correspondent Sunday, September 27, 2026

The Lab Built From A Purchase

In April 2026 Anthropic bought a small outfit called Coefficient Bio and turned it into a life-sciences lab, betting that the same AI agents it sells for coding and customer support could also do the first pass of biology research: not summarizing papers other scientists already wrote, but reading raw genetic sequence data and finding something nobody had described yet. It is a claim that the agents can be the ones doing the noticing. On September 23 the lab published its first attempt at proving it: a preprint describing a new enzyme system in bacteriophages, the viruses that infect bacteria, which it named ART, for array-associated reverse transcriptase. Before running anything, the lab set its own ceiling: work only at BSL-1 and BSL-2, the two lowest of four biosafety containment levels, meaning organisms that pose little to no danger to healthy adults, and touch nothing that infects humans. Nobody made them do that. No regulator has yet written a rule for this kind of work, so Anthropic wrote its own.

How Nine Hundred Agents Found ART

Anthropic set roughly 950 copies of its Claude model loose in parallel for 21 hours, burning about 210 million tokens (the units of text the model reads and writes, so 210 million is a rough proxy for how much reading and reasoning happened) combing public DNA databases, the kind any researcher can already download, for genetic patterns that looked unusual. The swarm started with thousands of candidate systems and, through iterative rounds of comparison and elimination, narrowed that list to about 20 worth a human's time. Only then did people take over: scientists at BSL-1/2 physically tested the shortlist and confirmed one system, ART, actually exists as a three-part unit; a reverse-transcriptase enzyme (which copies RNA back into DNA, the reverse of the usual direction), a partner gene, and a repeating DNA array resembling CRISPR's signature repeat structure, the pattern that makes CRISPR systems easy to spot in genomic data. The AI did not confirm ART works. It confirmed ART is real and unusual enough to be worth confirming, a step beyond what earlier automated gene-annotation tools, which flag sequences matching known families, are built to do.

The Gap Nobody Is Filling

950 agents, working without a human in the loop for 21 hours, decided among themselves what counted as 'compelling' before any scientist looked at the data. That judgment call, what to flag and what to discard, happened entirely inside the machine. TechCrunch's reporting on the run confirms the shape of it: massive parallel search, aggressive narrowing, human review only at the very end. Anthropic's own BSL-1/2 ceiling is doing the work a biosecurity regulator would normally do, and it is voluntary. Nothing stops a less cautious lab, or a future version of this same lab under different pressure, from pointing a similar swarm at BSL-3 material or human pathogens. Independent peer review of the ART preprint has not happened yet. ART's actual biological function, whether it is a novel gene-editing tool or something else entirely, remains unknown. What is known is that the discovery pipeline that found it can run again, faster, on something riskier, before any outside body has agreed on how to check its homework.

ART is real, confirmed in a wet lab, and still functionally a mystery. No regulator has yet reviewed the decision process that produced it. Anthropic's self-imposed BSL-1/2 limit is the only guardrail in place right now. No peer-review venue or timeline for validating ART's function has been announced, and no review body or deadline exists yet for auditing the swarm's judgment before the next one runs on something less contained.
