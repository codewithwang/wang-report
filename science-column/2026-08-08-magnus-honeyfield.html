Unverified reporting, attributed to Wang Report: The Seven-Hour Answer

Unverified reporting, attributed to Wang Report: Japan's Fugaku supercomputer spent roughly 700,000 processor-hours this summer trying to reproduce a single materials simulation, and it still could not match what an IBM quantum computer did in seven hours. Fugaku is not a toy: it was built for exactly this kind of physics problem. The task, run under a partnership between IBM and quantum-software firm Qedma, modeled how a material responds when struck by a laser: the electrons get knocked out of their resting state and the surrounding lattice starts to oscillate, and predicting the shape of that oscillation is precisely what classical simulators are supposed to be good at. IBM's quantum chip, called Heron, encoded the material's quantum state directly onto its own qubits (the basic quantum bit, which unlike a normal bit can hold a mix of 0 and 1 at once) instead of approximating the physics step by step the way a classical machine must. Fugaku ground through that approximation for the equivalent of roughly 80 years of one processor's time and never landed on the right oscillation. Eighty processor-years against seven hours is the number that should worry whoever is budgeting the next generation of classical supercomputers built to handle exactly this kind of physics problem.
