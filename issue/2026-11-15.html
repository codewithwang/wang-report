The Test of Fox’s Power Is How Its Rivals Reach Viewers

The Roku acquisition leaves the Justice Department relying too heavily on viewers’ ability to switch platforms to protect the businesses competing on them.
Walter Wang Editor-in-Chief
AI-assisted editorial · Human direction and editorial oversight

Fox has committed to preserve existing contractual firewalls around its competitors’ sensitive data on Roku for at least three years. The information covered includes clicks, searches, viewing and voice activity. These are unusually intimate details for an antitrust announcement. Fox has bought a business through which it can distribute its own programmes alongside other companies’ programmes while collecting information about their audiences.

The US Department of Justice published those commitments on November 6, when it closed its investigation into the acquisition. Fox announced completion the same day. According to the company, Roku’s platform reaches more than 100 million households worldwide.

The Justice Department concluded that competitive harm was unlikely, and its reasoning deserves a serious hearing. Roku needs a broad offering to attract viewers. Damaging rival content providers could therefore hurt Roku’s revenues and reputation. Viewers could switch to another connected television platform. The department also judged that entry by competing free, advertising-supported streaming providers would be enough to address its competition concerns.

The department makes a plausible case for Fox keeping Roku attractive to viewers. It is less convincing about how rival suppliers retain bargaining power. A platform can remain popular with its customers while demanding more from the businesses trading on it.

The Justice Department must examine the terms Fox sets for its rivals even when viewers are happy to stay.

Fox has brought Roku under the same ownership as Tubi and FOX One, along with its other media businesses. It now has interests on both sides of a commercial negotiation, supplying entertainment and providing a platform through which entertainment reaches viewers. There is nothing inherently unworkable about that arrangement. A successful platform benefits from offering programmes its owner could never produce alone.

Fox would make Roku less useful by driving every rival away. It has good reasons to avoid doing so. The acquisition announcement forecasts approximately $400 million in annual cost savings within two years of completion, alongside possible revenue benefits. Those are company expectations, but they provide a commercial explanation for the transaction that does not depend on mistreating competitors.

Fox could gain smaller advantages over its rivals without viewers noticing.

A rival service could remain available while becoming harder to discover. A content provider might accept less favourable terms because losing access to an audience would be more costly than accepting them. Neither possibility requires viewers to become dissatisfied enough to change platforms. Both are risks to examine, though the announcements establish no such conduct by Fox after the acquisition.

Fox’s commitments address some of these risks. It promises to continue carrying non-Fox content for at least three years, consistent with historical Roku practice. It promises not to discriminate against non-Fox content in search results. Users will retain the ability to rearrange applications on their home screens. A viewer who knows exactly what to watch has useful protections.

But finding a programme you know is different from discovering one you do not. The department’s statement does not spell out comparable rules for recommendations or advertising. That omission does not establish that abuse will occur. It does mean that a promise about search results cannot cover every way audiences encounter competing content.

Fox has also committed to continue providing third-party data products on commercially reasonable terms consistent with Roku’s standard distribution agreement. Separately, it will maintain existing contractual firewalls protecting competitively sensitive non-Fox content data for at least three years.

Audience information can influence bargaining before any programme disappears. Knowing what people search for, sample and return to can help a business understand demand. A supplier negotiating with a platform owner that also competes for viewers has good reason to care who can use that knowledge. The firewall addresses a commercial conflict at the centre of the deal.

The announcement sets no single expiry date for Fox’s commitments. The stated three-year minimum applies to carriage and the existing data firewalls. The announcement gives no equivalent duration for search neutrality or home-screen arrangements. It gives none for third-party data products either. Nor does “at least three years” mean protection ends the following morning. The department should explain what evidence will inform their continuation.

For an Asian producer seeking American audiences, access to a large distribution platform can be valuable even when the relationship is unequal. How viewers find its work and what audience information it receives affect the value of that access. So does the way disputes are handled. A promise to keep the content available answers only part of the commercial question.

The department’s strongest defence is that competition will do much of this work. Fox would have powerful reasons to change course if viewers left and advertisers followed. Losing attractive programmes would add to that pressure. Heavy restrictions could also obstruct improvements that benefit audiences and suppliers alike. Regulators should leave room for the company to deliver the gains it forecasts.

The department could strengthen that defence with evidence about how the combined business treats competing providers. It says it will continue evaluating competition and may act as circumstances change. It should explain how it will assess complaints and seek evidence about discovery and data access. It should also explain how it will review the commitments with stated minimum periods before those periods run out. Waiting for widespread viewer departures would set an unnecessarily late test.

Fox has acquired lasting control over a major route to viewers. Its competitors will negotiate with Fox long before most viewers consider switching platforms. The Justice Department should judge the deal by whether those businesses can compete fairly while viewers are still content to stay.
