Give Owners a Way to Stop Dangerous Work

Hong Kong’s maintenance reforms should give residents an enforceable route to intervention and require the government to show which existing powers failed.
Walter Wang Editor-in-Chief
AI-assisted editorial · Human direction and editorial oversight

Hong Kong’s proposed crackdown on renovation bid rigging would reach the appointment of consultants, before a builder submits a price. Owners choose the professional who will help them understand the work and assess the bids before spending their money. If that appointment is captured, competition can be compromised before anyone opens an envelope. [3]

According to Dimsum Daily’s October 30 account of the government’s proposals, the Wang Fuk Court fire committee reported serious collusion involving consultants, professionals, contractors and individuals linked to triad groups. The proposed response includes a maximum prison term of ten years and a fine set at the highest of HK$10 million, three times the commercial gains, or 50 per cent of the undertaking’s local turnover for each year of contravention. It also includes stronger investigative tools, backed by coordinated enforcement. These remain proposals. They should be judged by who gains the power to intervene, at what stage and on whose evidence. [3]

The government should intervene earlier in the procurement process. But it must distinguish the power to investigate a dishonest tender from the authority and duty to halt dangerous work. Both matter. Prosecuting a rigged tender does not establish that officials can stop unsafe work.

An owner may need professional help to assess technical specifications and contractors’ claims. They delegate expertise without intending to surrender control. Collusion at the advisory stage reverses that arrangement: the person retained to scrutinise the transaction becomes part of the transaction that needs scrutiny. Owners may receive several competing bids without a real choice between them.

Extending criminal liability to consultant appointments is defensible. It addresses an identifiable point at which owners can lose bargaining power. Procurement begins before a tender is issued and continues through the award of a contract. A ten-year sentence attracts attention, but covering the appointment that shapes every subsequent decision may do more useful work.

Officials must also answer the strongest objection to expanding their authority. Perhaps they already possessed sufficient powers and failed to exercise them. Information may have been available but badly handled. Officials may have neglected responsibilities they understood, or agencies may have waited for one another. If so, adding another offence could expand the statute book while leaving the operational failure intact.

Dimsum Daily’s account does not establish which existing safety powers were inadequate, or whether their absence prevented a particular intervention. It cannot support a conclusion that broader stop-work powers would have prevented the fire. Officials must establish that case before seeking additional authority. They should identify the decision that could not lawfully be taken and the information needed to take it, then name the institution responsible.

Where officials had sufficient authority, the remedy must include an enforceable obligation to decide. A resident raising a substantiated safety concern should be entitled to an assessment by a responsible authority, followed by a reasoned answer within a defined period. An unresolved disagreement between departments should trigger escalation. Residents should not have to master the government’s internal division of responsibilities to get an answer.

Where officials lacked authority, a targeted amendment can supply it. The conditions for emergency intervention should be clear. Officials should record their reasons, and those affected should have a route to review. Those safeguards protect owners as well as contractors. Stopping work can impose substantial costs. Continuing unsafe work can impose costs that no subsequent award of damages can adequately repair. Officials must be able to decide before a catastrophe removes all doubt.

The proposed bid-rigging task force offers some grounds for support. It would bring together senior representatives of the police, the Independent Commission Against Corruption and the Competition Commission. Its remit includes intelligence sharing and joint operations, along with risk alerts. Attendance at owners’ corporation meetings, coordinated through the Home Affairs Department, would give investigators access to the stage at which appointments and spending decisions are contested. [3]

Investigators attending those meetings could more readily detect intimidation and suspicious coordination. Their presence also demands restraint. Enforcement attendance should help owners exercise independent judgement, with clear rules governing what officers observe and how concerns are pursued. An official sitting at the back cannot make a procurement process sound.

The government also proposes bringing the new offence within organised-crime legislation, enabling applications for production orders and confiscation of proceeds. Obtaining records and removing gains can reach the economics of a syndicate more directly than a warning about future punishment. But these investigative and judicial mechanisms depend on evidence and competent case handling. Enforcement must be sustained. Announcing the powers establishes none of those outcomes. [3]

The Hong Kong Monetary Authority announced a more precise commitment on October 23, taking effect during the week covered by this essay. It increased the dedicated renminbi repo facility for twelve designated liquidity providers from RMB20 billion to RMB22 billion, effective October 27. It named the institutions and the instrument, specified the amount and gave the commencement date. Treasury desks can determine what changed. [2]

The two decisions can be compared on administrative design; banking liquidity has no causal connection to building safety. Nor does the announcement prove that every borrower will obtain cheaper funding. The HKMA did specify what it would provide and when. Maintenance reform deserves equivalent specificity: who receives the warning, who assesses it, who can order intervention and when a refusal must be explained.

Yicai’s report on Hong Kong’s IPO market offers a separate warning about aggregate success. It reported record fundraising of more than HK$388 billion through September, alongside eight first-day declines among fourteen listings since September. Those declines do not establish misconduct. The same report described Securities and Futures Commission action involving three named companies and suspected fraudulent schemes, including false share demand. [1]

Companies can raise record sums while particular transactions require intervention. Equally, disappointing investment performance does not itself justify enforcement. Officials earn confidence by identifying the conduct that warrants action. Neither a triumphant fundraising total nor an unhappy investment outcome supplies that justification. Building procurement requires the same discipline.

Owners of ageing buildings will pay for reform as well as depend on it. Independent advice costs money, as do credible inspections and properly documented procurement. Reform should make those costs visible and contestable. It should also prevent honest contractors from being disadvantaged by competitors whose apparent price depends on a fixed process or an unenforced obligation. Promising that stronger oversight will make every maintenance bill smaller would be dishonest. Owners deserve to understand what they are buying and to challenge how it was chosen.

The proposed attack on collusive consultant appointments deserves support because it addresses a specific way owners can lose control of their property. Wider safety powers require a separate, evidenced case. The government must provide effective intervention and account for why intervention failed. Residents will be able to judge the reform when the next credible warning produces a timely inspection and a defensible decision about whether work can continue. They have reason to demand that exercise of authority.
