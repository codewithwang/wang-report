UFC Buys China Cheaper Than Saudi Arabia Did

Song Yadong's Shanghai knockout cost UFC one $100,000 bonus, the same week Saudi Arabia's $6 billion, six-year LIV Golf subsidy formally ended, and the cheap bet won.
Dev Chatterjee Sports Desk, Senior Correspondent Sunday, August 30, 2026

The $100,000 Upset

Song Yadong walked out in Shanghai on Saturday night a five-to-one underdog and left it demanding a bantamweight title shot in front of his own hometown crowd. Umar Nurmagomedov, ranked No. 3 in the world at 20-1, walked into a check hook and a follow-up that DraftKings never priced for. The referee waved it off at 1:48 of round two. The crowd at Oriental Sports Center, overwhelmingly Song's own countrymen, chanted his name. 'Give me the title shot,' Song told the cage after. 'I will be the first Chinese UFC champion.' UFC handed him $100,000 in a Performance of the Night bonus on top of his purse. That is the entire capital outlay behind mixed martial arts' newest ready-made star: one fight card, one flight for a fighter already on the roster, and a bonus smaller than a mid-tier executive signing bonus. UFC has now run three events in Shanghai. This is the first one that produced a fighter the market did not need to import, market, or subsidize into relevance. He knocked himself into it.

Six Billion Dollars Later

Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund tried the other model, and the bill just came due. PIF confirmed in April it will stop bankrolling LIV Golf after the 2026 season, closing out six years and roughly $6 billion in direct subsidy since the league launched in 2022, according to Front Office Sports. LIV says an undisclosed new lead investor has signed on for a leaner 'LIV 2.0,' with the deal expected to close in September, and that September closing date is now the only fixed point left on the league's balance sheet. Six billion dollars bought LIV a roster of defectors and a league still searching for a sustainable business model. One hundred thousand dollars bought UFC a trending topic across Chinese social media and a fighter demanding, not accepting, his opportunity. Golf needed manufactured stars because a sport built around four majors and a decades-old calendar does not produce a breakout contender from Hangzhou on its own schedule. UFC needed one fight card and a countryman already in the cage. The unit economics are not close.

The Fight Still Waiting

Boxing sits at the far end of the same spectrum. Canelo Alvarez's biggest fight of the year, against Christian Mbilli, has already been pushed once, from September 12 to October 31, and it is still parked in Riyadh because Saudi purses are the only purses left large enough to make it happen. Golf tried buying stars outright and is now walking away from the bill. Boxing never stopped needing that bill paid; its marquee dates now default to Riyadh because no promoter outside the Gulf will match a Saudi-scale purse for a draw past his ticket-selling prime. UFC is the outlier of the three, and Shanghai explained why on Saturday. Dana White's promotion does not need to import a star for the China market when its own roster keeps producing them for free. Song is not the first Chinese fighter to headline in Shanghai. He is the first one to knock out a ranked contender there and ask for the belt on his way out of the cage. Golf and boxing paid for attention up front. UFC got it in the second round, at zero marginal cost above a fight card it already had to run.

Dana White has not named Song's next opponent, and a bantamweight top five stacked with bigger resumes will have something to say about a title shot earned in one homecoming knockout. Golf spent six billion dollars building stars from nothing and is walking away from the invoice, with PIF's backing set to run out at the end of the 2026 season. UFC spent a plane ticket and a bonus check, and got a hometown challenger the market never had to be told to want.
