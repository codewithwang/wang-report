Nineteenth To First, Sixty Years In Between

Kimi Antonelli won Monza from nineteenth on the grid on a seat Mercedes built for free inside its own junior program, in the same year Saudi Arabia's sovereign fund proved buying a champion outright is the pricier way to do it.
Dev Chatterjee Sports Desk, Senior Correspondent Monday, September 7, 2026

Nineteen To Win

Kimi Antonelli won the Italian Grand Prix at Monza on Sunday, starting nineteenth after a power-unit penalty and finishing first, the first Italian to win his home race since Ludovico Scarfiotti in 1966. Sixty years is long enough that most of the Tifosi who lined the pit straight at dawn were not alive for the last one, and the ones who were had stopped believing it would happen inside their lifetime. It nearly ended before it began: the race was red-flagged on lap two after Charles Leclerc crashed at Curva Alboreto, the kind of restart that usually rewards whoever survives, not whoever attacks. Antonelli attacked anyway, picking off the grid car by car through a session that Max Verstappen could only watch from third. He is twenty years old. He is also, as of Sunday, the championship leader by 66 points over his own teammate, George Russell, with Lewis Hamilton third and fading 76 points back after round thirteen. Mercedes did not just find a driver who can win a home race from the back of the field. Toto Wolff has spent two seasons telling reporters there was no rush to name a permanent Hamilton successor, and Sunday is the result cashing in on that patience. The kid who wasn't alive for fifty-nine of the sixty years just did.

Riyadh's Retreat

Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund has spent this year proving that the other way of building a champion, buying one outright, is the expensive way. On September 1, Kingdom Holding Company, 78.1 percent owned by Prince Alwaleed bin Talal, completed a SAR840 million ($224 million) purchase of a 70 percent stake in Al-Hilal from PIF, valuing the club's equity at roughly $319 to $373 million. PIF keeps 30 percent, and that remaining stake is the one still needing a buyer. Per its own governor Yasir Al-Rumayyan, PIF wants the same outcome across its Saudi Pro League holdings: 'a sale or an exit.' It is the second retreat this year. PIF confirmed on April 29 that it will fund LIV Golf only through the end of this season, after subsidies reached $5.3 billion since 2021, and LIV's own 2026 calendar already shrank to twelve events from a planned fourteen. Bloomberg has reported budget cuts of $200 to $400 million per Saudi Pro League club. This cuts against the read that Gulf capital only ever buys deeper into sport. Sometimes it sells.

Two Ways To Build One

Put the two stories on the same ledger. Antonelli has been inside Mercedes' junior program for years, funded through karting and Formula 2 the way every manufacturer academy funds a prospect it likes: seat time, engineering data, a contract, no transfer fee, no bidding war. Sunday's win cost Mercedes nothing beyond what it was already paying to develop him. PIF's approach to building sporting relevance ran the opposite math: import the finished product. Marquee wages, a $5.3 billion golf subsidy, foreign marquee names bolted onto Saudi Pro League rosters at market rate or above it. One method produces a homegrown champion who happens to be a national hero on delivery. The other produces a league that needs a buyer for its clubs and a new investor for its tour within five years of writing the checks. The Champions League's league phase opens this week with Real Madrid hosting Inter Milan among the opening fixtures, a competition still funded almost entirely by the broadcast-rights revenue UEFA distributes back to the clubs already on the pitch, which is its own version of the cheap model. Grow your own.

Antonelli didn't need a sovereign wealth fund behind him, just a Mercedes shirt from a young age and one Sunday at Monza to collect on it. PIF needed both, and is now selling the position it bought. Italy got its sixty-year wait answered by the cheaper bet. PIF's remaining 30 percent stake in Al-Hilal still needs a buyer.
