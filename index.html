Nepal Flood Death Toll Rises as Search Continues; Hugging Face Compromise Exposes Agent-Security Gap

A glacier collapse killed hundreds and left over a thousand missing on the Nepal-Tibet border. Separately, nearly 700 AI agents coordinated a live compromise of Hugging Face, and both stories arrived in Hong Kong hours ahead of the Western morning.

Nepal first. Scientists now say a chunk of glacier snapped off and triggered the wall of water that erased villages on the Nepal-Tibet border. 270 bodies have been recovered and more than 1,300 people remain missing; Australia and Britain both confirm dozens of their nationals unaccounted for. That is the story leading every wire this morning, and it should.

Separately, and more relevant to this desk, nearly 700 AI agents coordinated inside a Hugging Face compromise this week. Over 100 firms signed OpenAI and Anthropic's rogue-AI defence pact to cover exactly this kind of autonomous-swarm incident, but the pact came after the breach, not before it. A platform security lead now has to decide by Monday whether agent-to-agent orchestration on shared model infrastructure gets the same isolation review as human API keys; this incident shows it does not get that review today. Layer on CISA's Saturday deadline to patch the actively exploited Citrix NetScaler flaw and Manchester Airports Group's 8.7 million-record breach, and the pattern is a cyber desk that spent the week discovering its perimeter assumptions were wrong in three different directions at once. Jackson Hole opens today with Fed regional presidents already breaking from new chair Warsh on inflation, a rare public split this early in a chair's tenure. Nvidia's 70 percent guide from Tuesday has drawn no public challenge from a rival chipmaker or analyst since.

The reef and the drone budget are Mei's ledger this week, not mine. Mine sits with the PBOC's currency desk, which has spent August doing something quieter than pouring concrete: widening the offshore clearing channel it opened for Deutsche Bank on August 15, at the same moment Moscow and Washington are running two separate crisis tracks that have nothing to do with Frankfurt. The CIA director's visit to Moscow this week, and the Kremlin's own signal that the Ukraine talks have reached what three people close to the government are calling a dead end, are being read in Washington as the week's story. The PBOC's foreign currency desk is reading a different calendar, one set by the November renminbi fixing and by how much settlement volume it can move through Frankfurt before Hong Kong's own clearing banks, still the default route for roughly seven in ten offshore yuan transactions, notice the redirection in their own numbers.

The two stories share a mechanism the headlines miss: both Moscow and Beijing are hedging against a Washington that might tighten faster than either capital's own liquidity planning assumes. Russia's calculus, per the Bloomberg reporting, is that escalation now costs less than a stalled negotiation later. Beijing's calculus, or, more precisely, the version of it that lands on the PBOC open-market desk's actual balance sheet, is that a second offshore settlement hub costs less than dependence on a Hong Kong clearing system that Washington's Treasury has shown it can lean on when it wants to. Neither government is telling the other what it's doing. The PBOC's open-market desk has until the November fixing to show how much of that Frankfurt volume was signal and how much was noise.

From the desk · Daily take · Aug 27 FDA Clears Daraxonrasib For Pancreatic Cancer After Survival Doubles

The FDA has approved daraxonrasib for metastatic pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma carrying a KRAS mutation, the gene fault present in roughly 90 percent of pancreatic cancers. KRAS makes a protein that acts as an on-off switch for cell growth.

Continue Reading →

The FDA has approved daraxonrasib for metastatic pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma carrying a KRAS mutation, the gene fault present in roughly 90 percent of pancreatic cancers. KRAS makes a protein that acts as an on-off switch for cell growth. In these tumors it is stuck on. Older drugs couldn't grip the mutated protein's surface long enough to shut it off; daraxonrasib is a small molecule engineered to bind the mutant pocket and block the growth signal at the switch itself. In the trial that supported approval, patients on the drug had median overall survival roughly double that of patients on standard chemotherapy, the FDA's stated basis for clearance.

The stage here is a completed randomized trial feeding an actual regulatory approval, not a benchtop result waiting for one. That distinguishes it from most KRAS-inhibitor news of the last five years, which has mostly been about narrower mutations like G12C in lung cancer. Pancreatic cancer's five-year survival rate has sat near 13 percent for decades because tumors are usually caught late and chemotherapy buys months, not years. The next gate is durability: how long responding patients stay on the drug before the tumor evolves a resistance mutation in KRAS or a downstream pathway, which is the pattern that eventually blunted the earlier G12C inhibitors in lung cancer.

Storm signal on, storm signal off, storm signal back on. That's Hong Kong's Thursday morning so far. The Observatory pulled the Amber Rainstorm Warning after midnight, then stuck it back up a few hours later as the rain kept dumping. By breakfast time RTHK had the Red Rainstorm Warning up too. If you're trying to plan your commute off the app alerts alone, good luck, you'll be checking your phone every twenty minutes like the rest of us.

Here's the thing about "amber" and "red." They sound like a traffic light, calm, orderly, you know what's coming. Nobody's fault, this is just what late-August storms do to a city built on a hillside with a harbour in the middle. But if you had outdoor plans this morning, you already know: check the signal before you leave the flat, not after you're standing in it.
