Iran Names the Strait Its Own as Two Carriers Sit Offshore

Tehran and Washington are now making incompatible legal claims over Hormuz on the same day, and the water utility breach shows the exposure is no longer just tankers.

Trump says he will soon declare the Strait of Hormuz US territory. Iran and the US are making competing sovereignty claims over the same twenty-mile channel, not competing tactical claims, a category jump this column had missed in the tanker-and-blockade read of the past two weeks. Two UAE tankers were hit transiting the strait overnight, and Washington signals it can sustain the naval blockade indefinitely while widening sanctions rather than striking again, confirming Sunday's read that Washington had shifted from strikes to economic pressure. The Abraham Lincoln has now been deployed nine months with families describing conditions Trump dismissed outright, a second carrier group already inbound. On the desk that matters more this morning: Iranian state hackers have breached multiple US water utility systems, the same actor class that beat Microsoft to its own Windows patch this week. A tanker seizure is a blockade Washington can see and counter; a water utility SCADA breach means the attacker is already inside operational technology, undetected until it acts. A HK-licensed bank's third-party risk team now has to ask whether any vendor touching municipal water contracts in the US also serves clients here. Watch whether Iran's water-sector intrusion escalates to an actual disruption event by Monday, which would move Hormuz from a shipping story to a critical infrastructure one.

From the desk · Daily take · Aug 15 Anthropic's $2 Trillion Talk Meets a Chinese Price War

Anthropic is in funding talks that would value the company near $2 trillion, according to Ars Technica's August reporting, at the same moment Ars reports OpenAI and Anthropic are cutting API prices as Chinese labs close the capability gap. The two stories are one story: the valuation is a bet on locked-in enterprise deployment, the API business banks and hospitals actually wired into their compliance workflows, not on which lab ships the best model this month. That is why the price cuts matter more than the valuation headline. When a Chinese lab like DeepSeek or Moonshot's Kimi ships a model that scores close to Claude or GPT-5 on public benchmarks at a fraction of the inference cost, the fastest lever a US lab has to defend its installed base isn't a better model, it's a lower price, because switching costs (the audit, the compliance sign-off, the systems integration) protect the contract but not forever if the price gap gets too wide.

The number that will decide whether $2 trillion is a real price or a talking point is the training compute bill behind it. Anthropic runs primarily on Amazon's Trainium 2 clusters and Google TPU capacity, not the Nvidia B200 NVL72 systems that OpenAI and xAI depend on, and that dependency is now colliding with Nvidia's own reported $500 billion capacity bet on aging Hopper and Blackwell-generation supply. A valuation built on enterprise contracts still has to clear a compute bill set by whichever chip generation its cloud partners commit next, and Amazon and Google's Q3 2026 capital expenditure guidance, due in their October earnings calls, is the next number that tells you whether that bill is one Anthropic's revenue can actually cover.

From the desk · Daily take · Aug 13 China's StairMed Tests Brain Implant Delivered Through a Neck Vein

StairMed, a Chinese medical-device startup, has begun testing a brain implant that goes in through a blood vessel in the neck rather than through the skull. The standard route for a brain-computer interface, the kind Synchron and Neuralink both use in…

Continue Reading →

StairMed, a Chinese medical-device startup, has begun testing a brain implant that goes in through a blood vessel in the neck rather than through the skull. The standard route for a brain-computer interface, the kind Synchron and Neuralink both use in different forms, means either drilling through bone to place electrodes on the brain's surface or opening the skull outright. StairMed's device is a stent-like mesh of electrodes that a catheter threads up through the jugular vein into a blood vessel sitting against the motor cortex, the strip of brain tissue that fires when you plan a movement. Once the stent expands against the vessel wall, the electrodes pick up electrical activity from the neurons next door without ever touching them directly. The procedure takes about ten minutes, closer to placing a coronary stent than to neurosurgery.

The trade is signal quality for safety. Reading through a blood vessel wall means the electrodes sit further from the neurons than a probe planted directly in tissue, which typically means a noisier signal and fewer distinguishable movement commands. Synchron's own vessel-based implant, the Stentrode, has spent years in human trials refining how much a reader can extract through that same wall. StairMed's approach is still at the early human-testing stage, with no published trial data yet on how many commands its patients can reliably generate or how the signal holds up months after implantation. The next gate is that data: a peer-reviewed readout of accuracy and durability, the same bar Synchron cleared before its device moved toward a pivotal trial.

So here's a fun one this week. Government's finally moving on the taxi medallion mess, the fixed licences, about 18,000 of them, that decide who's legally allowed to pick you up in a cab. Problem is half of Hong Kong's actual point-to-point traffic runs through ride-hail apps and drivers nobody's checked a licence on in years. The medallion holders, plenty of them investors who never drive a metre, have been sitting on an asset. Everyone else just orders a car on their phone like it's 2026, because it is.

Now there's an enforcement push. Which sounds tough until you ask what "enforcement" means when the queue for a real taxi at Central on a Friday night is still twenty deep and the app car shows up in four minutes. You can fine as many unlicensed drivers as you want. The reason they're there is the licensed ones aren't. Fix the medallion price, or fix why nobody wants to be a taxi driver in this city, and the crackdown stops being theatre. Do neither, and it's just paperwork with flashing lights.
