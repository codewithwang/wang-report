Carney Declares Trade War as Beijing Buys Around Washington

Canada calls the tariff fight a war it did not start, while Beijing's same-day Stock Connect move for mainland insurers routes capital past Washington entirely without a single headline from it.

Read the synthesis →

Canada's Mark Carney told the FT overnight his country is now "at war" with the US over trade, hours after Washington's 50 percent levy on Canadian goods took effect and Ottawa promised to match it dollar for dollar starting September 8. That fight will dominate wires today, but the more durable move sits in Rachel Lam's column: Beijing granted mainland insurers same-day settlement through Stock Connect, the cross-border channel letting mainland and Hong Kong investors trade each other's listed shares, and didn't need to pass a law to pull capital away from Singapore's tax-incentive push. Washington escalates with tariffs that require enforcement. Beijing escalates with plumbing that requires nothing. Elsewhere, Microsoft patched a maximum-severity Entra ID flaw without disclosure, leaving bank security teams with no way to confirm they weren't already compromised before the fix landed, the fourth identity-platform vulnerability this year where the vendor's all-clear substitutes for evidence. Iran called Washington's new sanctions round proof the US failed militarily, not proof it's winning economically. A Russian strike killed 14 at a shopping centre in Zelensky's hometown overnight. Watch whether Ottawa's September 8 retaliation date holds or slips under fresh talks.

Washington and Beijing are locking the agenda for the Trump-Xi meeting, and the instrument that will actually determine what Xi can concede sits at Premier Li's economic working group, not at the Pentagon's Seventh Fleet. Mei Chen has already logged the strait transit and Taipei's restrained tracking of it; the question this desk asks is what the PLA Navy's shadowing does to the PBOC's calendar, because a summit built around Taiwan is also, or more precisely, a summit built around whatever tariff resolution Li's group has been holding monetary easing hostage to since March. That resolution requires a document, not a gesture, and no document has moved.

The mainland side has one lever it controls regardless of what the Seventh Fleet does in the strait: the PBOC's open-market desk sets the medium-term lending facility rate on the 15th of each month, and it has held that rate through three consecutive fixings while Li's group waited on the tariff track. A summit that produces a Taiwan Strait line without a tariff annex leaves the MLF rate exactly where it is, because the desk does not price naval transits, it prices the trade document Washington has not yet sent to Beijing's negotiators. The September 15 fixing is the next date that desk can move on, and it will only move if the annex exists by then.

The USS transited the Taiwan Strait on August 21, the Seventh Fleet said, and it did so in the same week Washington and Beijing finalized the terms of a Trump-Xi meeting. The Pentagon calls these transits routine, a designation it has used for every strait passage since 2007. Routine is the word for a thing repeated on schedule. It is not the word for a thing timed to arrive four days before its principal deploys to sit across a table from the country whose strait it just crossed.

Taiwan's defense ministry logged the PLA warships and aircraft shadowing the transit, the same tracking function it runs after every crossing, and released the count without characterizing it. That restraint is itself a data point: Taipei is not the party trying to signal anything this week. Washington set the timing, and a summit preceded by a show of naval access reads as a negotiating position, not a courtesy call. If the meeting produces a Taiwan Strait line in the joint statement, the transit was the opening bid; if it does not, the transit was the leverage Washington chose not to cash in.

From the desk · Daily take · Aug 23 Leaked AWS Keys From 2022 Are Still Live In 2026

BleepingComputer's review of public code repositories found more than 9,300 AWS access keys exposed between August 2022 and August 2026 that remain valid today, each one capable of full account takeover under whatever IAM policy it was issued with. The…

Continue Reading →

BleepingComputer's review of public code repositories found more than 9,300 AWS access keys exposed between August 2022 and August 2026 that remain valid today, each one capable of full account takeover under whatever IAM policy it was issued with. The keys turned up hardcoded in commits, CI config, and mobile app binaries, some four years old, none rotated. AWS access keys do not expire on their own. A key committed in 2022 works in 2026 unless someone deletes it, which is the entire finding.

The gap is between what AWS's shared responsibility model assumes and what a four-year-old commit history actually does. AWS scans public repositories for exposed keys and notifies account owners, a control that has existed since 2015. It caught these commits. It did not make anyone rotate the keys. For a CISO whose org has any engineering team with GitHub access, the actionable item is not a scan, it is a mandate: 90-day key rotation enforced by IAM policy (MaxSessionDuration and credential-expiry conditions), not by a ticket someone can snooze for four years.

From the desk · Daily take · Aug 23 Nvidia Buys Its Way Into an AI Coding Startup It Doesn't Control

Nvidia is putting up $12 billion for a stake in Poolside, the AI coding startup, structured as what Latent Space describes as a reverse deal: money flows in for a minority position and a compute supply agreement, not a takeover. That structure matters…

Continue Reading →

Nvidia is putting up $12 billion for a stake in Poolside, the AI coding startup, structured as what Latent Space describes as a reverse deal: money flows in for a minority position and a compute supply agreement, not a takeover. That structure matters because it tells you what Nvidia is actually buying. It isn't buying Poolside's model. It's buying a guaranteed customer for the next generation of its GPUs, the H200s and whatever ships after them, locked in before a competitor can offer Poolside a cheaper deal on someone else's silicon. Poolside gets the capital and the compute commitment to keep training; Nvidia gets a training partner whose GPU demand is now contractually Nvidia's, the same logic Microsoft ran on OpenAI's Azure contract and Anthropic runs with SpaceX's Colossus cluster. The deal size, larger than most sovereign AI funds, is the number that tells you Nvidia is now treating AI lab equity like inventory financing.

The read that matters for anyone buying GPU capacity this quarter: Nvidia is no longer just a vendor, it is becoming a lender-of-last-resort to the labs training on its chips, which means the chip supply chain and the venture capital stack are now the same balance sheet. If Poolside's training run stalls or its model underperforms, Nvidia absorbs that risk twice, once as an equity holder and once as the GPU vendor whose future orders depend on Poolside staying solvent. Watch whether Nvidia repeats this structure with the next coding or agents startup announcing a raise before Q4 2026 earnings; a second reverse deal turns this from an outlier into Nvidia's standard way of pre-selling Blackwell-generation capacity before it ships.

Continue Reading →

Unitree Robotics closed its first day on Shanghai's STAR Market up 460 percent on its offer price, putting the company's market value at $50 billion. That number matters less as a verdict on Unitree's G1 and H1 humanoids, which still ship in the low thousands of units a year, than as the first time a public market has had to set a price on humanoid robotics at all. Every prior valuation in the category, Figure, Agility, Boston Dynamics under Hyundai, was a private round negotiated between a founder and a handful of venture funds with every incentive to mark the number up.

A public float changes the mechanism that sets the price. STAR Market shares trade daily against disclosed revenue, and Unitree's prospectus put 2025 sales at a few hundred million yuan, mostly from research institutions and entertainment demos rather than factory floors. The gap between that revenue base and a $50 billion valuation is now visible to anyone with a Shanghai trading account, updated every session instead of once a funding round. The next test is Unitree's first post-IPO quarterly filing, due under STAR Market disclosure rules within three months of listing, which will show whether order volume is catching up to the price or the price is running ahead of it.

A woman was mauled to death at a pet hotel this week, and now everyone from lawmakers to dog owners in the group chats is asking the same question: why does Hong Kong have basically no rules for these places? Right now you can open a pet boarding operation with barely more oversight than a fruit stall. No mandatory staff-to-dog ratios, no licensing regime that checks how big dogs are kept, no standard for what happens when a large breed and a stranger end up in the same room.

That gap is what lawmakers are now pushing to close, and it's not the first time this has come up. Every few years a dog attack makes headlines, everyone agrees something should be done, and then it quietly drops off the agenda until the next one. The test this time is whether it turns into an actual licensing bill with teeth, not just a government working group that meets twice and produces a PDF. Watch what the relevant bureau puts on paper in the coming weeks, because that's the difference between a law and a headline.
