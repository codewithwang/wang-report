The PBOC's open-market desk enters the week pricing a variable it did not choose: Iran's vow that Hormuz stays closed lands on the same balance sheet that is managing the yuan's exposure to Gulf-linked energy settlement, and it does so as the Pentagon pulls its last Pacific carrier to reinforce Operation Epic Fury. Beijing imports roughly half its crude through the strait, mostly on contracts priced and hedged through desks in Shanghai and Singapore, and a sustained closure does not read to the PBOC as a Middle East story. The PBOC does not run a Taiwan Strait desk, but the People's Liberation Army's procurement calendar and the mainland's foreign reserve management sit closer together than the public accounts show, and a reserve manager watching Hormuz risk and a Pacific carrier gap in the same week has two separate tail risks colliding on one balance sheet. The open-market desk's next move is not a rate decision. It is how much dollar liquidity gets held back from the September MLF rollover against an energy shock that has not yet cleared.

From the desk · Daily take · Aug 17 OpenAI Cuts Prices as Chinese Rivals Win Ground

OpenAI and Anthropic dropped API prices this week as Chinese model providers took paying customers, and the pressure traces to one number: DeepSeek and Alibaba's Qwen have been quoting roughly a tenth of GPT and Claude's per-token rate since the spring,…

Continue Reading →

OpenAI and Anthropic dropped API prices this week as Chinese model providers took paying customers, and the pressure traces to one number: DeepSeek and Alibaba's Qwen have been quoting roughly a tenth of GPT and Claude's per-token rate since the spring, and enterprise buyers comparing outputs on coding and support tasks have started to notice the gap in quality is smaller than the gap in price. A procurement team switching a customer-support pipeline from GPT to a Chinese model doesn't need the Chinese model to be better. It needs it to be close enough, and every quarter it stays close enough, OpenAI and Anthropic lose the margin they were counting on to fund the next training run.

The number that makes this bite is the cluster cost, not the API price. Training GPT-5-class or Claude-class models runs on tens of thousands of Nvidia B200 GPUs, hardware that costs on the order of $2-3 billion per large training cluster once power and cooling are counted. That capital gets repaid out of API margin. Cut the price to match DeepSeek and the repayment window stretches; hold the price and lose the accounts price-sensitive buyers control. OpenAI's Denise Dresser departure this week is a boardroom footnote next to that math, but Anthropic's reported $2 trillion IPO valuation now has to survive a market where its own product line is discounting against itself. The quarter that tests this is the one where Anthropic's public filings first have to show whether API revenue per dollar of compute is rising or falling, and that filing is coming, not later than the IPO roadshow itself.

From the desk · Daily take · Aug 13 China's StairMed Tests Brain Implant Delivered Through a Neck Vein

StairMed, a Chinese medical-device startup, has begun testing a brain implant that goes in through a blood vessel in the neck rather than through the skull. The standard route for a brain-computer interface, the kind Synchron and Neuralink both use in…

Continue Reading →

StairMed, a Chinese medical-device startup, has begun testing a brain implant that goes in through a blood vessel in the neck rather than through the skull. The standard route for a brain-computer interface, the kind Synchron and Neuralink both use in different forms, means either drilling through bone to place electrodes on the brain's surface or opening the skull outright. StairMed's device is a stent-like mesh of electrodes that a catheter threads up through the jugular vein into a blood vessel sitting against the motor cortex, the strip of brain tissue that fires when you plan a movement. Once the stent expands against the vessel wall, the electrodes pick up electrical activity from the neurons next door without ever touching them directly. The procedure takes about ten minutes, closer to placing a coronary stent than to neurosurgery.

The trade is signal quality for safety. Reading through a blood vessel wall means the electrodes sit further from the neurons than a probe planted directly in tissue, which typically means a noisier signal and fewer distinguishable movement commands. Synchron's own vessel-based implant, the Stentrode, has spent years in human trials refining how much a reader can extract through that same wall. StairMed's approach is still at the early human-testing stage, with no published trial data yet on how many commands its patients can reliably generate or how the signal holds up months after implantation. The next gate is that data: a peer-reviewed readout of accuracy and durability, the same bar Synchron cleared before its device moved toward a pivotal trial.

John Niland played nine seasons at guard for the Cowboys, made six straight Pro Bowls from 1968 to 1973, and started on the team's first Super Bowl roster. He died Sunday at 82, one day before Dallas beat Seattle 17-7 in preseason with a defense that forced three turnovers and a debut from undrafted receiver Camden Brown that outplayed half the depth chart Niland's generation would not recognize. The Cowboys of 2026 carry a salary cap north of $290 million leaguewide, a number that did not exist when Niland signed his last contract, and a roster now built less by scouts than by a front office pricing every guard, corner and slot receiver against a cap sheet updated in real time.

That is the actual distance between Niland's Cowboys and Sunday's: not the win total, the mechanism. Camden Brown made a preseason roster fight look like theater because that is what it is now, a low-cost audition inside a system engineered to find $800,000 contributors before someone else does. Niland played on guaranteed money that guaranteed almost nothing and retired to open a marketing business, the standard offramp for a Pro Bowl lineman in 1975. Dallas will spend this week burying one Cowboy and roster-cutting several dozen others by a Tuesday deadline in September, and the franchise will not confuse the two.
