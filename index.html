Washington's naval blockade in the Strait of Hormuz under Operation Economic Fury lands on desks that have almost nothing to do with Iranian oil. The People's Bank of China's open-market desk has spent the past week digesting a claim, not a tariff schedule or a troop movement, but a claim of sovereign authority over a waterway that belongs to no one, and the desk's read is narrower than the diplomatic one: it is a demonstration of what Washington will spend ships to enforce, filed the same week the PBOC finalized the Frankfurt renminbi clearing mandate for Deutsche Bank. The two events share no cause. They share a reader.

Premier Li's economic working group, or more precisely the liquidity-tools desk inside it that has been signaling since March that easing is contingent on a tariff settlement Washington has not delivered, now has a second data point on how this administration uses force to establish facts rather than negotiate them. That matters for the PBOC because offshore renminbi settlement, the Frankfurt mandate included, is a bet that capital moves toward jurisdictions that behave predictably, and a blockade justified by a territorial claim with no precedent is the opposite signal. The open-market desk's November fixing is the next point where that judgment shows up in the rate, not the readout.

Washington told the Strait of Hormuz it belongs to the United States and started blocking Iranian oil there under Operation Economic Fury, a naval campaign the Trump administration launched this month to strangle Tehran's exports. The claim is not a lawyer's error. Hormuz has never been sovereign to any single state; the assertion is a signal about what Washington believes it may do in a chokepoint it does not own, backed by ships already on station and a blockade already running.

Taipei is the reader who has to price this. The Taiwan Strait is the other chokepoint where Washington's actual commitment has always lived in the gap between what a treaty says and what a president will spend ships to defend, a gap this desk has been tracking since the trigger clause obscured by design (2026-08-01). Hormuz shows the mechanism when Washington chooses to fill that gap: not a statement of law, a deployment. What Taipei's planners now have to weigh is whether Beijing reads the Hormuz claim as proof Washington will act unilaterally near a strait, or as proof it will only act where the ships are already sailing. The next test is what Washington calls the blockade in six months. If it survives, the vocabulary of ownership becomes reusable.

From the desk · Daily take · Aug 16 Anthropic and OpenAI Cut API Prices as Chinese Models Improve

The two US labs both dropped prices this week on their developer APIs, the pay-per-use plumbing that lets companies plug Claude or GPT into their own products. Anthropic and OpenAI cut per-token rates on their main model lines as competing systems out of Chinese labs closed the gap on the benchmarks that decide which model a company builds on: coding tests like SWE-bench, where a model has to fix a real GitHub issue end to end, not just answer a quiz question. When two vendors' models score close enough on those tests, the buying decision stops being about capability and starts being about price per million tokens, which is exactly the number Anthropic and OpenAI just moved. The timing lines up with Anthropic's own reported plan to go public at a $2 trillion valuation: a company selling API access at a discount is a company betting that locking in more developers now is worth more than the margin on each query today.

The number that decides who wins this is deployment count, not benchmark score: how many enterprise contracts, how many production API calls, how many paying seats a lab already has wired into customer systems before the Chinese labs' cheaper models show up in the same procurement conversation. A bank or logistics company that has already integrated Claude's API into its workflow does not re-architect that pipeline to save a fraction of a cent per token; the switching cost is engineering time, not sticker price. That is what protects Anthropic and OpenAI's current customer base even as the price gap narrows. What it does not protect is the next contract, the one still up for bid, where a Chinese lab's model at half the price becomes the default choice for any procurement lead who has not yet signed. Anthropic's IPO timeline, whenever it lands, will be read against exactly that number: new contracts signed per quarter, not the valuation headline.

From the desk · Daily take · Aug 13 China's StairMed Tests Brain Implant Delivered Through a Neck Vein

StairMed, a Chinese medical-device startup, has begun testing a brain implant that goes in through a blood vessel in the neck rather than through the skull. The standard route for a brain-computer interface, the kind Synchron and Neuralink both use in…

Continue Reading →

StairMed, a Chinese medical-device startup, has begun testing a brain implant that goes in through a blood vessel in the neck rather than through the skull. The standard route for a brain-computer interface, the kind Synchron and Neuralink both use in different forms, means either drilling through bone to place electrodes on the brain's surface or opening the skull outright. StairMed's device is a stent-like mesh of electrodes that a catheter threads up through the jugular vein into a blood vessel sitting against the motor cortex, the strip of brain tissue that fires when you plan a movement. Once the stent expands against the vessel wall, the electrodes pick up electrical activity from the neurons next door without ever touching them directly. The procedure takes about ten minutes, closer to placing a coronary stent than to neurosurgery.

The trade is signal quality for safety. Reading through a blood vessel wall means the electrodes sit further from the neurons than a probe planted directly in tissue, which typically means a noisier signal and fewer distinguishable movement commands. Synchron's own vessel-based implant, the Stentrode, has spent years in human trials refining how much a reader can extract through that same wall. StairMed's approach is still at the early human-testing stage, with no published trial data yet on how many commands its patients can reliably generate or how the signal holds up months after implantation. The next gate is that data: a peer-reviewed readout of accuracy and durability, the same bar Synchron cleared before its device moved toward a pivotal trial.

From the desk · Daily take · Aug 16 Two Border Crossings Reopen, One With A Terror Drill

Huanggang finally reopens this week after six years of rebuilding. Six years.

Continue Reading →

Huanggang finally reopens this week after six years of rebuilding. Six years. Long enough for a kid born the week it closed to be starting primary school now. The upgraded crossing between Hong Kong and Shenzhen is meant to move more people, faster, less standing around clutching your Octopus card wondering if the queue is even moving. Before it opens properly though, the government ran a counterterrorism drill there, complete with a "crowd flow" test simulating a sudden surge of passengers. So the first thing that happened at the shiny new border crossing wasn't a ribbon cutting. It was pretend terrorists and a fake stampede.

Look, I get why they drill it. Big new crossing, first months are chaos, better to practice before real crowds show up cranky and dragging suitcases. But there's something very Hong Kong about spending six years on the construction and then greeting the finished product with a simulated disaster before a single normal commuter gets to use it. We built you a nice new door. First thing through it: a drill for the worst day imaginable. When it actually opens for real people doing real crossings, that's the day worth marking on your calendar, not this one.

Novak Djokovic walked off a Cincinnati court on Saturday beaten by a man ranked 50th in the world, in ninety-degree heat that the twenty-four-time major champion later admitted he simply could not out-suffer anymore. Twenty-two years after his first tour title, the guy who used to treat five-set survival like a personality trait got outlasted by conditions, not just an opponent, and Damir Tirante will spend the rest of his career telling people about it. Djokovic is 39 in October. Cincinnati does not care about your résumé, only whether your legs still answer when it is 95 degrees and the guy across the net has nothing to lose and everything to gain.

Here is the part that matters more than one upset: Djokovic no longer needs Cincinnati to eat. He is a part owner of the Miami Open, sits on the Professional Tennis Players Association board he co-founded to fight the tour for a bigger cut of media rights, and has spent three years positioning himself as management as much as talent. A first-round exit to a qualifier-level ranking barely dents the balance sheet of a man now negotiating tennis's economics rather than just competing inside them. The US Open starts August 24. That is the one where his body, not his boardroom seat, still has to answer the bell.
