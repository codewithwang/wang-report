Tankers Turn Back in Hormuz as Trump Pauses Canada Tariffs

Three Chinese supertankers reversed course in the Strait of Hormuz overnight while Washington stood down two separate fronts, in Seoul and in Ottawa, in the same news cycle.

Read the synthesis →

Three China-linked supertankers U-turned in the Strait of Hormuz overnight, Bloomberg reports. Shippers are routing real cargo and real insurance around Trump's territorial claim over the chokepoint rather than testing it. This column has tracked the Hormuz standoff turning from military threat to verbal posturing over the past week; the reversal shows shippers treating it as neither and refusing to carry the risk regardless of label. Two other retreats landed alongside it. Seoul cut short joint war games with Washington after a request from the US side, not Korean initiative, a sequencing that changes how allies read American reliability more than the drills themselves did. And Trump paused the 50% Canada tariff for three days, a stay of execution, not a deal. A trade compliance officer at a HK bank with Canadian exposure now has a three-day window, not a resolution, to reprice that book. Layer in US public debt crossing $40 trillion and Fed officials flagging persistent inflation, and the overnight story is retreat without resolution on three fronts at once. Watch whether the tanker U-turns repeat by Friday or prove a one-day blip.

The escalation calendar Vincent's desk-mate is tracking on Taiwan has a monetary counterpart most trading desks are not yet pricing correctly: the Strait of Hormuz. Washington's 60-day ceasefire deadline with Iran expired this week with no deal, and President Trump followed the lapse with a threat to bomb Oman and a demand for Iran's surrender, terms that raise the odds of a shipping interruption through the strait that carries roughly a fifth of global oil flow. The PBOC's open-market desk, or more precisely the currency and liquidity arm that sets the daily fixing band, has spent 2026 signaling that further easing depends on an external cost shock not materializing. An oil spike out of Hormuz is exactly that shock, and it now sits alongside the PLA's cash-seizure drills against Taiwan as a second calendar the mainland's monetary planners did not set and cannot control.

The mainland imports roughly 70% of its crude, and a large share still transits the strait despite Beijing's decade-long effort to diversify supply through pipelines from Russia and Central Asia. A sustained price shock forces Premier Li's economic working group to choose between absorbing imported inflation through the currency, which the PBOC has resisted since March, or loosening liquidity into a shock the fixing band was not built to buffer. The desk that matters here is not Beijing's alone: it is now reading Muscat and Tehran with the same attention it gives its own November fixing.

Taipei's defence ministry, under a cabinet that spent 2026 fighting the opposition-controlled legislature over every drone dollar, is no longer negotiating the number. It is setting it and daring the legislature to cut it during an active PLA rehearsal cycle. That sequencing matters more than the total. A record budget proposed beside a cash-seizure drill is Taipei pricing in a scenario the opposition spent months treating as rhetorical. The next test is whether the legislature, which restored every contested dollar once already this year, holds the full NT$949.5 billion through committee review before the fiscal year closes.

The Java web shell BleepingComputer tied to Clop this week wasn't a generic loader. It was built specifically for PTC Windchill and FlexPLM servers, with functions to decrypt stored credentials and enumerate file repositories inside those platforms. That is not the profile of an opportunistic script kiddie grabbing whatever RCE happens to be trending. It is a group that read the Windchill schema first and wrote the tool second.

Windchill runs product lifecycle management for manufacturers, the CAD files, bill-of-materials data, and supplier records that make a physical product buildable. A web shell purpose-built for it means Clop had already mapped where the valuable files sit before touching a target's network, not after. The control that would have changed this outcome is internet-facing exposure management on PLM servers specifically, not general patch cadence. Any manufacturer running Windchill or FlexPLM reachable from the internet should be checking exposure today, not waiting for PTC's next advisory.

Anthropic told staff its annualized revenue has reached $65 billion, up from roughly $1 billion two years ago, according to TechCrunch's August 17 report. Annualized revenue means taking the most recent month or quarter of income and multiplying it out to a full year, so the number moves fast in either direction, but the direction here has been almost straight up since Claude's enterprise API business started compounding contract renewals into training budget. The company is now in talks that would value it near $350 billion in its next funding round, and Ars Technica reported the same day that a public listing could value Anthropic near $2 trillion. Anthropic's compute for training runs comes overwhelmingly from Amazon's Trainium chips and AWS data centers under the companies' multi-year cloud agreement, the same dependency that lets a bank or hospital sign a Claude enterprise contract and trust the vendor has the hardware lined up for the next two years of model upgrades, not just the current one.

The number that should worry Anthropic's two US rivals more than the valuation is the one buried in the same week's Ars Technica report on the price war: Chinese labs are closing the gap on price just as Anthropic's revenue proves enterprise customers will pay a premium for reliability, not raw benchmark scores. That is the bet Anthropic's $2 trillion IPO math rests on, that a compliance officer at a bank who has already wired Claude into risk-modeling workflows will not rip it out for a cheaper Chinese model even if the eval scores converge, because the contract, the audit trail, and the AWS uptime guarantee do not come with the cheaper copy. Suzhou and Hsinchu-based manufacturers building the server racks for both sides of that price war do not care who wins it. Anthropic's IPO timeline, whenever it lands, is the first real market test of whether that enterprise stickiness is worth the multiple.

Unitree listed on the Shanghai exchange this week on record order demand for its shares, the clearest signal yet that investors are pricing China's humanoid robot leader as an industrial company rather than a research project. The company's G1 and H1 platforms have spent two years racking up viral demo videos, backflips, stair climbs, martial arts routines, and that footage is doing real work now: it is the thing the prospectus points to when it explains why a company that ships mostly to research labs and a handful of industrial pilots deserves a public listing.

The mechanism worth naming is the one every humanoid maker is still stuck on: a robot that can climb a staircase once, on camera, in a controlled take, is not the same machine as one that climbs ten thousand different staircases in ten thousand different buildings without a human minder nearby. That gap, between a motion-control demo and a repeatable industrial deployment, is why Chinese humanoid makers are already being described as facing a harder second act once the IPO cash lands: the actuators, battery life and fall-recovery software that make a robot useful in a warehouse are a different engineering problem than the one that makes it useful in a highlight reel. Unitree's next real test is not another video. It is whether its order book, beyond the IPO subscription numbers, shows paying customers running its robots on shift, not just at trade shows.
