North Korean Hackers Beat Microsoft to Its Own Patch

A Windows zero-day used against US defense contractors forces CISA into an emergency directive, while Wall Street's Nvidia depreciation bet and a decoy Air Force One over Iranian threat intelligence round out a morning about who moves first when the warning is already late.

Read the synthesis →

The lead this morning is North Korean state hackers running a Windows zero-day against US defense contractors long enough that CISA had to order every federal agency to patch on an emergency clock, not a routine one. Exploitation preceded the fix rather than following it: Microsoft's own advisory confirms active use before disclosure. Pyongyang's operators are running a patched-late campaign against the exact contractor base that feeds Indo-Pacific weapons programs, which is why this sits above the Nvidia depreciation story on Wall Street's ledger despite the bigger headline number there. On the physical security side, the Air Force One decoy detail from Trump's NATO trip, flown empty as a diversion after Iran had reportedly mapped his stay down to a shoulder-fired missile threat, follows the same logic in a different domain: the defense moved before the disclosure, not after. Inflation cooling to 3.4% and the Lakers' record $12.5 billion sale are noise against that pattern. A Hong Kong-based CISO reading this before the open has one job today: confirm patch status against the specific CVE, not the vendor's general advisory language. What to watch: whether CISA's directive names a hard compliance deadline by end of day. An open-ended order means agencies self-police the exposure.

From the desk · Daily take · Aug 13 PBOC's Open-Market Desk Faces a Quieter PLA Navy

Retired Rear Adm. Mark Montgomery's warning this week, that Russia is transferring submarine-quieting technology to China, lands on a different ledger than the Pentagon's. The PLA Navy's undersea posture has always been financed off-budget, through the state shipbuilders and defense conglomerates whose bonds the PBOC's open-market desk buys and rolls as ordinary collateral, not through a line item Beijing debates in public. A quieter submarine fleet is a capital allocation decision before it is a strategic one, or, more precisely, it is a strategic decision that already cleared a financing committee months before Montgomery's warning reached Washington, which means the capability gap he is describing was underwritten, not decided, this week.

Taiwan's own response, the throttled internet drill running alongside Han Kuang's missile and special-forces scenarios, tests coordination under a blackout Taipei's planners now treat as a naval opening move rather than a cyberattack. What it does not test is the financing question sitting one level down: whether the People's Insurance Company of China and the state banks that hold PLA-linked defense paper get asked, in the next quarterly funding round, to absorb a larger and less liquid slice of that debt as Beijing accelerates procurement. The PBOC's open-market operations desk publishes its collateral eligibility list on a rolling basis; the next revision is the figure to watch, not the submarine.

From the desk · Daily take · Aug 13 Gemini's Billion Users Run on Google's Own Chips, Not Nvidia's

Google said this week that Gemini crossed one billion monthly users faster than any product in the company's history, according to Ars Technica's August 12 report. ChatGPT hit the same billion-user mark around the same time, per The Verge, so both now sit…

Continue Reading →

Google said this week that Gemini crossed one billion monthly users faster than any product in the company's history, according to Ars Technica's August 12 report. ChatGPT hit the same billion-user mark around the same time, per The Verge, so both now sit at roughly the same audience size. But the number that matters is what's underneath, not the user count. OpenAI serves ChatGPT off Nvidia GPUs rented through Microsoft Azure. Google serves Gemini largely off TPUs (Tensor Processing Units, chips Google designs in-house rather than buying from Nvidia) running in its own data centers. That means every Gemini query answered is a query Nvidia doesn't get paid for and Google doesn't have to queue behind anyone else's GPU allocation to serve.

The billion-user headline reads as a horse race between two chatbots. The real story is a supply chain question: who owns the silicon under a billion daily queries. Nvidia's H100 and B200 chips are the bottleneck every other AI lab (OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta) has to buy around, at prices and lead times Nvidia sets. Google doesn't have that constraint because it started building its own TPU fleet in 2015, long before this demand existed. That's why Google can absorb a billion-user product without asking Nvidia for anything, while OpenAI's growth to the same scale runs through Azure's GPU capacity and Microsoft's own build-out schedule. Watch Google's next TPU generation announcement, expected alongside its Cloud Next event, for the number that will tell you how much of that headroom is actually available to rent to everyone else.

From the desk · Daily take · Aug 13 China's StairMed Tests Brain Implant Delivered Through a Neck Vein

StairMed, a Chinese medical-device startup, has begun testing a brain implant that goes in through a blood vessel in the neck rather than through the skull. The standard route for a brain-computer interface, the kind Synchron and Neuralink both use in…

Continue Reading →

StairMed, a Chinese medical-device startup, has begun testing a brain implant that goes in through a blood vessel in the neck rather than through the skull. The standard route for a brain-computer interface, the kind Synchron and Neuralink both use in different forms, means either drilling through bone to place electrodes on the brain's surface or opening the skull outright. StairMed's device is a stent-like mesh of electrodes that a catheter threads up through the jugular vein into a blood vessel sitting against the motor cortex, the strip of brain tissue that fires when you plan a movement. Once the stent expands against the vessel wall, the electrodes pick up electrical activity from the neurons next door without ever touching them directly. The procedure takes about ten minutes, closer to placing a coronary stent than to neurosurgery.

The trade is signal quality for safety. Reading through a blood vessel wall means the electrodes sit further from the neurons than a probe planted directly in tissue, which typically means a noisier signal and fewer distinguishable movement commands. Synchron's own vessel-based implant, the Stentrode, has spent years in human trials refining how much a reader can extract through that same wall. StairMed's approach is still at the early human-testing stage, with no published trial data yet on how many commands its patients can reliably generate or how the signal holds up months after implantation. The next gate is that data: a peer-reviewed readout of accuracy and durability, the same bar Synchron cleared before its device moved toward a pivotal trial.

Buss got her number. Jones was never going to give anyone the chance to find his.
