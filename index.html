Taipei spent this week blockading its own bridges and throttling internet speeds in a capital-defense drill; Washington, through the State Department, asked Beijing to halt pressure on Taiwan. Read against a ledger rather than a headline, the asymmetry is the story: Taipei's drill has a budget line, a Ministry of National Defense sign-off, and a named logistics assumption about how long the capital can hold. Beijing's response, so far, has none. The PBOC's open-market desk has issued no defensive liquidity signal this month that would suggest the mainland's financial authorities are pricing a confrontation timeline anywhere near Taipei's, and Premier Li's economic working group has kept its cross-strait guidance unchanged since the spring tariff cycle.

That gap matters more than the drill itself. A capital-defense rehearsal is a fiscal commitment as much as a military one, and Taipei's finance ministry has to fund a second one if the Ministry of National Defense schedules it before year end, or, more precisely, has to fund the standing assumption a second drill would represent. Beijing has not moved a matching instrument, not a bond issuance, not a capital-control adjustment, not a PBOC liquidity facility, that would signal its own institutions are budgeting for the same afternoon. Until one of Beijing's desks puts a number against this, the mainland side of the ledger stays blank, and the next entry to watch is whether the PBOC's November fixing carries any language addressing cross-strait risk at all.

Taipei ran police blockades on the bridges into its own capital this week, part of a war drill that also throttled internet speeds and rehearsed a tunnel blockade meant to stop People's Liberation Army forces from moving through the city. Taiwan's coast guard, the smaller service historically built for fisheries patrol and smuggling interdiction, is being armed for direct confrontation with Chinese vessels. Neither move treats invasion as a distant contingency. Both are logistics for a specific afternoon: the one when Beijing's coast guard and PLA assets are already inside Taiwan's contiguous waters and the government has hours, not weeks, to move personnel and slow an advance through Taipei's own streets. Washington's response, delivered through the State Department, was a request that Beijing halt pressure on Taiwan. The request and the drill are not answering the same question.

Taipei's planners are building for the days after a blockade begins, which means they have already concluded that deterrence, on its own, will not hold that day. A bridge blockade drill only pays off if the bridges are contested, which means the exercise assumes PLA forces reach the capital's perimeter. Washington's statement, filed the same week, asks Beijing to stop something without naming the something, which leaves Taipei to plan for the case where the ask goes unheeded. The next signal to watch is whether Taipei's Ministry of National Defense schedules a second capital-level drill before year end. One is a rehearsal. Two, within months, is a standing assumption.

From the desk · Daily take · Aug 14 Nvidia Sells Aging GPUs Through a $500 Billion Financing Bet

Nvidia's latest plan puts $500 billion behind a bet that customers who can't get the newest chips will still pay for the previous generation, according to reporting out August 13. The mechanism matters more than the headline number: Nvidia is using…

Continue Reading →

Nvidia's latest plan puts $500 billion behind a bet that customers who can't get the newest chips will still pay for the previous generation, according to reporting out August 13. The mechanism matters more than the headline number: Nvidia is using financing and cloud-capacity deals to keep H100 and H200 clusters, the generation before its current Blackwall-era B200 NVL72 hardware, in paying service rather than write them off. That distinction, financing an asset's second life instead of just selling a new one, is the whole story. A GPU that ages out of frontier training runs at OpenAI or Anthropic doesn't stop working. It gets resold, leased, or bundled into a cheaper compute tier for smaller labs and enterprise customers who never needed the newest silicon in the first place.

Every APAC sovereign AI program stood up in the past year, Singapore's national compute cluster among them, has been shopping exactly this tier: capacity a generation behind the frontier, priced to move. Nvidia's $500 billion commitment effectively subsidizes that secondary market into existence at scale, which is also why the Bureau of Industry and Security's October 2023 export controls matter here in a way they don't for new-chip sales. Controlled hardware doesn't stop being controlled because it's a year old. A financing structure built to keep H100 clusters earning for another five years is also a financing structure that has to keep tracking, for that same five years, exactly which buyers the controls still allow. Nvidia's next 10-Q will show whether that tracking cost shows up as a line item or an asterisk.

From the desk · Daily take · Aug 13 China's StairMed Tests Brain Implant Delivered Through a Neck Vein

StairMed, a Chinese medical-device startup, has begun testing a brain implant that goes in through a blood vessel in the neck rather than through the skull. The standard route for a brain-computer interface, the kind Synchron and Neuralink both use in…

Continue Reading →

StairMed, a Chinese medical-device startup, has begun testing a brain implant that goes in through a blood vessel in the neck rather than through the skull. The standard route for a brain-computer interface, the kind Synchron and Neuralink both use in different forms, means either drilling through bone to place electrodes on the brain's surface or opening the skull outright. StairMed's device is a stent-like mesh of electrodes that a catheter threads up through the jugular vein into a blood vessel sitting against the motor cortex, the strip of brain tissue that fires when you plan a movement. Once the stent expands against the vessel wall, the electrodes pick up electrical activity from the neurons next door without ever touching them directly. The procedure takes about ten minutes, closer to placing a coronary stent than to neurosurgery.

The trade is signal quality for safety. Reading through a blood vessel wall means the electrodes sit further from the neurons than a probe planted directly in tissue, which typically means a noisier signal and fewer distinguishable movement commands. Synchron's own vessel-based implant, the Stentrode, has spent years in human trials refining how much a reader can extract through that same wall. StairMed's approach is still at the early human-testing stage, with no published trial data yet on how many commands its patients can reliably generate or how the signal holds up months after implantation. The next gate is that data: a peer-reviewed readout of accuracy and durability, the same bar Synchron cleared before its device moved toward a pivotal trial.

Customs and the ICAC went into a bunch of renovation firms this week and pulled apart a scheme worth about half a billion Hong Kong dollars. The setup was simple and it is one every flat owner in this city has felt in their bones: get three quotes, watch two of them somehow land near identical, wonder if you just got fleeced. Turns out sometimes you did, and there was a syndicate behind it.

Here is the bit nobody tells you when you sign a renovation contract. You are not just hiring a contractor, you are betting that the quote you got was an honest one and not a number three companies agreed on over coffee beforehand. Renovation in Hong Kong runs almost entirely on trust and word of mouth, uncle knows a guy, cousin used this team last year, and that is exactly the gap a cartel walks through. No permit stops a rigged quote. No licence catches a wink between "competitors." The raids this week prove the trust was misplaced for a lot of owners, and the only real fix now is watching whether any of the operators charged actually lose their business licence, not just a fine that gets folded into next year's markup.

The Lakers just changed hands for $12.5 billion, sold by the Buss family to a group led by Mark Walter alongside Jared Kushner and Bob Iger, and the number alone rewrites what an NBA franchise is worth. Jeanie Buss's family bought full control of the team from Jack Kent Cooke for $67.5 million in 1979. Forty-seven years later the exit price is roughly 185 times that, for a team that has not won a title since 2020 and just watched Russell Westbrook, one of the great engines of the league's last two decades, announce his retirement the same week. The trophy case did not set this price. The regional television deal, the Los Angeles market and the league's new national broadcast contracts did.

Kushner's arrival is the part worth sitting with. He built his sports-ownership profile on the LIV Golf-PGA Tour merger talks and Saudi PIF money reshaping golf's economics from outside the room, and now he is inside the room for one of the four or five most valuable franchise brands in American sport. Iger brings Disney-ABC-ESPN broadcast relationships that make the Lakers' next local rights renegotiation a very different conversation than the one the Buss family would have had alone. Westbrook walks away a $200 million-plus career earner and a Hall of Famer by any reasonable vote. The team he help define just became a line item in a very different kind of portfolio.
