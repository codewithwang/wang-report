Iran Ends the Lull, Hits Two Gulf Neighbors in One Night

Iran retaliated for last week's US strikes by firing on Gulf states hosting American forces, ending a monthlong pause and forcing Rubio into a sanctions threat that will hit Asian buyers of Iranian crude first.

Read the synthesis →

The lull broke overnight. For roughly a month after Washington's strikes on the tunnel and base targets this column has tracked since Larak, Tehran held fire on third parties even as it kept hitting US assets directly. That restraint ended: Iran fired on its Gulf neighbors, the states hosting the American bases Iran has been targeting since the war reopened, retaliation NPR and Reuters both frame as the monthlong pause giving way to open regional war fears rather than a contained US-Iran exchange. Trump, asked about further action, said Washington was prepared to strike Iran "anytime we want," a line that reads less like deterrence and more like an admission that the August ceasefire this column called thin has now failed. By morning the diplomatic track had scrambled to catch up: Secretary of State Marco Rubio, meeting the moment India's Modi held talks with Iran's president, warned publicly that no nation should help Iran, a signal of sanctions aimed squarely at the countries still buying Iranian oil. That is where this lands for the reader in Hong Kong. Gulf-neighbor strikes mean tanker risk in waters Asian refiners depend on, and a US sanctions threat aimed at Iran's buyers puts trade-finance desks in Singapore and Hong Kong on notice that letters of credit tied to Iranian-linked cargo need re-screening before counterparties get flagged, not after. The falsifiable call: this is not a two-actor war anymore. Once Iran starts hitting the neighbors instead of only the Americans, the decision to escalate or de-escalate passes out of Washington and Tehran's hands and into whichever Gulf state decides its own retaliation is next. Watch whether Riyadh or Abu Dhabi responds by the weekend; that answer decides whether this stays a US-Iran story or becomes a regional one.

From the desk · Daily take · Sep 3 NBA Strips Clippers Pick, Bans Ballmer $10M Over Kawhi Deal

A $28 million no-show sponsorship contract for Kawhi Leonard cost the Clippers a first-round pick and cost Steve Ballmer the largest personal fine in league history.

Continue Reading →

The NBA spent the offseason investigating why Aspiration, a now-bankrupt "green banking" startup, agreed to pay Kawhi Leonard $28 million to do essentially nothing, and on Tuesday commissioner Adam Silver answered his own question with a gavel. The league stripped the Clippers of a future first-round pick and fined owner Steve Ballmer $10 million personally, the largest fine ever levied against an individual owner, for what investigators concluded was a side deal engineered to route money to Leonard outside his actual contract. Mark Cuban, who spent a decade getting fined by this same office for far smaller sins, posted his approval before the ink dried. Ballmer, worth $170 billion, is not going to miss the check.

Here is the number that actually stings: that $10 million fine is 0.006 percent of Ballmer's net worth, roughly what a Microsoft alum spends on a nice boat slip. The real punishment is the pick, because in a hard-capped league the draft is the only place a $170 billion owner cannot simply outspend the problem. The Clippers built a supposed title window around a $28 million accounting trick, and the league just repossessed a piece of their future to make the point. Kawhi Leonard, for the record, remains on the roster, remains healthy for about four games a season, and remains the single most expensive mystery in professional sports.

From the desk · Daily take · Sep 3 Xi's Officer Purge Hits PLA Payroll Before 2027

The 46 officers stripped of Communist Party legislature seats keep their pay grade, and the finance department that decides otherwise sits inside the Central Military Commission Xi just cut to two members.

Continue Reading →

The 46 officers Xi Jinping removed from National People's Congress and CPPCC seats this week lost their political standing, not their commissions. That distinction runs through the PLA's General Political Work Department, or more precisely through the officer-management bureau inside it that decides whether a stripped legislature seat converts into a stripped rank, a reassignment, or nothing at all. Mei Chen's brief today reads the purge against Beijing's blockade planning for Taiwan; the ledger that matters this week sits one floor down, in the CMC's own finance and audit arm, which has to decide within its normal autumn budget cycle whether those 46 officers keep drawing PLA pay while their political status is unresolved.

The precedent is the 2023 rocket force purge, when Beijing took roughly eight months to move from legislature removal to formal military tribunal for General Li Yuchao, a gap during which his unit's procurement budget kept flowing under his successor's signature, not his own. Li Shangfu's ouster the same year ran a similar clock: seat stripped in a July plenum session, court-martial confirmed the following June. If the CMC's audit arm, now reporting to a body Xi has cut to two standing members from the customary seven, runs the same eight-to-eleven-month interval on this cohort, the formal rank action lands sometime between April and July 2027, inside the same year Xi has set as his combat-readiness deadline and Taipei's NT$1 trillion defense budget takes effect.

From the desk · Daily take · Sep 3 China Signals Full Blockade Planning For Taiwan

Beijing's blockade posture and Taipei's record defense budget are converging on the same calendar, and neither side is pretending otherwise.

Continue Reading →

The People's Liberation Army is preparing contingency plans for a full blockade of Taiwan, according to reporting this week that moves beyond the "quarantine" exercises Beijing ran in 2025. A blockade is not a quarantine with a bigger perimeter. A quarantine inspects; a blockade denies passage entirely, and the legal distinction matters because Beijing has spent three years building the operational muscle for the latter while briefing the former to foreign correspondents. The same week, the PLA ran live-missile patrols over a disputed shoal, a rehearsal detail that reads less like signaling and more like inventory management. President Lai Ching-te's cabinet responded on its own calendar: a proposed NT$1 trillion defense budget for 2027, the first time Taipei's annual defense line has crossed that threshold, up from the record NT$1.12 trillion already passed for 2026.

The number that should worry a strategy desk is not the budget. It is the sequencing. Xi Jinping has removed 46 officers from PLA leadership positions this year, a purge wide enough to strip his Central Military Commission down to two members ahead of his own 2027 deadline for combat readiness, the same year Taipei's new budget targets. A commander does not usually accelerate blockade planning while gutting the officer corps meant to execute it, unless the purge and the planning are running on separate tracks: one for loyalty, one for capability, both converging on 2027. If Beijing's blockade contingency moves from planning document to exercise rotation before Taipei's NT$1 trillion budget clears the legislature, the next Taiwan Strait crisis will be dated to a defense appropriations calendar, not a diplomatic one.

Aya Nakamura 中 村 彩 AI Desk, Senior Correspondent
From the desk · Daily take · Sep 3 OpenAI's Astra Can Hack, Not Yet Defend At Scale

Astra's cyber skills arrive without the enterprise deployment contracts that would put those same skills to work patching networks first.

Continue Reading →

OpenAI's new model, Astra, is what the company calls its most capable coding and reasoning system yet, and it ships with something new attached: the ability to find and exploit software vulnerabilities on its own, well enough that OpenAI briefed the Pentagon and CISA before release. That is a plain description of what happened this week, confirmed in OpenAI's own release notes and reported by Wired and TechCrunch on September 1. The capability is not a benchmark score in a paper. It is a model that can chain together the steps a human penetration tester takes, scanning a system, finding the weak point, writing the exploit, without a human at each step.

The gap is what Astra does not yet have: a deployment base of security teams actually running it as a defensive tool at the scale it could be run offensively. Anthropic's Claude and Google's Gemini already sit inside thousands of enterprise security operations centers, wired into SIEM platforms and patch-management pipelines, the products of contracts signed and compliance-cleared over the past two years. Astra has the exploit-generation skill first and the defensive install base second, and that ordering is the whole risk. A vulnerability scanner that only attackers have integrated is not a balanced tool, it is a head start, and OpenAI's own safety researchers said as much to The Verge on the same day the model shipped: the monitoring systems meant to catch misuse of Astra were not the ones stress-tested against a model this good at breaking in. The clock that matters now is not a training run. It is how fast CISOs at named enterprises, not hypothetical ones, get equivalent tooling under contract before the exploit side of that ledger gets used against them.

Magnus Honeyfield Science Desk, Senior Correspondent
From the desk · Daily take · Sep 2 Two Superconductors Were Hiding Inside One Crystal

A material long filed as a single superconductor turns out to run two separate superconducting states at once, and separating them is what tells you which one to engineer.

Continue Reading →

Superconductivity is usually described as a single switch: cool the material past a critical temperature and electrical resistance drops to zero. A study covered by ScienceDaily this week describes a material that flips that switch twice, because it turns out to contain two distinct superconducting states layered on top of each other, not one. Researchers had been measuring the combined signal for years and reading it as a single, slightly odd superconductor. The new work separates the two states by tracking how each responds differently to temperature and magnetic field, the way a chord only reveals its two constituent notes once you filter one out. That separation matters because the two states almost certainly come from two different physical mechanisms for pairing electrons, the actual engine of superconductivity, and a material running both at once has been quietly miscategorized as a single, harder-to-explain phase.

This is benchtop physics: the sample was characterized in the lab, not built into a device, and the paper's contribution is identifying the two-state structure rather than exploiting it. The next gate is whether the two states can be tuned independently, by pressure, doping, or magnetic field, since a material where you can dial one superconducting channel up and the other down would be a genuinely new knob for critical-temperature engineering, the decades-long project of finding materials that superconduct at higher and higher temperatures. Until a group publishes that independent control, this is a reclassification of what the material is doing, not yet a route to a warmer superconductor.

From the desk · Daily take · Sep 3 Schools Cut 126 Primary Classes As Kids Run Out

Hong Kong axed a record number of Primary One classes this term, and the reason is the plainest math in the city: not enough babies were born six years ago.

Continue Reading →

Schools cut 126 Primary One classes this year. That is a record. Not "record" like a typhoon record where you shrug and check the signal. Record like the line just fell off the chart. Every one of those classes was a room with desks and a whiteboard and a teacher, and now it is just a room.

Here is the part nobody wants to say plainly: this was booked in already, six years ago, when the birth numbers came in low and everyone in education knew exactly how many Primary One kids Hong Kong would have this September. It is not a surprise. It is a bill arriving on the date printed on it. The surprise, if you can call it that, is watching schools scramble for enrolment, merge classes, and quietly let contracts lapse for a shortfall the government had six years to plan for.
