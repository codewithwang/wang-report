Carney Declares Trade War as Beijing Buys Around Washington

Canada calls the tariff fight a war it did not start, while Beijing's same-day Stock Connect move for mainland insurers routes capital past Washington entirely without a single headline from it.

Canada's Mark Carney told the FT overnight his country is now "at war" with the US over trade, hours after Washington's 50 percent levy on Canadian goods took effect and Ottawa promised to match it dollar for dollar starting September 8. That fight will dominate wires today, but the more durable move sits in Rachel Lam's column: Beijing granted mainland insurers same-day settlement through Stock Connect, the cross-border channel letting mainland and Hong Kong investors trade each other's listed shares, and didn't need to pass a law to pull capital away from Singapore's tax-incentive push. Iran called Washington's new sanctions round proof the US failed militarily, not proof it's winning economically. A Russian strike killed 14 at a shopping centre in Zelensky's hometown overnight. Satellite imagery this week showed construction on a new artificial island feature attributed to China, timed alongside a Chinese research vessel that Taiwan's coast guard warned off its waters. Neither event carries a defense ministry communique, and neither needs one: the Institute for the Study of War's tracking places both inside a coercion architecture that has migrated, deliberately, into instruments a naval liaison office logs but a foreign ministry spokesperson never has to explain.

Read against Taipei's record NT$949.5 billion budget request, or, more precisely, against what that budget actually appropriates, the mismatch is procedural rather than rhetorical. A defense line item funds fighters, submarines, and coastal missiles; it does not fund the agency that would answer a dredging barge or a research vessel operating inside contested waters, because that agency is the coast guard and the coast guard's budget was not the one making headlines this week. The PBOC's own signaling calendar runs separately from this: Beijing's financial desks have given no indication that the Taiwan Strait posture shift is priced into liquidity policy, which means the coercion campaign is currently running on a books entirely its own, cost-free to the mainland's monetary side and correspondingly free of the one constraint that has historically made Beijing negotiate on a fixed timetable.

What it does not do is specify which agency in Taipei owns the response to the half that looks like a survey ship and a dredging barge instead of a fighter jet.

From the desk · Daily take · Aug 22 Rust Supply Chain Attack Hit Crates With 245 Million Downloads

A maintainer account on crates. io was compromised and used to publish malicious versions of three widely used Rust crates, adding a typosquatted dependency to the build chain before the Rust Project pulled the releases.

Continue Reading →

A maintainer account on crates.io was compromised and used to publish malicious versions of three widely used Rust crates, adding a typosquatted dependency to the build chain before the Rust Project pulled the releases. The combined download count on the affected crates runs to 245 million, which is not a measure of how many builds pulled the poisoned version, only a measure of how many builds trusted the account that got taken over. Package registries verify the publisher's credentials. Nothing downstream verifies the code.

The pattern matches the Rust Foundation's existing guidance on npm-style typosquatting, published after prior registry incidents on other language ecosystems, and crates.io still relies on maintainer 2FA as opt-in rather than mandatory for high-download packages. Any CI pipeline pinning these crates by version tag rather than checksum pulled the malicious build automatically on the next `cargo update`. The control that would have changed the outcome is mandatory hardware-key 2FA on publish rights for crates above a download threshold, a policy crates.io has not adopted as of August 22, 2026.

From the desk · Daily take · Aug 22 Nvidia Turns GPUs Into Loan Collateral

Nvidia is restructuring how it finances the buildout of AI data centers: instead of customers buying B200 chips outright, Nvidia and partners including Goldman Sachs and BlackRock are structuring deals where the GPUs themselves back the debt, the same way…

Continue Reading →

Nvidia is restructuring how it finances the buildout of AI data centers: instead of customers buying B200 chips outright, Nvidia and partners including Goldman Sachs and BlackRock are structuring deals where the GPUs themselves back the debt, the same way an aircraft backs a leveraged lease. The chips sit in a leasing vehicle, cloud operators pay to rent capacity, and the lenders get repaid from that rental stream. It's a bet that a five-year-old B200 will still be worth renting in 2031, which matters because the last generation of AI chips didn't hold value that long. Nvidia's own H100, launched 2022, is already being discounted by resellers as newer Blackwell-generation hardware displaces it in top-tier clusters.

The reason this matters beyond Nvidia's balance sheet: asset-backed lending only works if the collateral has a resale market, and GPU clusters don't have one the way office buildings or airplanes do. A defaulted data center in Suzhou or Hsinchu can't easily sell its Blackwell racks to a buyer who wants last-generation compute, because the entire industry is racing to the next chip generation on a roughly two-year cycle. If lease payments from cloud tenants slow (Poolside's $12 billion reverse-acquihire into Nvidia last week is itself a sign that even well-funded AI labs are running short on independent cash), the test of this structure comes at the first missed payment, not before.

Continue Reading →

Unitree Robotics closed its first day on Shanghai's STAR Market up 460 percent on its offer price, putting the company's market value at $50 billion. That number matters less as a verdict on Unitree's G1 and H1 humanoids, which still ship in the low thousands of units a year, than as the first time a public market has had to set a price on humanoid robotics at all. Every prior valuation in the category, Figure, Agility, Boston Dynamics under Hyundai, was a private round negotiated between a founder and a handful of venture funds with every incentive to mark the number up.

A public float changes the mechanism that sets the price. STAR Market shares trade daily against disclosed revenue, and Unitree's prospectus put 2025 sales at a few hundred million yuan, mostly from research institutions and entertainment demos rather than factory floors. The gap between that revenue base and a $50 billion valuation is now visible to anyone with a Shanghai trading account, updated every session instead of once a funding round. The next test is Unitree's first post-IPO quarterly filing, due under STAR Market disclosure rules within three months of listing, which will show whether order volume is catching up to the price or the price is running ahead of it.

A woman was mauled to death at a pet hotel this week, and now everyone from lawmakers to dog owners in the group chats is asking the same question: why does Hong Kong have basically no rules for these places? Right now you can open a pet boarding operation with barely more oversight than a fruit stall. No mandatory staff-to-dog ratios, no licensing regime that checks how big dogs are kept, no standard for what happens when a large breed and a stranger end up in the same room.

That gap is what lawmakers are now pushing to close, and it's not the first time this has come up. Every few years a dog attack makes headlines, everyone agrees something should be done, and then it quietly drops off the agenda until the next one. The test this time is whether it turns into an actual licensing bill with teeth, not just a government working group that meets twice and produces a PDF. Watch what the relevant bureau puts on paper in the coming weeks, because that's the difference between a law and a headline.
