Hong Kong Should Judge the Exit as Well as the Entry

Crypto.com plans to end its Hong Kong consumer app services on February 1, 2027, while a separate exchange application remains pending.
Walter Wang Editor-in-Chief
AI-assisted editorial · Human direction and editorial oversight

Crypto.com’s general account closure guidance directs customers whose balances fall below the minimum withdrawal amount to customer support. It also warns that closure permanently ends access to transaction history and advises downloading it for tax purposes. [3]

These are small details with a substantial consequence. A provider’s decision to leave a market can leave work that customers cannot complete alone. Hong Kong should judge a financial business partly by how it finishes that work, even while welcoming a different business from the same group.

On November 3, a Crypto.com spokesperson told Cointelegraph that its consumer app services would end in Hong Kong on February 1, 2027. The spokesperson said affected customers had received advance notice and would receive closure instructions and support to withdraw their funds. The company described the decision as part of allocating resources towards sustainable opportunities. [4]

Cointelegraph reports that Foris DAX MT Limited provides the consumer app services. A separate entity, Foris DAX HK Limited, is pursuing a licence for the Crypto.com Exchange serving institutional clients. [4] [1]

The Securities and Futures Commission’s register, last updated October 16, lists Foris DAX HK Limited as an applicant deemed to be licensed under transitional arrangements. The regulator explicitly warns that deemed-to-be-licensed applicants are not formally licensed. [2] [4]

The distinction matters. The reported consumer app departure does not establish the end of every financial relationship with the group. Nor would an eventual exchange licence settle the arrangements for leaving the consumer service. An entry decision and a departure deserve their own judgements, attached to the right entity and the right customers. [4]

A financial centre has good reasons to be selective. A firm may decide that a market no longer suits a particular service. A regulator should be able to insist on standards without treating every departure as a defeat. Requiring firms to stay, or relaxing protections simply to retain familiar brands, would make the number of participating companies a poor substitute for the quality of their conduct.

There is no need to reject that argument to demand an orderly exit. Freedom to leave and responsibility for finishing the work can belong in the same commercial calculation. The cost of settling accounts belongs in the decision, even when it makes the projected savings less attractive.

The company’s general help page is not its Hong Kong shutdown plan. Its global account requirements and warning about access to records cannot be assumed to describe the arrangements for affected Hong Kong customers. The page does, however, document two ordinary closure issues: residual balances requiring assistance and records a customer should preserve. It establishes no deficiency in this departure. [3]

Those examples give a departure standard something concrete to do. A modest balance may be uneconomic for its owner to pursue through repeated correspondence. That does not make it ownerless. Where an exception requires assistance, somebody must retain the authority to resolve it. A support address is useful only if the people behind it can finish the cases they receive.

The same principle applies to records. Moving the money and accounting for it are different tasks. Customers should have a workable opportunity to obtain a usable history, with a defined route for later disputes or incomplete entries. That need not mean keeping a consumer app fully operational indefinitely. It means designing the retirement of a service around the obligations that may survive it.

The strongest objection is that the proposed standard may already be met. Crypto.com’s general guidance explicitly directs small balances to support and warns customers to preserve their histories. Its spokesperson has promised assistance for the Hong Kong departure. These are meaningful provisions, not evidence of neglect. [3] [4]

The company should be judged on the arrangements it delivers. The appropriate question is whether customers can complete the process, including the exceptions, rather than whether an observer can find friction in a generic help page. Nothing in the available evidence justifies treating the departure itself as misconduct.

There must also be limits to what a provider is expected to carry. Customers need to respond, supply necessary information and decide where their assets should go. Reasonable deadlines and proportionate procedures are defensible. Requiring perpetual operation until the last unresponsive account holder acts would give individuals a veto over a commercial exit.

A board approving a departure can hold both sides of that argument. It should ask what will remain after routine withdrawals are complete, who owns those cases and what resources remain to settle them. A funded process with a documented conclusion is a more credible answer than assuming every account will fit the simplest procedure.

For Hong Kong, demanding that answer would strengthen the commercial case for entry as well as departure. Businesses would remain free to try, succeed or leave. Customers could assess a service with greater confidence that a change in strategy would come with a practicable means of settling its unfinished affairs.

The separate exchange application will have its own merits and its own regulatory decision. The consumer app’s departure should be measured closer to the customer: whether someone with a small unresolved balance can reach a person empowered to settle it, and leave with a usable record. That customer may never earn the provider another dollar. Finishing the work should already be in the budget. [4]
