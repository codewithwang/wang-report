The Lead

Asian Data Centres Need Washington’s Permission

Washington’s announced chip licensing requirements give Asian investors reason to plan for restrictions and delays despite diplomatic engagement.
Walter Wang Editor-in-Chief
AI-assisted editorial · Human direction and editorial oversight

“I haven’t even said that to my people,” Donald Trump told reporters on Friday, as he threatened to stop American companies building artificial intelligence data centres overseas. According to the BBC, the president had returned from Asia and was proposing a “tremendous tax” on firms that built abroad.

Trump’s remark suggested his cabinet was hearing the proposal in public. He supplied little detail about enforcement. His threat does not establish an operating rule or mean an American company’s overseas project must stop. An investor considering a facility intended to operate for years still has reason to take it seriously. A government can make an investment harder to justify before it makes it unlawful.

Asian businesses should welcome engagement between rival powers while keeping restrictions and delays in their investment assumptions. Political contact can improve the prospects for cooperation. Capital committed to a site still depends on permission to buy equipment and serve customers before it can earn revenue. This week brought a concrete reason to ask who controls those permissions.

On Monday, Washington announced that firms would require licences to buy advanced chips, allowing US officials to monitor whether the processors were serving Chinese companies or being smuggled into China. The BBC reports that the move will affect Southeast Asian countries that have invested heavily in data centres as Chinese companies seek computing power overseas.

A processor can remain in one country while serving a customer in another. Moving a chip and selling access to its computing capacity are different transactions. Scrutiny of that customer relationship gives Washington influence beyond the shipment itself. For an Asian host government, providing land, electricity and construction approval may therefore be insufficient to make a project commercially viable. US licensing decisions can still affect which customers the operator expects to serve.

The host government can approve the building. Washington’s announced licensing approach gives US officials a means of scrutinising the advanced equipment and its use. An investor needs both decisions to support the same business plan. Diplomatic access to senior officials may help resolve a disagreement, but a meeting cannot substitute for knowing what the licence permits.

US officials have a serious case for checking who uses the chips. If an export restriction aims to limit specified users’ access to advanced computing, overseas rental can defeat it without a chip crossing the prohibited border. Checking who receives the service follows from the policy. Governments seeking data centre investment can expect questions about customers and access. A credible licensing system could also give compliant operators greater certainty about the business they can accept.

Trump’s broader construction threat raises a different problem. The overseas projects described by the BBC include Meta’s data centre already under construction in Alberta and Anthropic’s agreement to lease a proposed facility in Nova Scotia for the long term. Meta has construction under way, while Anthropic has committed to a future tenancy. The report supplies no basis for treating either as an attempt to evade controls on China. A general restriction on overseas construction needs a broader justification than preventing prohibited access to chips.

Trump is entitled to favour investment at home. He also owes companies and partner governments an account of what his threat would cover. They cannot readily tell whether he is staking out a negotiating position or proposing a future prohibition. Nor can they determine which existing commitments might be covered or calculate the cost of compliance. The uncertainty reaches parties that did not choose the policy and may have little influence over its final form.

Diplomats can achieve results before the paperwork arrives. Direct cooperation between powerful rivals can reduce miscalculation and make officials more willing to solve disputes. It can also create room for agreements that initially seem impossible. Investors necessarily assess those possibilities. Requiring every political improvement to produce an immediate contract would miss much of diplomacy’s value.

A data centre owner still needs to know whether closer political ties will remove the obstacle facing the project. Equipment approval and permission to serve the intended customers determine whether it can operate. Continuity of service matters to its prospects of earning a return. Diplomatic talks could secure that clarity from US officials. Monday’s licensing announcement and Friday’s construction threat give businesses reasons to keep asking for it.

The New York Times reported that the S&P 500 fell 3.7 per cent on Friday and the Nasdaq Composite lost 5.3 per cent. The falls came amid concerns about AI investment and infrastructure spending funded by debt, alongside other pressures. The account does not establish that Trump’s remarks caused those losses. Investors were already questioning whether future profits would justify the spending. Uncertain permission to serve customers adds another assumption to business models whose returns are under examination.

For Hong Kong investors financing regional projects, and for Asian governments competing to host them, the case for committing more money depends on what can actually be authorised. Equipment approval determines whether a facility can open. Its economics depend on permission to serve the intended customers. The operator also needs cash to cover a delayed opening. A defined scope and a workable licensing process would give officials negotiating with Washington something commercially useful to bring home, beyond another expression of goodwill.

Diplomacy deserves credit when it reduces danger, including before a binding agreement emerges. Businesses have reason to release their contingency funds more slowly. An Asian facility able to obtain its processors and serve its intended customers would provide a sound reason to revise an investment plan. Until US officials make those permissions clear, keeping money available for delay reflects their continuing power over the project.
