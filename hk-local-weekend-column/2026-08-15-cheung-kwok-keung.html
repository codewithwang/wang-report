Housing Bureau Calls A Worse Wait Good News

The public housing wait crept to 4.8 years, and the Housing Bureau called it a 'positive sign,' the same week the plan meant to fix it closed for submissions.
張 國 強 · Cheung Kwok-keung HK Desk, Senior Correspondent Sunday, August 16, 2026

The Positive Sign Problem

Hong Kong's public rental housing wait hit 4.8 years at the end of June, up from 4.7 in the first quarter, and the Housing Bureau called the move a 'positive sign.' The government's own target for 2026-27 is 4.5 years. The actual number came in 0.3 years worse, and the official explanation is that this is good news, because long-queued applicants in the urban core are finally being housed, which drags the average up before it settles back down. Maybe. About 100,500 households are still on the general waiting list. Wait in the New Territories for Light Public Housing and you're looking at roughly two years. Wait for something in the urban area and it's closer to six. Same waiting list, two completely different lives, depending on which side of the harbour you land on. A landlord who tells you the ceiling leak got worse because now he knows exactly which bucket to bring isn't wrong, technically. He's still not fixing the ceiling. The Housing Bureau's 'positive sign' is the same move: correct on the mechanics, quiet on the part where 100,500 families are watching a number that keeps moving the wrong way.

Where The Money Sits

The real decision on the wait sits in a folder in Central right now. Hong Kong's first Five-Year Plan consultation closed on August 14 with more than 16,000 submissions, after about 100 sessions, some 30 of them run personally by the Chief Executive. AmCham and the Federation of Hong Kong Industries both used their submissions to push for a single statutory body to fast-track the Northern Metropolis, the 30,000-hectare zone the government itself markets as a 'housing hub, growth engine, and university town,' which is a lot of hats for one stretch of land. That's the project meant to produce the flats that bring 4.8 years back down. The blueprint is due in September. The Development Bureau has not disclosed how much of the September blueprint is Northern Metropolis money against everything else fighting for the same page, and nobody has said when a household on today's list would actually get a key. The government did raise its 2026 GDP forecast to 3.5 to 4.5 percent this week, on the back of 5.1 percent growth in the first half, so there's no shortage of money to talk about spending. There's a shortage of anyone saying where it lands first.

The blueprint lands in September, the same month the government wants to open the redeveloped Huanggang crossing after this weekend's 5,000-strong evacuation drill. Two ribbon cuttings, one calendar, and one number that still hasn't moved. The September blueprint will set a construction timeline for the Northern Metropolis; so far no minister has put a household-level date on it, a name and a deadline the 100,500 families on the list could actually hold the government to.
