THE DAILY NOTE · 2026-09-12

The Saturday That Isn't a Recovery Day. Saturday gets treated as the day you make up for the week: the one big outing, the errand marathon, the birthday party circuit. None of that is time with your kid, it's logistics with your kid present. The fix isn't clearing the whole day, it's carving fifteen minutes out of it that belong to nothing else on the list. Before the first errand, before the party, before the swim lesson drop-off. Pick a window and protect it the way you'd protect a client call. He won't remember the errands. He'll remember whether Saturday had a part that was just his. You don't need a plan for that fifteen minutes, you need to actually stop moving during it. That's the whole trick, and it's the one thing a full week makes you forget how to do.

Tonight: Fifteen minutes, no errands. Before the first Saturday task starts, sit down with him for fifteen minutes and do whatever he picks. Set a timer if you need permission to stop.

2026-09-11 · Friday Night, the Bar Is One Thing. You are tired in a specific way tonight, the kind where you want the evening to just happen without you steering it. That is fine. Lower the bar on purpose instead of failing to hit a high one by accident. Pick one thing, not the whole checklist: bath, story, teeth, bed. One. Tell your kid the plan out loud, so it sounds like a decision and not a surrender: "we're doing pajamas and one story tonight, that's it." A Friday with one deliberate thing beats a Friday with five half-done things, every time. You are not failing at Friday. You are choosing what Friday gets. Face down still buzzes, still glows, still pulls your eyes sideways every four minutes. A closed door between you and the notification does more than any willpower does. Renegotiating the Rules at Bedtime
THE WEEKLY · EDITION 10 · WEEK OF 2026-09-13

A rule a child can argue with is a rule they respect enough to test. Bedtime renegotiation at six is not defiance, it is a child discovering that language can move boundaries, and that is a skill worth having everywhere except at nine at night. The move is not to out-argue him. It is to make the rule bigger than the moment: decided earlier, in daylight, by both of you, so that at 8pm there is nothing left to negotiate, only a thing to follow. Firm and kind, decided before the fatigue hits either of you.

Toddler (1 to 3)

The game: The Same Song Twice. Pick one short song for the walk from bath to cot. Sing it every night, same order, same words. When he asks for a different one, sing it once more and then stop, moving into the cot regardless. A fixed sequence he can predict closes the gap where negotiation usually opens.

The book: Time for Bed (Mem Fox). The whole book is a chain of animals going down for the night, one after another, with no character allowed to stay up. The rhythm does the arguing for you. 中文: 睡覺時間到了 (Chinese edition, Grimm Press)

Preschooler (3 to 5)

The game: The Ticket System. Give him two paper tickets at the start of the bedtime routine, each good for one small ask: one more hug, one more sip of water, one more question. He hands one over each time he uses it. When both are gone, the routine ends without discussion. A visible, finite currency turns an open negotiation into a closed transaction he can see running out.

The book: The Going to Bed Book (Sandra Boynton). A boatload of animals brush teeth, exercise, and climb into bed in strict order, and the joke is how absurd it would be to skip a step.

Early Primary (5 to 8)

The game: The Bedtime Contract. On a Saturday, not at night, sit down together and write the bedtime rule on an index card: lights out time, how many books, how many call-backs allowed. Both of you sign it. Tape it to the door. When he argues at night, you point to the card instead of re-arguing the case. A rule he helped write on a calm afternoon is harder to relitigate than one handed down at 8pm.

The book: Bedtime for Frances (Russell Hoban). Frances runs the full playbook of stalling tactics on her father, tigers, spiders, a crack in the ceiling, and he answers every one without raising his voice or changing the rule. 中文: 小熊法蘭西斯系列 (if available through your local library's Chinese picture book collection)

The situation: The bedtime rule renegotiation. Lights are meant to be out. He is sitting up with a new argument ready: one more story, one more light on, one more reason why tonight is different from every other night.

"The rule is the rule from Saturday. Lights out now. I'll see you in the morning."

Naming the rule as something you both already agreed to, rather than something you are deciding right now, takes you out of the argument entirely.

This week, write the bedtime rule down on a Saturday afternoon, with him, before either of you is tired. One card, one signature each, taped to the door. The card argues at night so you don't have to.

Edition 9 · 2026-09-06 · The Rules You Set Once

A boundary you enforce the same way every time stops being a fight and becomes furniture. Kids do not resent limits, they resent limits that move depending on your mood or how tired you are. The games below all practice the same muscle: holding a line calmly, without raising your voice, until the child learns the line is load-bearing. That predictability is what lets a three-year-old borrow your calm instead of testing for the edges of it every single day.

Toddler (1 to 3)

The game: The Same Door Every Time. Pick one exit routine, shoes on at the same spot, same order, every time you leave the flat. When he stalls or bolts, walk him back to the spot and start the sequence again without commentary. Do this for a week straight. A toddler stops negotiating a transition once the sequence itself becomes the thing that is fixed, not you.

The book: No, David! (David Shannon). David breaks every rule and gets caught every time, and the mother's love never wavers, which is the exact pairing a toddler needs to see: firm and kind in the same page. 中文: 《大衛，不可以！》(Grimm Press Taiwan edition)

Preschooler (3 to 5)

The game: The Broken Record. When he pushes on a rule you have already stated once, repeat the exact same sentence back, word for word, no elaboration, no new argument. Three repeats maximum, then physically redirect him. Practice it once tonight on something low-stakes like bath time. A child stops arguing with a wall faster than he stops arguing with a negotiator, and repeating yourself verbatim turns you into the wall.

The book: David Gets in Trouble (David Shannon). David's excuses map almost exactly onto a preschooler's own, which makes the book a mirror he will laugh at instead of resent. 中文: 《大衛惹麻煩》(Grimm Press Taiwan edition)

Early Primary (5 to 8)

The game: The Family Rule Book. Sit down for fifteen minutes on a Sunday and write three household rules together on an index card, in his handwriting where possible. Post it somewhere visible. When a rule gets broken during the week, point at the card instead of arguing. A rule he helped write is a rule he enforces on himself, which is the entire point of the exercise at this age.

The book: The Judy Moody series (start with Judy Moody Was In a Mood) (Megan McDonald). Judy's mood swings and the family's steady, unimpressed response to them give an early reader a script for what firm and kind sounds like from the kid's side of the table.

The situation: The bedtime rule renegotiation. Lights are supposed to be off. He asks for one more thing, a story, water, a hug, and when you say no he escalates to tears or a full renegotiation of the entire bedtime.

The rule is lights off now. I love you, and the rule does not change tonight.

Naming your love and naming the limit in the same breath tells him the boundary is not a withdrawal of affection, which is the fear driving the escalation in the first place.

Pick one rule this week, just one, and enforce it the exact same way every single time it comes up, even when you are tired and it would be faster to bend. The consistency is the whole intervention. By Friday the fight over that one rule should be shorter than it was on Monday.

Edition 8 · 2026-08-30 · The Work Only You Can Do

There is a category of care a mother's presence does not replace: risk-taking under supervision, and the fixed point a child returns to after testing a limit. Komisar's research on father presence keeps circling this, not as a division of labour but as a distinct function. The child needs someone who will let him climb one rung higher than feels safe and stay close enough to catch him. This week's picks all live in that register: a bit more risk than the default, a bit more independence than yesterday, with you standing close enough to matter.

Toddler (1 to 3)

The game: The High Step. Find a low wall, a raised curb, a step that is slightly taller than what he'd choose alone. Hold one hand, not two. Let him work out the leg swing himself. Three or four climbs and he is done. One held hand is enough safety to attempt something his body was unsure about.

The book: Little Blue Truck (Alice Schertle). A small vehicle helps the big ones when they get stuck in the mud. The toddler in your lap is learning that size does not decide who is useful. 中文: 藍色小卡車 (widely available in Hong Kong bookstores as the Chinese edition)

Preschooler (3 to 5)

The game: The Blindfold Walk. In the flat or a quiet corridor, tie a light scarf over his eyes and let him walk five steps guided only by your voice, not your hand. Swap: he guides you. Two minutes each way is plenty. Trusting a voice instead of a grip is a small rehearsal for trusting himself.

The book: Iggy Peck, Architect (Andrea Beaty). A boy who builds compulsively gets told to stop, then gets proven right when the skill is needed. Good for any child whose enthusiasm outruns the room's patience.

Early Primary (5 to 8)

The game: The Solo Errand. Send him to the corner shop or the next stall alone with exact change and one item to buy, while you watch from a distance he doesn't clock. Debrief on the walk home: what did he say, what did he get back. A task with real consequence, completed without you standing over it, builds competence that praise cannot.

The book: Hatchet (Gary Paulsen). A boy alone in the wilderness works out, piece by piece, that panic is a choice he can decline. Long enough for a child reading independently or a chapter-a-night with you. 中文: 手斧男孩 (available through Hong Kong and Taiwan children's publishers)

The situation: The refusal to try. He sees the monkey bars, the bike without stabilisers, the deep end, and says no before attempting a single try. You know he can. He has decided he can't.

I'm not going to make you. I'm going to stand right here while you look at it for a minute.

Removing the pressure to perform is what lets a child move toward the risk on his own schedule instead of yours.

Pick one activity this week where you'd normally hover and step back a body's length instead. Not out of sight, just out of reach. Watch what he does with the room you gave him.

Edition 7 · 2026-08-23 · The Repair That Actually Sticks

A fix and an apology are not the same event. You correct the thing, the room goes quiet, and you assume the account is settled. It is not, not for a child. Komisar's presence work is specific here: repair is a second act, separate from the correction, where you come back into the same room as a body he can lean on rather than a verdict he has to accept. Skip that second act and the silence hardens into distance instead of dissolving. This week's picks all practice the same move: return, don't just resolve.

Toddler (1 to 3)

The game: The Floor Sit. After any correction or telling-off, get down on the floor near him, no talking, no re-explaining the rule. Just sit close enough to touch for a minute or two until he climbs into you or hands you a toy. At this age reconnection is physical before it is verbal, and your body returning is the whole apology.

The book: Llama Llama Time to Share (Anna Dewdney). A conflict, a consequence, and a return to warmth, all inside one bedtime-length story, which gives you the shape without needing to explain it. 中文: 有中文版《拉玛拉玛该分享了》

Preschooler (3 to 5)

The game: The Second Hello. Once things have calmed down, knock on the door frame of wherever he is and say hello again, like you are walking back into the room for the first time. Let him decide how to respond, wave, ignore you, climb on you. The re-entry ritual gives him a low-stakes way to signal he is ready without having to say so out loud.

The book: The Rabbit Listened (Cori Doerrfeld). Already used for a different situation this season, but its second half, about someone coming back and just sitting there, is the exact move this week is teaching, so it earns a reread if it is on the shelf. 中文: 有中文版《小兔子聽你說》

Early Primary (5 to 8)

The game: The One Good Minute. That evening, spend one minute doing something he picked, a card trick, a Lego build, a video he wants to show you, no correcting, no teaching, just watching. Say nothing about the earlier incident. A specific, undiluted minute of your attention tells him the relationship is intact more efficiently than any conversation about it.

The book: Because of Winn-Dixie (Kate DiCamillo). A story built entirely on people who mess up, go quiet, and slowly find their way back to each other, which is the whole curriculum for this week in novel form. 中文: 有中文版《因為溫迪克斯》

The situation: The silent treatment after the fix. You corrected something, calmly, fairly, hours ago. He has not looked at you since. You start to wonder if you need to explain yourself again.

I'm here. I'm not upset anymore. You don't have to talk, I'll just sit with you.

Explaining the correction again reopens the case; sitting there closes it, because presence is the evidence he is actually waiting for.

Pick one correction this week, however small, and follow it with a deliberate return: sit near him, watch what he's doing, say nothing about the incident. That second act is the part he remembers.

Edition 6 · 2026-08-16 · Repairing the Break, Not Rushing It

Komisar's presence work makes a distinction fathers skip past: the transition after a rupture matters more than the rupture itself. A tantrum, a slammed workbook, a meltdown at the market, these end the moment they end. What the child remembers is what you did in the sixty seconds after. Rushing to fix the mood, or punishing the mood, both teach the same wrong lesson: that big feelings make the adult unreliable. Staying boring and present teaches the true one. This week's picks are all about that after-moment, the repair, sized small enough to fit a Tuesday.

Toddler (1 to 3)

The game: The Puddle Jump Reset. After any meltdown ends, no debrief, no lecture: just crouch down, take both his hands, and do three exaggerated jumps together like landing in puddles, even on dry floor. Then go back to whatever was next. The physical reset signals the account is closed without a single word about the meltdown itself.

The book: Llama Llama Mad at Mama (Anna Dewdney). A meltdown in a shopping cart, resolved with a hug and no moral attached, which is the model worth repeating out loud. 中文: 《小羊駝生氣了》(繁體中文版)

Preschooler (3 to 5)

The game: The Do-Over Replay. Once things are calm, five or ten minutes later, say 'let's do that bit again' and physically re-walk the last thirty seconds before the blowup, this time narrating the better version together. He plays himself, you play yourself. Re-running the moment as a scene rather than a scolding lets him rehearse the version that works.

The book: The Rabbit Listened (Cori Doerrfeld). Every animal in the book tries to fix the sad rabbit's mood and fails; the one who just sits there wins, a useful mirror for a father mid-repair.

Early Primary (5 to 8)

The game: The Rewind Button. After a blowup and a cooldown, ask him to be the director: 'if you could rewind to right before that and change one thing, what would you press?' Let him say it, don't correct it, just nod and say 'noted for next time.' Naming his own better move, instead of hearing yours, is what actually gets rehearsed and remembered.

The book: Ramona Quimby, Age 8 (Beverly Cleary). Ramona blows up constantly and is loved constantly anyway, which is the whole curriculum in one paperback.

The situation: The silent treatment after the fix. The meltdown is over. He got what he needed, or didn't and accepted it, but now he won't look at you, won't answer, arms crossed on the sofa.

"You don't have to talk to me yet. I'm going to sit right here until you're ready." Then sit down near him and say nothing else.

You're not owed forgiveness on your timeline; staying near without demanding a response is the working brake doing its job after the surge has passed.

One repair a day this week, nothing more. When something ends badly, don't lecture and don't over-apologize: just get physically close again within a few minutes and let the nearness do the talking. That's the whole rep.

Edition 5 · 2026-08-09 · The Brake System, Not The Referee

A three-year-old melting down at the market and an eight-year-old slamming a workbook shut are running the same program: too much feeling, not enough capacity to hold it. Your job is not to talk the feeling away or explain why it is unreasonable. Your job is to be the steadier nervous system in the room until his catches up. Komisar's point about borrowed calm holds at every age band below. This week's picks all do one thing: put you in the room, on the floor, or across the table for ten honest minutes, no equipment, no lecture.

Toddler (1 to 3)

The game: The Bear Hug Hold. When he is winding up (not mid-tantrum, the ten seconds before), scoop him up and hold him snug against your chest, swaying, saying nothing. Count to twenty in your head. Let him squirm and settle on his own clock. Deep pressure and a steady body regulate an escalating nervous system faster than any word can.

The book: The Kissing Hand (Audrey Penn). A small raccoon learns his mother's comfort travels with him even at a distance, which is the whole toddler curriculum in one bedtime read. 中文: 《魔法親親》(繁體中文版, 上誼文化)

Preschooler (3 to 5)

The game: The Volcano Check. Ask him to show you on a scale from sleeping volcano to erupting volcano how he feels right now, using his hands to demonstrate. Do this at random moments all week, not just during meltdowns, so it becomes a normal check-in rather than a crisis tool. Naming the feeling as a scale instead of a verdict gives a preschooler language for something he cannot yet explain in sentences.

The book: Sometimes I'm Bombaloo (Rachel Vail). A girl who turns into a wordless, growling version of herself when she's furious, and finds her way back, narrated entirely from inside the feeling.

Early Primary (5 to 8)

The game: The Two-Column List. When homework or a task stalls, draw two columns on scrap paper: 'Things I can do alone' and 'Things I need help with.' Let him sort the stuck task into the columns himself. You only act on the second column. Sorting the problem restores a sense of control faster than either doing it for him or telling him to keep trying.

The book: Alexander and the Terrible, Horrible, No Good, Very Bad Day (Judith Viorst). A kid narrates a day where everything goes wrong and nobody rescues him from the feeling, they just let him have it and it passes. 中文: 《亞歷山大和他可怕、很可怕、非常可怕、糟糕透頂的一天》(遠流)

The situation: The workbook slam. He throws the pencil down, says he can't do it, and refuses to look at the page again. You have twenty minutes before dinner and a rising urge to just tell him the answer.

I can see this is frustrating. We're stopping for five minutes, then we'll look at it together.

Naming the feeling once and setting the pause without negotiating gives his nervous system the exit it's asking for, and the timer, not you, ends the standoff.

Pick one moment this week, market, homework, bedtime, where you usually talk first. Try holding still and quiet instead, and let him find his own way down. Ten seconds of your calm is the whole intervention.

Edition 4 · 2026-08-02 · The Weekly Deliberate Practice, Week 4

Regulation is a skill you lend before it is one they own. A toddler mid-meltdown, a preschooler frozen at the top of a slide, a seven-year-old stuck on a word problem: same nervous system problem, three sizes. Your job is not to fix the feeling or solve the problem. It is to be the steady adult in the room long enough that the child's own system learns the shape of steady. That is what this week's three picks and the script below are built around. None of it takes a free Saturday. It takes ten minutes on a weeknight, done on purpose.

Toddler (1 to 3)

The game: The Heavy Work Carry. Hand him something with real weight, a bag of rice, a stack of books, a full water bottle, and give him a job: carry it to the kitchen, put it by the door. Walk beside him, narrate nothing, let him feel the effort land in his arms and shoulders. Two or three trips is the whole game. Proprioceptive input, the feeling of muscles working against real resistance, is one of the most reliable calm-down levers a small body has, no screen or snack required.

The book: The Runaway Bunny (Margaret Wise Brown). A call-and-response of a bunny threatening to run and a mother matching every escape with pursuit, read aloud it becomes the toddler's own bedtime proof that he cannot outrun being found. 中文: 逃家小兔 (available in Traditional Chinese, Hong Kong bookstores stock it under the Chinese title)

Preschooler (3 to 5)

The game: The Weather Report. Ask her to be the weather forecaster for her own body: is it sunny, stormy, foggy, windy today? Let her answer in one word and draw the sky on a scrap of paper if she wants. No follow-up questions, no fixing, just log the report. Naming an internal state in a low-stakes, playful frame builds the vocabulary she will need to name it in a high-stakes one.

The book: When Sophie Gets Angry, Really, Really Angry (Molly Bang). Sophie erupts, runs, climbs a tree and lets the world hold her until the anger passes, giving your child a picture of a big feeling that ends without anyone being the villain. 中文: 蘇菲生氣了，非常非常的生氣 (in print, commonly stocked in Hong Kong libraries and bookstores)

Early Primary (5 to 8)

The game: The Stuck Point Log. When homework hits a wall, don't solve it. Say 'show me exactly where it stopped working' and have him point to the line or the number. Write just that phrase in a small notebook you keep for this. Close the notebook, take a two-minute break, come back to only that line. Isolating the exact stuck point turns a wall of frustration into one solvable inch, which is the actual skill under the homework.

The book: Ralph S. Mouse (Beverly Cleary). Ralph keeps failing at driving his toy motorcycle and keeps trying anyway, a slow-build model of competence built from repeated, unglamorous attempts rather than one lucky run.

The situation: The homework shutdown. Books open, pencil down, arms crossed, and the first sign of trouble is not tears, it is silence and a flat refusal to try the next line.

I can see this is the hard part. Show me exactly where it stopped making sense, just that one spot.

Naming the wall out loud and shrinking it to one line converts a shutdown into a task small enough to restart.

Pick one: the heavy carry, the weather check-in, or the stuck-point notebook. Run it twice this week, same rough time of day if you can manage it. The tool matters less than the repetition. By the third time, he will start doing half of it without you.

Edition 3 · 2026-07-26 · The One-Thing Rule for Summer Weeks

Fatherhood advice fails when it asks for more than a working week can hold. The developmental research does not actually ask for more: rough play, a nightly book, a script for the hard moment, each done once, done consistently, does more than a weekend of ambitious plans that collapse by Wednesday. The child is not counting hours. He is counting on the pattern repeating. Pick one thing this week and let it repeat. That is the whole method, not a starter version of a bigger method.

Toddler (1 to 3)

The game: The Sock Slide. Clear a metre of hallway or living room floor, put socks on him, and let him skate. You hold both hands for the first few runs, then step back to one hand, then just your voice. He will ask for more than you have time for. It hands a toddler controlled speed and controlled falling, which is most of what rough-and-tumble play is actually training.

The book: Owl Babies (Martin Waddell). Three owl chicks wait out the dark for their mother and she comes back, every single read. For a child this age the plot is the reassurance. 中文: 貓頭鷹寶寶 (Traditional Chinese edition, widely stocked in HK bookstores)

Preschooler (3 to 5)

The game: The Delivery Run. Give him a small object (a spoon, a toy, a folded napkin) and a destination across the room or flat. He delivers it, you give him the next one, and the runs get sillier: hopping, crab walk, eyes closed with your hand on his shoulder. It turns a preschooler's need to move into a sequence with a clear finish, which is the skill behind following any multi-step instruction later.

The book: The Rabbit Listened (Cori Doerrfeld). A rabbit sits with a child through a hard feeling without trying to fix it, which is the exact move you are asking of yourself most weeks.

Early Primary (5 to 8)

The game: The Fact or Fiction Round. At dinner or in the lift, each of you states three things about your day, one of them false. Everyone else guesses which. He gets a turn to catch you lying too. It gets a kid this age narrating his day without the flat 'nothing happened' answer, because now there is a game reason to give detail.

The book: The One and Only Ivan (Katherine Applegate). A gorilla telling his own story in short chapters, funny and sad in the same page, right at the edge of what a strong early reader can handle alone or aloud with you. 中文: 我是艾文 (Traditional Chinese edition available through major HK book retailers)

The situation: The homework shutdown. He is stuck on one question, the pencil is down, and the next sound out of him is going to be either tears or a thrown eraser.

Edition 1 · 2026-07-13 · The One Rep You Owe This Week

A child's nervous system learns its ceiling from the adults who hold it. Rough play teaches the ceiling for excitement. A bedtime slot teaches the ceiling for waiting. A market meltdown teaches the ceiling for wanting. None of it needs a curriculum or a free Saturday. It needs one repetition, done the same way, by the same person, often enough that the child stops testing whether it will happen and starts trusting that it will. Pick one thing from this week's list. Do it more than once.

Toddler (1 to 3)

The game: The Catch and Drop. Hold him under the arms and lift him a foot off the ground, then set him down. Do it again the second he says or signals more. Stop the instant he says stop, no exceptions, and name it out loud when you do: 'You said stop, so I stopped.' A toddler who controls the brake on a big-feeling game learns that big feelings have brakes.

The book: Where the Wild Things Are (Maurice Sendak). A boy gets sent to his room angry, sails to where the wild things are, runs the whole show, and comes home to dinner still hot. It is the only picture book that takes a toddler's rage seriously without moralizing it. 中文: Traditional Chinese edition 《野獸國》available from Hong Kong booksellers (Grimm Press / 格林文化).

Preschooler (3 to 5)

The game: Footbridge Statues. On any footbridge or MTR platform wait, call out 'freeze' at random and both hold whatever position you're in until you call 'go.' No props, no setup, works in a queue. Practicing stop-on-command in a low-stakes game builds the same muscle he needs when you say stop for real.

The book: Ish (Peter H. Reynolds). A boy quits drawing when his brother laughs at a picture, until his sister reframes 'bad' as 'ish' and he starts making things again. Useful for the age where first attempts get abandoned fast. 中文: Traditional Chinese edition 《像不像,沒關係》available in Hong Kong (小天下).

Early Primary (5 to 8)

The game: The Ten-Minute Interview. One night this week, sit with him for ten minutes and ask three questions you don't already know the answer to: what was the best part of today, what was hard, what does he want to build this weekend. No phone, no multitasking, just the questions and his answers. Named, undivided attention at this age teaches him his inner life is worth reporting, not just his behavior.

The book: Frindle (Andrew Clements). A fifth grader invents a new word for pen and watches it spread past his teacher's control. Good for a kid old enough to enjoy a protagonist who wins by being stubborn in a good direction.

The situation: The wet market meltdown. He wants the toy at the stall, you said no once already, and now he's escalating in front of a market full of people who have all seen this exact scene before.

I know you really want it. We're not getting it today. I'm right here.

One line for the feeling and one for the limit is enough information; repeating it calmly is what tells him you're not going to be talked out of the limit, which is what lets him stop testing it.
