A Demo Video Doing A Prospectus's Job

Deep Robotics timed a single-take video of a robot climbing stairs to the week China's humanoid IPO mania peaked, because four units sold in two years can't do that job.
Magnus Honeyfield Science Desk, Senior Correspondent Sunday, August 16, 2026

The Stair Climb's Real Job

Deep Robotics published a single-take video on August 13 showing its DR02 humanoid climbing an outdoor concrete staircase without a handrail, a support cable, or a cut in the footage. That last detail matters more than the stairs. The unbroken-take standard exists because robotics demo videos have long drawn skepticism, and a cut is exactly where you would hide the fall that came before the good run. What the robot is actually doing, step by step, is a problem roboticists call terrain-adaptive balance: reading the height and angle of the next step through onboard sensing, shifting its weight forward before the foot lands, and correcting mid-stride if the surface gives differently than the last one did. Wheels don't need this. Legs do, which is why stair climbing has stood in for 'the robot is real' in humanoid robotics demos. The video is genuine engineering. It is also, transparently, a sales document: Deep Robotics has sold four DR02 humanoid units combined across 2024 and 2025, and its $1.48 billion STAR Market listing application has sat under Shanghai review since May. A demo reel is what you publish when the order book can't make your case yet.

The IPO That Set The Clock

Deep Robotics didn't pick this week by accident. Unitree Robotics, its Hangzhou-based rival, priced China's first humanoid-robot IPO on August 6 at 150.8 yuan a share, a $9.04 billion valuation, with retail demand so lopsided the offering was oversubscribed more than 8,000 times, a STAR Market record; ordinary investors who queued for shares had roughly a 0.018 percent chance of an allocation. The stock begins trading in Shanghai this week, sometime between August 17 and 21. Unitree's numbers back the enthusiasm better than most IPO manias do: 2025 revenue of about 1.7 billion yuan, four times the year before, on more than 5,500 humanoids shipped at 60 percent gross margins. The more interesting number is smaller. DeepSeek, the Hangzhou AI lab behind the open-weight models that rattled Silicon Valley earlier this year, bought a 2.31 percent stake for 140.8 million yuan, locked up for 36 months, in a deal giving each company priority access to the other: robot hardware for DeepSeek's models, real-world training data for Unitree's machines. That is the AI story sitting inside the robotics story. Whoever builds the 'brain' for a humanoid needs a fleet of bodies to learn from, and Unitree just became one company's fleet.

A robot climbing stairs on a single unbroken take tells Shanghai investors what they already believe: yes, the legs work. It doesn't answer the commercial question. Four units sold is not a market, and the STAR Market's review of Deep Robotics' listing application, pending since May, is the actual gate that decides whether the company's engineering translates into the kind of valuation Unitree just proved possible at 219 times earnings. Unitree's own trading debut this week is the only real data point either company has. Watch where it opens.
