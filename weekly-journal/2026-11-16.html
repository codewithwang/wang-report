Cyber

Cursor traced an October marketplace compromise to credentials exposed through Fireworks

The disclosure explains how historical coding records enabled malicious software distribution, while successful compromise of recipients remains unquantified.

Cursor's 13 November update, reported by The Hacker News on 15 November, supplied a fuller account of an earlier intrusion. The company said an access key exposed through Fireworks AI allowed an attacker to copy a shared storage bucket containing coding agent traces, mostly from 2025, from sessions outside Privacy Mode. Unredacted credentials in those records enabled access to selected internal services and further information. [S7]

On 29 October, the attacker used a stolen credential to place malicious Claude Code extension builds directly on the cache serving Cursor's marketplace. Cursor identified versions 2.1.312 and 2.1.313 and an exposure window of approximately 11 hours. It estimated that at most 5,000 users requesting the affected cached versions potentially received a malicious build. That is a potential receipt estimate, not a count of computers successfully compromised. The separate figure of fewer than 0.01% of customers concerned API credentials exposed in the historical traces. [S7]

Cursor reported removing the builds, notifying affected installers, revoking and rotating credentials, and ending Fireworks' access to shared data and inference traffic. It said no additional malicious activity had been detected since 1 November. It also reported no evidence of impact on Privacy Mode users, customer code repositories, its production environment, the Cursor application and official updates, or other marketplace extensions. These remain the company's stated investigation boundaries. Independent findings and examination of affected computers would help establish whether those boundaries hold and how many installations produced persistent access. [S7]

CISA's supplied catalogue extract records 12 vulnerability additions dated 9 through 13 November. Its preceding week contains three, dated 3, 4 and 6 November. This measures additions to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue within the extract. It does not measure attack volume, establish when exploitation began or provide a complete census of incidents. [S39]

A separate surveillance development concerned newly identified customers. Citizen Lab's account dated 12 November described Bloomberg reporting that expanded the known purchasers of Penlink's advertising based surveillance system Webloc, including Singapore and several European states. It extends earlier research into the system's customer network. Acquisition alone does not establish an agency's deployment practices or the lawfulness of particular uses; procurement documents, deployment evidence and oversight findings remain necessary to assess those consequences. US departures resumed, but flight cancellations continued the following day

Restoring air traffic control operations allowed departures to restart before airlines had recovered their schedules.

The nationwide US ground stop ran from 15:54 to 20:15 EST on 13 November, equivalent to 04:54 to 09:15 HKT on 14 November. Officials said a router installed during an upgrade from copper wiring to fibre flooded the aviation communications network with connection attempts. Seventeen of 22 air route traffic control centres experienced instability. The FAA said identifying the maintenance location took 45 minutes because records were maintained on paper. Officials reported no indication of a cyberattack. [S11]

The BBC cited FlightAware figures of more than 8,500 delayed and nearly 8,000 cancelled flights on 13 November, followed by more than 7,000 delays and 5,900 cancellations on 14 November, using the report's US dates. These are separate daily flight counts, not unique travellers. Their continuation after the ground stop ended establishes the remaining operational disruption. Investigators were still examining whether faulty equipment or the installation work caused the failure. Findings on that question, revised maintenance arrangements and subsequent flight performance would show whether recovery extended beyond reopening the network. [S11]

Shanghai imposed research sanctions over safety and disclosure failures

The findings document failed safeguards around an earlier fatal treatment, while the effect of corrective work on patient protection remains unverified.

Shanghai's investigation group announced its findings on 13 November concerning experimental gene editing treatment for a child with Snijders Blok Campeau syndrome. The child received treatment on 24 March 2025 and died on 31 March. On 2 April, Xinhua Hospital's emergency ethics review attributed the death to thrombotic microangiopathy as a gene therapy complication and required the study to end. The related Nature paper appeared on 18 February 2026. This week's development was the investigation's findings and disciplinary action. [S32]

The official notice identified failures before treatment and during publication. Primate safety results were not promptly communicated to the clinical lead. The hospital approved research while requested safety material remained outstanding, and consent did not sufficiently emphasise the risk of death. The paper omitted the negative clinical outcome, relevant animal safety findings and gifts received from the family. Investigators reported no evidence of data fabrication, while identifying discrepancies between the methods used and those described, alongside other research practice failures. [S32]

Two researchers were dismissed and barred from specified research projects and awards for five years. Other personnel and institutions received sanctions, and authorities reported inspections and corrective work. Those actions are documented responses. Evidence that safety reports now reach decision makers, consent documents adequately explain risks and approvals depend on completed checks would show whether patient protection has improved. [S32]
