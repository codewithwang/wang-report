Larak Strike Escalates as Iran Hits Jordan, Oil Hits $90

Overnight retaliation widened from a single strait strike into a multi-front exchange, and oil at 90 dollars a barrel is the number APAC treasurers now have to hedge against before Tehran's next move is known.
Walter Wang · WR Editor-in-Chief · Hong Kong

Saturday's strike on Larak Island was framed here as a reopening of a war Washington had called finished. By Monday it had become something wider. Iran answered not at the strait but at bases in Jordan, according to CNN, pulling a third country's territory into the exchange for the first time since the ceasefire broke. The UAE said it intercepted an Iranian drone over its own territorial waters, the first confirmed engagement on Gulf Arab soil rather than at sea. The WSJ reported fighting flaring around Iranian rocket launchers hit in a follow-up US strike, meaning both sides have now traded blows on at least three occasions since Saturday, not one. The market moved before the wires caught up. The New York Times put oil at 90 dollars a barrel by Monday, the first attacks in a month having jolted energy markets, with US gasoline still running 37 percent above its pre-war level. The trading desks that price tanker risk through the strait are now treating this as sustained conflict rather than a one-off exchange, a shift Saturday's ceasefire language did not anticipate. For a Hong Kong or Singapore trading desk, the number that matters by the 06:30 open is not the missile count but that 90-dollar print, since it now sits inside every APAC refiner's and airline's hedging book at a level last modelled a month ago. A regional airline's treasury desk now has to decide by this week's fuel-hedge review whether to lock in at 90 or bet on de-escalation, a bet the last month has twice punished. The strait itself stays open for now. Whether it stays that way depends on whether Tehran's next move lands in Jordan again, in the Gulf, or back at the water.
