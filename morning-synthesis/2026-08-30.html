Larak Strike Reopens the Iran War Washington Called Won

US forces hit Iranian missile launchers again overnight, the first strike since July, while the White House markets a Venezuela oil deal that leans on the same untouched sanctions machinery.
Walter Wang · WR Editor-in-Chief · Hong Kong

Washington spent July calling the Iran campaign finished business. That story broke overnight when US forces struck Iranian missile launchers on Larak Island, an Iranian island in the Strait of Hormuz that has become a staging point for anti-ship weapons, the first such strike since July, according to SCMP citing a US official. The trigger, per Axios, was intelligence that Tehran was moving to mine the Strait of Hormuz, the chokepoint that carries roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil. That is not a regime that sued for peace three months ago; it is one still contesting the waterway that sets the price of every barrel APAC refiners import. The Washington Post reports military leaders are now warning Defense Secretary Hegseth against extending the operation further, so the dispute over whether last month's campaign actually ended is playing out inside the Pentagon, not just in Tehran. This column called the earlier phase concluded. The Larak strike says it was a pause, not a resolution, and that call was wrong. The same 48 hours produced Donald Trump's "biggest oil deal in world history," a Venezuela arrangement that hands US firms access to 65 billion barrels of reserves and leans, per the New York Times, on Alejandro Betancourt, a politically connected private operator, rather than the state oil company directly, the same kind of sanctions workaround Washington is simultaneously trying to shut down against Iran. Two barrels of oil, two sanctions regimes, one administration deciding which chokepoints to police and which to route around. For a Hong Kong trade desk, the number to watch is Brent's reaction to Larak by the London open; APAC crude buyers who priced in a quiet Gulf for autumn now have to re-run hedges before the next tanker transits Hormuz. Watch whether Hegseth extends the campaign against his own generals' advice, the point where a policy dispute becomes an operational one.
