  • 00:15 HKT Attackers compromise .gh, .sl and a third registry, get Google certificates Kai Tanner explains how a registry compromise becomes a valid padlock, and why every organisation under those domains shares the exposure. read more

    Kai Tanner, CYBER INTEL

    A certificate authority issues a certificate to whoever can show control of a domain. For a domain under a country registry, that control ultimately runs through the registry. Google said on October 6 that attackers compromised three country-code top-level domains and obtained unauthorized HTTPS certificates for several Google domains.

    The certificates are valid. A browser shown one for a Google domain, signed by a certificate authority it trusts, raises no warning. Anyone who can also steer the victim's traffic can sit in the middle of that session and read it, with a padlock on the screen.

    The report names .gh and .sl among the three. Google's account of its own position is short: the systems it runs were not the point of entry. Google was the target here. It was not the weak point.

    Now take an Asian bank with a regional office registered under .gh or .sl, or under any country registry it has never had reason to contact. Its web login, its mail gateway and its API endpoints carry certificates that depend on a registry run by people the bank's third-party risk team has not assessed. The vendor register lists the cloud provider and the managed security service. It does not list the registry.

    The administrator who owns that .sl or .gh domain has one place to look this morning: the public Certificate Transparency logs, which record every certificate a trusted authority issues. A certificate for their hostname, dated around the compromise, that nobody in the company ordered, is the finding. If the logs are clean, that is also the finding, and it costs ten minutes.

    The control that changes the outcome is a Certification Authority Authorization record on the domain, restricting issuance to the one authority the company uses, paired with an alert on new entries in the transparency logs. It does not stop a registry compromise. It narrows which authority the attacker has to persuade, and it turns an undetected certificate into a ticket.

  • 00:19 HKT Chinese militia boat off Taiwan's east coast, where US and Japan would arrive Mei Chen on a Chinese militia boat found off Taiwan's Pacific coast, the side any US or Japanese reinforcement would approach. read more

    Mei Chen, GEOPOLITICAL

    Maritime militia boats are fishing vessels that take orders from a military. One has now been spotted off Taiwan's east coast, for the first time, according to a patrol's findings reported this week.

    The east coast is the Pacific side. Chinese pressure on Taiwan has been drawn on the western map, across the strait, where the PLA's aircraft and ships have been logged for years. Our flashpoint map has recorded 60 moves in the Taiwan Strait since September 8, the latest on October 6.

    A civilian-flagged hull on the other coast belongs to a different map. It is the water through which American and Japanese forces would arrive in a crisis, and it is where the PLA would want to know who is already there.

    The timing matches the exercise calendar. SCMP reported this week that expanded Japan-US military exercises could complicate PLA planning over Taiwan. A militia boat is the cheapest answer to that reporting: it costs a fishing license, and it can sit still without anyone calling it an act of war.

    The method is not new. The Philippines flagged this week that the Chinese presence in disputed waters has nearly doubled. Manila learned the pattern first: the hulls arrive as fishermen, stay as a fixture, and the coast guard that answers them gets to choose between ignoring a trespass and treating a fishing boat as a warship.

    Taiwan's coast guard now has that choice on a second coastline. A patrol that finds one boat on the east coast will be sent back to count the next.

    Cheung Kwok-keung, HK DESK

    Here is a sentence you don't hear often. Three of the six buildings are coming down.

    That's CK Asset's "starter homes" project. Starter homes are the flats meant for first-time buyers. People who saved for years and wanted a front door of their own.

    The developer says demolishing and rebuilding three blocks is the most "time-saving" solution. Read that again.

    If knocking a building down and starting over is the quick way, the slow way must have been something. Fixing steelwork flat by flat, probably, with an inspector at every step. Nobody wants to wait for that. So the cranes come in instead.

    The trouble started with a steelwork scandal, and it has now run for two years. That is a long time to wait on a flat you were told was a starter. By the time anyone gets keys, "starter" will be the wrong word.

    Look, I'm glad they're not patching things up and hoping. Nobody wants to live in a block that needed an argument to prove it was safe. But "time-saving" is a funny way to describe a fix that begins with a wrecking ball.

    Three blocks stay up. Three come down and go up again. If you know someone in this project, ask them which half they're in.

Attackers Compromise Three Country Registries and Obtain Google Certificates

Google was the target, not the weak point, and the episode shows that a domain's security can rest on a registry its owner has never assessed.

Attackers have taken control of three country-code domain registries and used that position to obtain unauthorized HTTPS certificates for several Google domains. Google disclosed the compromise on October 6, according to The Hacker News, which names .gh and .sl among the three registries.

The mechanism is the part worth understanding. A certificate authority issues a certificate to whoever can show control of a domain, and for a domain under a country registry, that control ultimately runs through the registry. Take the registry, and you can make the case for a certificate that looks entirely legitimate. Google's own account is brief: its systems were not the point of entry. It was the target, and it was not the weak point.

Kai Tanner's Cyber Intel desk has the full reading, and it is worth your time. The certificates are valid. A browser shown one for a Google domain, signed by an authority it trusts, raises no warning at all. Anyone who can also steer a victim's traffic can sit in the middle of that session and read it, with the padlock still showing. Kai also sets out why this reaches well beyond Google: every organisation with a name under those registries inherits the same exposure, and most have never had a reason to think about who runs the registry above them.

That is the quiet lesson in the episode. Companies spend years assessing the cloud provider, the managed security service and the software supplier. The registry that sits above their domain name rarely appears on any vendor list, yet it can decide who is allowed to vouch for them. The padlock, in other words, certifies the certificate. It has never certified the chain of people behind it.

Google says the damage began and ended above its own systems, and nothing in the reporting so far says how long the attackers held that position or whose certificates beyond Google's were affected.

What this means is plain enough. Trust on the internet is built in layers, and the top layer is run by institutions most users and most companies never examine. When one of those institutions is compromised, even the best-defended company in the world can be impersonated, and the company does not need to have done anything wrong.
