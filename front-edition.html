  • 06:31 HKT BeyondTrust vault theft leaves stolen passwords working after the fix Kai Tanner explains why repairing BeyondTrust can leave stolen passwords working on the systems they still unlock. read more

    Kai Tanner, CYBER INTEL

    An administrator can fix the BeyondTrust flaw and still have an attacker holding a working password for another system. Fixing the defect alone cannot invalidate a password already copied out. The receiving system still accepts the credential.

    An October 19 report says attackers exploited CVE-2026-102764 for seven weeks before disclosure, draining enterprise credential vaults. It attributes the operation to Chinese state actors. That attribution remains the report’s claim.

    The vulnerability concerns improper origin validation in internal messaging components of BeyondTrust Remote Support. Our vulnerability feed records its addition to the US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency’s Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on October 16. That establishes exploitation of the flaw; it does not independently establish who emptied a particular vault.

    For the person restoring access, the vault theft changes the unit of recovery. A repaired BeyondTrust installation answers whether that entry point has been closed. It says nothing about whether a separate system will accept a stolen password.

    The distinction has an operational cost. Replacing a vault entry and changing the password at the system it unlocks are separate actions. A new value stored in the vault cannot revoke the old value somewhere else.

    Recovery therefore reaches beyond the affected product to credential replacement at those receiving systems. Until an exposed password is invalidated there, it can still admit an attacker to the system it unlocks.

    Mei Chen, GEOPOLITICAL

    Taiwan has detained two drone executives over Chinese-made chips in military contracts, putting the people supplying its defence under direct scrutiny.

    The detentions are a concrete consequence. Detention alone establishes neither their guilt nor a defect in the equipment.

    The consequential next step lies with the military customer: whether scrutiny reaches the contracted drones themselves. A case against suppliers and a decision about equipment require different evidence. The reported detentions do not establish that the drones have been rejected or withdrawn from use.

    Taiwan also reported driving away Chinese vessels in coordinated maritime harassment. In the Taiwan Strait, the military must confront pressure from outside while examining what enters through its own purchasing system. Chinese origin acquires a different consequence when it appears inside a military contract.

    Our flashpoint register previously recorded Chinese naval vessels and official ships around Taiwan. The procurement case brings that scrutiny inside the supply relationship. Counting ships cannot answer what the military has bought.

    If scrutiny establishes that the contracted equipment breaches procurement requirements, the military buyer will have to decide whether it can accept or continue using those drones.

French Authorities Impose Curfews After Riots Overwhelm Police

Restrictions in Paris and other cities bring a confrontation over policing and living conditions into the daily lives of people beyond the protests.

French authorities have imposed curfews in parts of Paris and other cities after nationwide riots overwhelmed police, the BBC reports. A confrontation that grew from school protests has reached the point where the government is restricting movement to regain control. The immediate change is concrete: the response now extends beyond confronting people involved in violence to limiting when others can be outside. That brings the consequences of the unrest into the lives of residents who may have taken no part in it.

The New York Times traces the escalation through the injury of a French student struck by a tear gas grenade. Its reporting places that injury within a wider confrontation over policing and living conditions. Those grievances matter to the sequence: school protests became a national conflict, police were overwhelmed, and authorities imposed curfews. The supplied accounts do not establish that every city followed the same course or that everyone involved wanted the same thing. They do establish that the dispute has expanded beyond its original setting, leaving the government confronting both violence and anger about how the state exercises its authority.

The curfews give authorities a means of restricting movement; they do not, by themselves, settle either grievance. Restoring order and restoring confidence involve different demands on the government. People need protection from violence, and the use of police force remains part of the dispute they are living through. A restriction can be announced more readily than confidence can be restored. Governments do tend to favour the part with an announcement.

The significance is that France's response has moved from policing a confrontation to restricting ordinary public life across parts of several cities. That may constrain the unrest, but it also broadens the reach of the government's decisions. The underlying political problem survives the curfew: authority has to protect people while answering for how it treats them.
