  • 06:11 HKT Three Linux backdoors disguised as Asian mail security products found on edge systems Kai Tanner on three backdoors posing as mail security products. The gateway nobody questions is the perfect hiding place, and he explains the check that catches them. read more

    Kai Tanner, CYBER INTEL

    Dark Reading reports three newly discovered Linux backdoors built to look like legitimate mail security products from Asian vendors. The write-up says they walk and quack like legitimate edge solutions, so it is hard to tell they are not.

    A mail gateway sits at the network boundary and handles every inbound message. It runs with high privilege, and nobody expects to read its process list. An implant named after the product the administrator is paying for does not need to hide. It only needs to be where the product would be.

    The same week, Fortinet warned that CVE-2026-104286, a path traversal flaw in FortiMail rated 9.8 out of 10, was being exploited as a zero-day to run unauthorized code, and CISA added it to its exploited-vulnerabilities catalogue on 1 October. Neither report links the two. A defender reading both still ends up with the same task, which is to find out what is running on the mail gateway.

    Many organisations may not have a ready answer to that. Edge appliances tend to be treated as sealed units, patched when the vendor says so and inspected when something breaks.

    The control that changes this is a baseline. Take the file hashes and running services from a known-clean gateway, store them off the device, and compare against them on a schedule. A backdoor named after the product passes any check that reads names. It fails a check that reads hashes.

  • 06:13 HKT OpenAI Delays Its IPO Over Safety, Cuts Ties With Three Safety Researchers, and Keeps Shipping Agents Aya Nakamura on OpenAI's three speeds: an IPO delayed for safety, researchers let go, and an agent that orders dinner anyway. read more

    Aya Nakamura, AI FOCUS

    Three OpenAI desks are moving at three different speeds this week, and the gaps between them are the story.

    The first desk is finance. Ars Technica reports that OpenAI has delayed its IPO over AI safety concerns. An IPO is the moment a company opens its books and its risks to public investors, so a delay for safety reasons means the company judges something about its own systems to be harder to explain to shareholders than it wants.

    The second desk is the people who do the safety work. TechCrunch, citing the Wall Street Journal, reports that OpenAI has cut ties with three safety researchers. I have no reporting on why, and the sources here do not say. What the sequence shows is that a company which says safety is delaying its IPO has also parted with three of the people hired to do that work.

    The third desk is product. The Verge's hands-on piece describes OpenAI's Dot agent ordering dinner for you. An agent that orders dinner has your payment details and permission to act without asking each time. That is a different risk from a chatbot that gives a wrong answer, because a wrong action costs money and cannot be unsent.

    Here is why that matters. Security researcher Matthew Green, quoted by Simon Willison on October 1, argues that sandboxing alone may not contain a compromised agent. A sandbox is a sealed room for the agent to work in. His point is that a malicious payload which hijacks one agent can be handed to the next agent it talks to, so the seal protects the room and not the conversation between rooms. An agent like Dot sits at the end of a chain of services. Each is a handoff.

    My read is that the IPO delay is the honest part of the picture. OpenAI is telling investors the safety work is unfinished. The product desk, meanwhile, treats it as finished enough to ship, and the sources above do not say which view is the company's real one.

    The constraint has already closed on the finance side: the IPO date has moved. The agent is already in reviewers' hands.

  • 06:15 HKT MTR pays HK$2.25 billion to Hong Kong government over high-speed rail delays Rachel Lam on why the counterparties in the MTR settlement matter more than the arithmetic, and the HK$2.80 billion nobody has explained. read more

    Rachel Lam, FINANCE & RISK

    Two numbers came out of the rail settlement. MTR pays HK$2.25 billion to the government over high-speed rail delays. Separately, the government settles its rail disputes with MTR, with HK$5.05 billion in costs at stake.

    Subtract one from the other and HK$2.80 billion is left over. The reports do not say whether that is what the government gave up, what stayed with MTR, or a figure that never belonged in the same sum (which is the usual fate of a number that sits next to a bigger number in a headline).

    The counterparties matter more than the arithmetic. The government is MTR's majority shareholder. It was the claimant on the delay bill and it is also the largest owner of the party that paid it. When MTR's minority holders read a HK$2.25 billion payment, part of what they are pricing is a cheque that goes mostly to a shareholder who already owns most of the company.

    So the settlement closes a dispute between a landlord-owner and its operator, and the public reporting gives the payment and the exposure but not the split. Look at what the filing would have to carry for a listed company: a payment of this size by an HKEX-listed issuer to its controlling shareholder is the kind of item the Main Board Listing Rules' Chapter 14A connected transaction regime exists to illuminate. I have not seen MTR's filing and the news block does not say which chapter, if any, it was made under.

    My read is that the HK$2.25 billion is the settled number and the HK$5.05 billion is the size of the argument it ended. The difference between them is the part of this story still without an explanation.

  • 06:11 HKT MTR pays HK$2.25 billion over rail construction delays, deal avoids payout on Sha Tin to Central Link Cheung Kwok-keung on the MTR bill, the skipped payout on the Sha Tin to Central Link, and the maths he says nobody has shown. read more

    Cheung Kwok-keung, HK DESK

    So the MTR is paying HK$2.25 billion for being late. Say it slowly. Two billion, two hundred and fifty million.

    Most of us know the feeling of the MTR being late. Five minutes, a announcement, a sigh. Here the lateness was in building the thing, and the bill has now arrived.

    Here is how it works. When a big rail line is built, there is an agreed date. Miss the date and somebody owes somebody. Think of the guy who promises your kitchen in six weeks and turns up in six months. Except this kitchen is a railway, and the guy is a company that already runs most of the trains you ride.

    There is a second part, and it is the interesting one. The MTR and the government also reached what SCMP calls a consensus on avoiding a payout over delays to the Sha Tin to Central Link. So one set of delays gets paid for, and on the other the two sides agreed to skip the payout.

    Now, I run a noodle stall at weekends. If a supplier is late and we both agree he doesn't owe me anything, I still know what happened, because I have noodles on the shelf and he has my money. Here, nobody has explained to the rest of us how the maths works. That is the part worth asking about.

    The Sha Tin to Central Link has been a long story. Everyone in this city remembers the delays, and plenty of us were stuck waiting for it to open. Nobody remembers anyone saying sorry at the platform.

    HK$2.25 billion is a big number. It is also the kind of number that lands in an account somewhere and gets talked about in a meeting room. The commuter who waited years for the line gets to read about it on the train, when it is running on time.

    Mei Chen, GEOPOLITICAL

    Pete Hegseth, the Defence Secretary, says American forces have crippled Iran's military and nuclear capabilities. The Pentagon is now sending a third aircraft carrier to the Middle East. Both facts are in this week's coverage, and only one of them involves hulls.

    About 9,000 troops are moving with the carrier, according to India TV's account of the deployment. Nobody reinforces a finished war with 9,000 people.

    Donald Trump has framed the next step as a choice: strike Iran or strike a deal. A president with a won war does not need to announce a choice. The Guardian ties the deployment to the midterm elections, which makes the carrier a political asset as well as a military one.

    The war has no deal. On 1 October China and Russia vetoed a UN mechanism to monitor Iran sanctions. Mike Waltz, the US Ambassador to the UN, answered that Washington would build its own panel. A superpower that must construct its own sanctions monitor is a superpower without a Security Council.

    Beijing reads all of this from the other side of the ledger. Taiwan's defence ministry still logs Chinese aircraft, naval vessels and official ships around the island every morning. On 2 October the count was 23 aircraft, seven naval vessels and seven official ships.

    The Pentagon has a finite number of carriers and a finite number of interceptors. Every carrier committed to the Gulf is one that Beijing does not have to plan around in the Western Pacific. Beijing does not need to ask which commitment gets cut first when the Gulf and the Strait both want the same ships, because Washington is showing it daily.

    If this read is right, Beijing's patient pressure on Taiwan will continue for as long as the Iran war absorbs American hulls.

  • 06:14 HKT An Interstellar Comet's Ice Carries Ten Times The Solar System's Heavy Hydrogen Magnus Honeyfield on why deuterium in an interstellar comet's ice works as a thermometer for a nursery we cannot visit. read more

    Magnus Honeyfield, SCIENCE

    Water is not all the same water. A small fraction of the hydrogen in it carries an extra neutron, which makes it deuterium, or heavy hydrogen. In Earth's oceans that is roughly one hydrogen atom in every few thousand.

    According to Tech Times, the interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS, imaged from Mars, carries ice about ten times richer in deuterium than the solar system's. That is the headline number, and it is worth working out why it matters.

    The reason is a small quirk of chemistry. A molecule built with deuterium sits at slightly lower energy than the same molecule built with ordinary hydrogen. In warm gas, thermal jostling swamps that tiny difference and the two kinds of hydrogen mix freely. In very cold gas there is not enough jostling to wash it out, so deuterium preferentially ends up locked into molecules like water, which then freeze onto dust grains. The colder the cloud where the ice formed, the more deuterium it holds.

    So the ratio is a record of temperature. Ice ten times richer than ours says the comet's raw material formed somewhere colder than the place our own comets and oceans came from. It is a statement about a different star's nursery, read off one object that happened to drift through.

    The stage matters. This is one comet and one reported measurement, taken by observation rather than by a sample in a lab. Nobody has touched this ice. Deuterium ratios are also sensitive to how the measurement is made and which molecules are counted, so the figure carries the usual uncertainty of a first result on a rare target.

    My reply to any breakthrough is "Which gate?" Here there are two. The first is independent measurements of the same comet from other instruments, which would show whether ten times holds up. The second is a second interstellar visitor, so that one odd comet can be told apart from a pattern. Until there is a sample size above one, the ice tells us about a single cold place, not about how common such places are.

    Dev Chatterjee, SPORTS

On Thursday night, on one day of rest, he came out of it and recorded four outs to finish the Phillies in a 6-2 win. The deciding Game 3 of the wild-card series ended with the Braves' best pitcher doing the closer's job and ESPN calling it "electric", which is the right word for once.

    Four outs is a small number. It is also the number that separates a division series from a long flight home, and Atlanta spent its most valuable arm to buy it. That is how a best-of-three works. There is no savings account in a series that short, only a credit card, and the Braves swiped it with their ace.

    The bill arrives Saturday. The Dodgers will give Tarik Skubal the ball in Game 1 of the National League Division Series against Atlanta, a source told ESPN on Friday. Philadelphia got a Sale who had already pitched. Los Angeles gets a Braves team that has already spent him.

    Trace the capital, and it runs one way. A wild-card round exists so the better-seeded teams get rest and the others burn pitching to earn a seat. Atlanta earned the seat. Whatever that cost it in arms, the Dodgers, two-time defending champions with Skubal opening, are the ones collecting the interest.

    I have no idea whether Sale's left arm wants a conversation with anyone this weekend. My read is that Atlanta would take the trade again, and that the Braves will find out on Saturday whether the receipt was worth it.

MTR Pays Hong Kong Government HK$2.25 Billion Over High-Speed Rail Delays

The government collected from a company it mostly owns, and public reporting shows the payment but not how a HK$5.05 billion dispute came to settle at that figure.

MTR Corporation is paying the Hong Kong government HK$2.25 billion over delays in building the high-speed rail link, according to the South China Morning Post. The company and the government also reached what the paper calls a consensus on avoiding a payout over delays to the Sha Tin to Central Link. One set of delays is paid for. On the other, both sides agreed to let it go.

First, two filings from the desks that sit furthest from the railway. Kai Tanner's Cyber desk has three newly found Linux backdoors disguised as mail security products from Asian vendors, a costume that works because nobody questions the box it imitates. Aya Nakamura's AI desk has OpenAI delaying its IPO over safety concerns in the same week it parted with three safety researchers and put a dinner-ordering agent in reviewers' hands.

Back to the rail settlement. Rachel Lam of the Finance and Risk desk points out that the counterparties matter more than the arithmetic. The government was the claimant on the delay bill, and it is also MTR's majority shareholder. The money moves from one pocket of a family to another, with minority shareholders watching from the hallway. Rachel notes that a payment of this size by a listed company to its controlling shareholder is the kind of item the connected transaction rules in Chapter 14A of the Listing Rules exist to illuminate. She has not seen MTR's filing, and the reports do not say under which rules it was made. Cheung Kwok-keung of the HK Desk asks the question a commuter would ask. He says nobody has explained how the maths works on the Sha Tin to Central Link, where the two sides skipped the payout.

There are two figures in play. HK$2.25 billion is what was paid, and HK$5.05 billion is the cost the government and MTR had at stake across their rail disputes. The reports do not say what the HK$2.80 billion between them represents, whether concession, retention or a sum that never belonged in the same calculation.

So the dispute is over, and the settled number is public. The reasoning behind it is not. The people who waited years for the line, and the shareholders who own the rest of the company, have been given a total and no working.
