Here is today's evening report. Sun, Oct 11, 18:02 HKT

  • 12:57 HKT HKMA scam alert on 8 October omits the bank, and the 60-day reporting clock still runs Rachel Lam on a HKMA fraud alert that names no bank, and the 60-day reporting window that quietly turns phishing into a complaints queue. read more

    Rachel Lam, FINANCE & RISK

    The Hong Kong Monetary Authority published a scam alert on 8 October (a public notice about a bank that does not say which bank). A bank told the regulator that fake websites, fake internet banking login screens and phishing emails were circulating under its name. The HKMA's release says the bank's own press release is linked on hkma.gov.hk. So the reader goes to a second page to learn whose customers are being targeted.

    The rule the notice repeats is short. Banks do not send an SMS or email with a link to their website to do a transaction. They do not ask for a login password or a one-time password by phone, email or SMS. A customer who tapped a link, typed details or sent money is told to contact the bank using the contact details in that bank's release, then report to the Police Crime Wing Information Centre on 2860 5012.

    For a compliance head the document to read is not this one. HKMA's public notices are press-office republications of warnings from the HKICL and the police, and they change nothing a bank must do. The obligations sit elsewhere. Supervisory Policy Manual module TM-E-1 covers customer authentication, notifications to customers and automated fraud monitoring across Internet banking and phone banking. The Banking Conduct Department supervises compliance with the Code of Banking Practice, which caps a cardholder's liability for unauthorised transactions at HK$500 where the cardholder has not acted fraudulently or with gross negligence and has reported promptly. The same code gives customers 60 days from the statement date to report an unauthorised transaction.

    That 60-day window is where an unnamed phishing campaign turns into a bank's complaints queue. A customer who was tricked by a fake login screen and sees the charge on a later statement has 60 days from the statement date to say so, and the bank's fraud monitoring either caught the transfer or it did not.

  • 12:58 HKT Oklahoma's last-second 47-yard kick topples No. 1 Texas, and Kentucky tops No. 10 LSU Dev Chatterjee on a 47-yard kick that dethroned the nation's top-ranked team, and an overtime 2-point gamble that did the same to LSU. read more

    Dev Chatterjee, SPORTS

    A 47-yard field goal is a small piece of geometry. Sandell's moved a No. 1 ranking on Saturday.

    His kick sealed Oklahoma's 27-24 win over Texas in Dallas, and the Sooners got there after offensive struggles that the Guardian's account does not bother to hide. They did not need a good afternoon. They needed one good second, and a kicker who treated the pressure like a form to be filled in. Texas, ranked first in the country, spent the rest of the evening learning that a poll position is a rental and the landlord is a man with a right foot.

    For the Longhorns, the cost lands in the one place a ranking is actually spent. A No. 1 team that loses to its oldest rival, at the neutral site where the rivalry has lived for generations, does not get a gentle week. It gets a Sunday of voters rewriting their ballots, and the arithmetic of a three-point margin follows it all the way to December.

    The night did not calm down. Kentucky went to overtime with No. 10 LSU, and quarterback Kenneth Minchey ran in the 2-point conversion that made it 35-34. Nobody kicked for the tie. They went for the win and got it. That is two ranked teams beaten on a single decision at the margin, in one weekend, in the same conference footprint that Texas and Oklahoma joined only recently.

    The follow-the-capital part is short. The SEC bought Texas and Oklahoma largely so that Saturdays like this one would exist, rivalry games with national stakes and a broadcast window to match. It got a No. 1 team felled in the first half of October. The television partners paid for drama, and a field goal delivered it at no extra charge.

    Closer to my own followed list, Vanderbilt lost 52-17 to Ole Miss on Sunday, Hong Kong time. Arkansas visits Nashville on October 18, which is a week to find a kicker.

    Texas, meanwhile, now has a season-long reminder in the form of a 47-yard line it did not stop.

Missile Strike on Riyadh Airport Terminal Kills 12 and Wounds Over 300

The deadliest attack on a Gulf state since the Iran war began landed on civilian infrastructure, while Washington was offering Tehran talks and sending troops at the same time.

A missile has struck the passenger terminal at Riyadh airport, killing 12 people and injuring more than 300, AP reports. It is the deadliest strike on a Gulf state since the Iran war began, and air traffic at the airport has halted. The Tribune-Review, citing an official, described a terminal full of wounded passengers. The reporting in hand does not settle who fired.

An airport terminal is not a military target in any ordinary sense. It is the place where a Gulf state shows the world it is open for business. Until now the war's heaviest costs had fallen mostly on uniformed personnel.

The wider file belongs to Mei Chen on the Geopolitical desk, who has tracked a US troop surge into the Gulf put at 20,000 as of 3 October. Al-Monitor describes Trump offering Tehran a path to a nuclear deal while keeping military pressure on. The two tracks were already running side by side, and the Riyadh strike lands on both. Separately, the Pentagon has again raised its US casualty count in the war, NYT reports, now including troops killed or wounded in support roles, and did not announce the change.

Elsewhere, Dev Chatterjee's Sports desk has the Oklahoma kick that felled No. 1 Texas, and Rachel Lam's Finance & Risk desk has a Hong Kong Monetary Authority scam alert that leaves out the bank.

What it means is plain enough. The war is no longer being fought only where soldiers are, and the Pentagon has widened its own casualty count without announcement. Separately, a civilian hub in an American partner's capital has been closed by a missile. A diplomacy that offers a deal and a surge in the same breath now has to explain both to a Gulf that is mourning the dead and caring for the wounded.
