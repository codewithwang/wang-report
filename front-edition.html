  • 06:08 HKT SonicWall SMA1000 flaw CVE-2026-102255 exploited three days after patch Kai Tanner on why a Tuesday patch and a Friday attack make the weekly change window the weak point. Start here. read more

    Kai Tanner, CYBER INTEL

    The patch for CVE-2026-102255 shipped on Tuesday. By Friday, BleepingComputer reported that attackers were already exploiting it on SonicWall SMA1000 appliances, a flaw carrying the maximum severity rating.

    Three days. For most organisations that is not enough time to finish a change request.

    An SMA1000 sits at the edge, which is where remote staff and contractors authenticate. A bug rated at the top of the scale on a device built to face the internet is the combination that change-advisory boards exist to rush, and rarely do. The usual sequence is a Tuesday advisory, a Thursday test cycle, a Sunday maintenance window and a Monday ticket closure. The attackers did not wait for the ticket.

    We covered Fortinet's FortiMail flaw, CVE-2026-104286, on 2 October, where the vendor said code was already running on customer systems before anyone had a fix. That one gave defenders no head start. This one gave them one, and the head start lasted until Friday.

    By our count, four flaws on our tracked list entered CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue in the seven days to 9 October, out of 250 we follow. The SonicWall bug is a reminder of the other kind of risk: a fix already published, and the exposure sitting in the gap before it is applied.

    The check takes minutes. Pull the firmware version from every SMA1000 in the estate, compare it against Tuesday's release, and read the appliance's authentication and admin logs back to Tuesday for anything that does not belong to a person you can name. Where the box cannot be patched this weekend, taking its management interface off the internet is the control that changes the outcome. A weekly window cannot.

  • 06:07 HKT One Stanley developer offers six-year structural warranty and free inspections amid steel bar probe Cheung Kwok-keung on what a six-year warranty and a developer-paid inspection really promise One Stanley owners while the probe stays open. read more

    Cheung Kwok-keung, HK DESK

    Picture a One Stanley owner this weekend. You paid for the flat. Now the news says there is an investigation into suspected steel reinforcement problems in the building.

    Then the developer rings with good news. Six-year structural warranty. Free independent inspections. Lovely.

    Let me walk you through what that actually is. A warranty is a promise to fix something later. The developer is offering that promise before anyone has said publicly what, if anything, needs fixing. It is like a restaurant handing you a voucher for the stomach pills while the kitchen is still being checked.

    The free inspection is the better part. Someone independent looks at your flat. Fine, good, do it. But think about who is paying for it. The developer. Hong Kong buyers have heard "independent" before, and they will want to know who picked the inspector.

    Here is the number to repeat at lunch: six years. That is how long the promise runs. A flat is the biggest thing most families here will ever buy, and it comes with a mortgage that runs a lot longer than that.

    The timing matters too. The CK Asset building fiasco has people calling for a tougher inspection regime. So the city is asking who was checking the steel in the first place. The developer's answer is to offer more checking, afterwards.

    Honestly, I would take it. If the developer wants to pay for a stranger with a torch to look at my walls, I say yes and I keep the paperwork. If you live there, put the offer in writing and keep every page.

    The probe is still open. The warranty clock and the investigation are now running at the same time.

  • 06:12 HKT Warner's film chiefs are out under Skydance as Zaslav leaves with $606 million Joy Lee on Skydance closing its merger, the Warner film chiefs heading out, and the very large cheque Zaslav takes with him. read more

    Joy Lee, LIFE

    There have been many quiet Mondays at the Burbank offices of Warner Bros. Pictures, Variety notes, usually after a bomb. This one followed Tom Cruise's "Digger," a cryptic eco-disaster that the studio has just finished absorbing, and it arrived with new owners.

    The deal closed October 6. Skydance now holds Warner Bros., Paramount Pictures, HBO, CBS, CNN and TNT Sports under one roof. A roof like that needs a lot of chairs, and the first ones pulled out belong to the Warner film chiefs, who are out under Skydance.

    Read the org chart, not the press kit. Skydance's new TV leadership, per Deadline, is built from Co-Chairs only, each sharing a division with a partner, and all the pairs come from the same side. Two executives per job is a merger's way of saying the other side of the deal has not been fully forgiven yet. It also means one more name on every approval email.

    Meanwhile, David Zaslav walks away with $606 million, according to Yahoo Finance. A man leaves a company he ran into a sale holding a number like that. The people who get to watch "Digger" land on their performance reviews are, by comparison, on salary.

    Zoom out to the customer, who is also tired. Investor's Business Daily reports that US consumers are growing dissatisfied with streaming services amid steady price hikes, and that the hikes are testing the limits of what people will spend. The industry's answer to subscribers running out of patience has been to raise prices and then consolidate the people who set them.

    Netflix, which has no merger to blame, is reportedly planning to cut about 5% of its staff as soon as next week. Streaming's growth years rewarded everyone, and the repricing years are sending out invoices.

    My read: the merger is celebrated as scale, but scale is also a bigger place to lay people off from. Expect the Warner lot to get quieter on more days than Monday, and expect Zaslav's number to be the one everyone remembers.

    Dev Chatterjee, SPORTS

    Brian Schottenheimer stood in his own building on Friday night and watched a team with no wins take it away from him.

    Tampa Bay came to Dallas 0 and whatever the opposite of momentum is. It left with a 24-16 victory, the Buccaneers' first of the season, and the Cowboys went into the weekend with a result that no coach survives explaining.

    The play that did the damage was Bucky Irving's 72-yard run, which turned a game Dallas was managing into one Dallas was chasing. Irving finished with two touchdowns. Rookie Jalon Daniels led the comeback, which is the sort of sentence a head coach reads once, quietly, and then does not read again.

    Outkick put it plainly: Schottenheimer sits on the hottest seat in the NFL. A seat gets that label by losing to the teams it is supposed to beat, and a winless team in your own stadium is the purest version of the test. There is no scheme excuse and no injury alibi in a loss to a team that had not yet found a way to win.

    Dallas is the most valuable franchise in American sport, and it sells that value on the promise that Sundays, and the odd Friday, end the other way. Owners who carry that kind of number do not usually carry patience with it. A coaching change in Dallas is not a football decision so much as a brand-maintenance one, and the Cowboys have rarely let a bad week sit unreviewed.

    The schedule offers no soft landing. Dallas travels to Green Bay on 2026-10-19, to face a Packers team that Atlanta beat in their own home opener last month. If the Cowboys lose there, the conversation about Schottenheimer stops being a column and starts being a deadline.

Attackers Exploit Maximum-Severity SonicWall SMA1000 Flaw Three Days After Patch

SonicWall shipped the fix on Tuesday and attackers were using the flaw by Friday, so the risk now sits in the time it takes organisations to apply a patch.

A maximum-severity flaw in SonicWall's SMA1000 remote access appliances, tracked as CVE-2026-102255, is now being exploited in attacks, BleepingComputer reports. The patch shipped on Tuesday. By Friday, attackers were already using the bug against devices that had not yet been updated.

Kai Tanner's desk has the detail. An SMA1000 sits at the edge of a company's network, the place where remote staff and contractors sign in, so a top-rated bug on a device built to face the internet is about as valuable as a flaw gets. Three days is not long enough to finish most change requests. It was evidently plenty for the other side.

The contrast with Kai Tanner's earlier coverage is useful. The Fortinet FortiMail flaw, CVE-2026-104286, which the desk covered on 2 October, was being exploited before any fix existed, so defenders had no head start at all. This time they had one, and it ran out on Friday. By the desk's count, four flaws on its tracked list entered CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue in the seven days to 9 October, out of 250 followed.

Elsewhere, Cheung Kwok-keung of the HK Desk has the One Stanley developer's six-year structural warranty and free inspections, offered while the steel bar investigation stays open. Joy Lee has the Warner film chiefs leaving under Skydance, and David Zaslav departing with $606 million. Dev Chatterjee has the winless Buccaneers beating the Cowboys 24-16 in Dallas.

Back to SonicWall, the point is plain. Publishing a fix does not close a vulnerability. It starts a race between the people who must test and schedule the update and the people who can read the advisory and build an attack from it. Once the patch is public, the flaw is public too, and the weekly maintenance window is the stretch of time in which a company is exposed.
