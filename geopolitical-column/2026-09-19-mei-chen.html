Trump Calls Taiwan's Weapons 'Chips To Play'

Trump's own description of Taiwan's arms shipments as bargaining chips is the real Taiwan policy this week, not whatever he and Xi say for cameras on September 24.
陳 美 · Mei Chen Geopolitical Desk, Senior Correspondent Sunday, September 20, 2026

The Word Trump Chose

Donald Trump called Taiwan's own American-made weapons 'chips to play' in his negotiation with Xi Jinping, in a September 14 interview with Foreign Policy conducted ten days before the two leaders sit down at the White House. He added that 'the last thing we need right now is a war that's 9,500 miles away.' Read plainly, that is not a slip. It is Trump naming the terms he intends to trade on.

The framing has a history. At their first 2026 summit, held in Beijing on May 14, Xi Jinping told Trump that differences over Taiwan could lead to 'conflict.' Trump did not answer that warning with a deterrence pledge. He answered it four months later by describing the very arms packages meant to deter China as inventory he can move. Washington's decades-old vagueness over whether it would intervene militarily for Taiwan, the policy still called strategic ambiguity, was built to keep Beijing guessing. Trump's sentence removes the guessing. It tells Beijing that the arms shipments are the lever in Washington that actually turns.

Colby Digs In

Elbridge Colby, the Pentagon's policy chief, said the department is 'digging in' to hold a credible deterrent posture along the first island chain, the line of Japanese, Taiwanese and Philippine territory that Chinese naval planners must cross to reach the open Pacific. He said it the same week Trump was calling Taiwan's weapons chips. The timing was not an accident of scheduling. It was the Pentagon's policy shop stating, in public, the position Trump's own instincts keep undercutting.

Colby cannot contradict his president in a press release, so he does not try. He restates the deterrence mission and lets the contrast do the work. His statement commits the Pentagon to a deterrence posture; it commits nothing about the arms pipeline that posture depends on. A posture statement backed by delayed hardware is not deterrence. It is a talking point. The next scheduled Taiwan arms delivery, not Colby's phrasing or Trump's, will settle whose Taiwan policy the Pentagon is actually running.

Pressure Before The Summit

Two incidents this month read as pressure tests timed to the summit calendar, not as unrelated crises. On September 8, at the Seoul Defense Dialogue, a Chinese military attache handed Philippine Defense Secretary Gilberto Teodoro a note mid-speech. Teodoro read it aloud to the room and called Beijing's conduct 'coercion, bullying and aggression.' Ten days later, a China Coast Guard cutter rammed the Philippine Bureau of Fisheries vessel Magat Salamat roughly 54 nautical miles off Palawan, inside Manila's own exclusive economic zone, tearing a hole in its side. No one on board was hurt. The ramming became, this month, the incident that shapes what Xi Jinping brings to Washington.

The same day, Taiwan ran its first exercise combining attack drones with anti-air, anti-ship and anti-landing missile fire. President Lai Ching-te called it proof of 'adaptation to modern warfare.' Beijing did not need to read that drill as a threat. It needed only to clock that Taiwan is still building the deterrent Trump is describing as a chip.

Nothing is decided until Trump and Xi sit down on September 24. What decides it will not be a joint statement. It will be whether the next scheduled shipment to Taiwan ships on time. A delay or bundling confirms that Trump's chips have already been spent. No change confirms that Colby's Pentagon is writing the policy Trump only describes for cameras, until that next shipment date arrives and settles the question.
