Xi's Military Command Down To Two

Beijing dismissed its second-highest military officer this week, leaving Xi Jinping's command body with two members before his 2027 deadline, and the field record argues against reading it as retreat.
陳 美 · Mei Chen Geopolitical Desk, Senior Correspondent Sunday, August 30, 2026

The Committee Of Two

China's National People's Congress Standing Committee dismissed General Zhang Youxia as vice chairman of the Central Military Commission on August 28, along with CMC member General Liu Zhenli, and stripped both of their seats as NPC deputies. Two more former officers, Zhong Shaojun and Ju Qiansheng, lost their deputy status in the same session. Beijing's Defense Ministry gave the reason it gave in January, when it first announced the investigation: both men were suspected of 'serious violations of discipline and the law.' Zhang Youxia was not a rival Xi tolerated. He was a Politburo member and Xi's de facto second-in-command on the CMC since 2017, the general who outranked everyone in uniform except Xi himself. Removing him does not trim the command structure. It guts it. The CMC now holds two active members: Xi Jinping and Zhang Shengmin, the commission's discipline chief. Seven seats built for collective military command now hold two men, with eighteen months left before the 2027 deadline Xi set for the PLA to finish modernizing.

The Field Didn't Pause

The purge took seven months to finish, from the January 24 announcement of the investigation to the August 28 dismissal. Nothing on the ground waited for it. Commercial satellite imagery reviewed by Reuters and captured on August 17 showed China had built out roughly 1,490 acres of reclaimed land at Antelope Reef in the Paracels, on pace to become the largest artificial feature in the South China Sea. Vietnam's foreign ministry spokesperson Pham Thu Hang demanded on August 28, the same day the CMC dismissals were announced, that China 'immediately end' the construction. Beijing did not pause for that either. The same week, thousands of Taiwanese troops on the Penghu Islands ran drills rehearsing the country's response to a Chinese first strike, part of the Han Kuang-linked exercises. A military commission can lose five of seven seats and a construction crew keeps pouring concrete on a reef far away.

Two Readings, No Verdict

A two-member commission has produced two incompatible readings, and they cannot both be right. The Australian Strategic Policy Institute's read is that Xi doubts his own military's readiness enough to gut its top command eighteen months before a deadline he set himself, which argues for less appetite for near-term action against Taiwan, not more. A second camp, closer to Jamestown Foundation analysis, reads the same event as consolidation: replace independent generals with loyalists, shorten the chain from Xi's desk to the field, and the risk is not restraint but miscalculation, a command structure with fewer people positioned to tell Xi no. Both readings assume the purge changes the PLA's near-term behavior. The reclamation crews at Antelope Reef and the troops drilling on Penghu did not get that memo, or if they did, they ignored it. This desk has argued that Washington's deterrence timeline and Taipei's budget politics run on different clocks. Add a third: Beijing's personnel clock and Beijing's operational clock are not synchronized either, and the next fixed point that could resolve which one is running ahead is the 2027 PLA modernization deadline Xi set himself.

Taiwan's defense ministry and US Indo-Pacific Command now plan against a Central Military Commission with two working members and an undisclosed bench behind them, without knowing whether the men who fill the empty seats will be more cautious or more compliant. The reclamation at Antelope Reef will keep advancing regardless of who sits on the commission. Taipei's defense ministry has already answered the practical question with the Han Kuang drills; Beijing's CMC appointments before 2027 will answer the rest.
