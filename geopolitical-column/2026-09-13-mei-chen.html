Beijing Prices Trump's Summit At $14 Billion

China's threat to cancel Xi's September 24 White House visit over a $14 billion Taiwan arms sale turns Taipei's procurement calendar into Washington's summit currency.
陳 美 · Mei Chen Geopolitical Desk, Senior Correspondent Sunday, September 13, 2026

The Frozen Package

Taiwan's Ministry of National Defense has spent four months waiting on a $14 billion arms package that Washington will not confirm and will not kill. The package includes F-16 fighter parts and munitions Taipei requested well before this year. It has sat since May, and this week Beijing gave Washington a fresh reason to leave it there: China told US officials it could cancel Xi Jinping's September 24 visit to the White House, his third face-to-face meeting with Trump in a year, if the sale clears before then, the Taipei Times reported. For the maintenance crews at Taiwan's air bases who have logged those parts as overdue since spring, the message is specific and unflattering. Their resupply schedule is now a bargaining chip in somebody else's diplomatic calendar. Taiwan's defense ministry was not in the room when that trade got made, and it has no vote on the date either.

May's Dress Rehearsal

This is not the first time the package has been held hostage to a summit. Ahead of Trump's May 14 trip to Beijing, his administration paused public announcement of the same $14 billion sale, reportedly to keep Xi's welcome from souring, the Washington Post reported on May 23. CNN had already flagged Taiwan's anxiety over the sale ten days before that summit happened at all.

At the meeting itself, Xi told Trump that Taiwan was 'the most important issue' in the US-China relationship and warned that mishandling it could 'trigger a conflict,' according to the Taipei Times. Trump got his summit. The package stayed parked. Four months later the same mechanism is running again: a Chinese threat, a pending Taiwan sale, a Trump summit on the calendar. May 14 was the first date the package was held against; September 24 is the second. What May showed, and September is repeating, is that the diplomatic calendar can reset both dates to zero whenever Trump wants a photograph with Xi.

The Hold Nobody Will Name

Secretary of State Marco Rubio, who has run Taiwan policy at the State Department since Trump's second term began in January 2025, now calls the package 'under review, not paused.' That is a careful phrase for a shipment that has already missed one summit and is on track to miss a second. Other reporting describes an active hold tied directly to Beijing's leverage over the September 24 date, and nobody in the administration has reconciled the two versions.

Whatever gets settled in Washington's East Room will not touch what China is already doing at sea. A study by the Asia Maritime Transparency Initiative found that Chinese coast guard vessels interdicted or questioned crews on nearly 200 commercial ships in the Philippine Sea between June and August, after five months of almost no presence there at all, a pattern the study reads as a rehearsal for a quarantine effort aimed at Taiwan. A calm September in Washington buys Xi a photograph. It does not buy Taiwan a quiet ocean.

Whether the $14 billion package clears before September 24 will answer whether Washington still arms Taiwan on its own schedule, or lets Beijing set the delivery date. If Trump shelves it again to protect the summit, Taipei will have learned that the F-16 parts and munitions it has waited on since spring move only when the White House needs a photograph that month. If it clears anyway, Beijing will have learned the threat was worth less than the summit.
