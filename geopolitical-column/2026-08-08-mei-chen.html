Beijing's Martyrs Were Killed By Beijing

Beijing waited eleven months to admit its coast guard killed two of its own sailors chasing a Philippine boat, then invoked the deaths to justify harsher tactics ahead.
陳 美 · Mei Chen Geopolitical Desk, Senior Correspondent Sunday, August 9, 2026

The Collision Beijing Buried

China's People's Armed Police confirmed on August 7 that two of its personnel died in a South China Sea collision last August, the first acknowledged Chinese fatalities from a South China Sea confrontation since 2001. The dead were not killed by the Philippines. They were killed by their own coast guard, chasing a Philippine Coast Guard boat so hard it rammed a Chinese navy destroyer. The cutter, hull CCG 3104, struck the Guilin, a Type 052D destroyer, while both vessels were pursuing the Philippine boat BRP Suluan near Scarborough Shoal. Yi Xinyu was 21. Cheng Long was 25. Both died on August 11, 2025, according to the Taipei Times and the South China Morning Post. Beijing held that fact for nearly a year, then released it as martyrdom: the People's Armed Police named the two men martyrs of a 'rights defense operation.' The operation was against the Philippine Coast Guard. The Philippine Coast Guard calls the outcome 'unfortunate' and blames Beijing's own high-speed maneuvering. Manila is not wrong about the physics. It is wrong to think the physics settle the politics.

A Martyr Narrative, Banked

Beijing surfaced the deaths on August 7, two days into Taiwan's Han Kuang exercises, the island's largest annual live-fire drills, running August 5 through 14 with a first-time test of relocating weapons production under simulated wartime conditions. President Lai Ching-te joined the 'Wan Chun' bunker evacuation drill for the first time as president on August 5. Beijing spent the same week ordering mandatory traffic control on ships crossing the southern Taiwan Strait during Typhoon Dolphin, an order Taipei called 'utterly ridiculous' and refused to recognize over international waters. Into that week, Beijing dropped a domestic casualty story that never mentions Taiwan at all. The coercion has already migrated from Taiwan's airspace to Philippine coast guard cutters in the South China Sea. Friday's disclosure gives Beijing a domestic narrative it can invoke for a harder chase, accurately or not, the next time a confrontation escalates. Philippine Coast Guard crews running resupply missions to Scarborough Shoal now do that job knowing Beijing holds a martyr story it can invoke for a harder chase.

What Beijing has not decided, publicly, is what invoking the deaths of Yi Xinyu and Cheng Long buys: a freer hand for coast guard cutters that chase Philippine boats, or a domestic pretext banked for a harder confrontation still to come. The next Scarborough resupply run will start to answer it. If a Chinese cutter escalates against the Philippine Coast Guard boats making that trip, Beijing already has a martyr story on file to justify it.
