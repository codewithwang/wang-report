The Rate Hike That Missed Hong Kong's Mortgages

The HKMA raised its Base Rate to track the Fed, but a wall of mainland liquidity had already set Hong Kong's real borrowing cost 138 basis points lower.
黎 國 楷 · Vincent Lai Geopolitical Desk, Occasional Contributor Sunday, September 20, 2026

The Formula Fires

The Hong Kong Monetary Authority raised its Base Rate 25 basis points to 4.25 percent on September 17, the mechanical output of a formula that has governed the peg since 1998: fifty basis points above the lower bound of the US federal funds target, reset the day the Federal Reserve moves. The Fed had moved the day before, voting 12-0 on September 16 to lift its target range to 3.75 to 4.00 percent, its first hike since 2023. The HKMA's desk did exactly what the formula requires, no discretion involved, no committee statement for the market to parse. HSBC and Bank of China (Hong Kong) then held their prime lending rate at 5.00 percent. Standard Chartered held its best lending rate at 5.25 percent. Three of the city's largest banks looked at the same headline number and treated it as background noise, or, more precisely, decided that the gap between what the peg formula says and what their treasury desks actually pay for money had grown too wide to close by matching the HKMA's move at all.

The Wall Behind HIBOR

One-month HIBOR fixed at 2.87 percent on September 18, about 138 basis points below the Base Rate the peg had just produced. That gap is not a lag, it is a liquidity pool, and the pool is mainland money. Southbound Stock Connect average daily turnover rose 154 percent year-on-year in the second quarter, and Hong Kong's IPO pipeline is on pace to raise roughly HK$380 billion this year, according to PwC, pushing settlement cash through the city's banks faster than the HKMA's Base Rate can be repriced into loan books. John Lee's new Five-Year Plan, unveiled the same day as the Fed's vote, proposes settling government expenditure in renminbi and deepening Hong Kong's role as the largest offshore RMB hub.

Who Feels The Gap

The gap lands unevenly. Mortgage-broker data shows 61 percent of newly approved Hong Kong mortgages in July referenced HIBOR, against 1.3 percent linked directly to prime, so the households carrying H-plan loans on flats in Sha Tin or Tseung Kwan O are paying a rate set by 2026's liquidity glut, not by the HKMA's headline hike. The banks that held prime steady are not passing the Fed's move to borrowers at all; they are banking it as margin, a wider spread between what depositors earn on HKD savings accounts and what the loan book brings in. The Hong Kong Monetary Authority's rate hike, in other words, now governs almost nothing except how much room HSBC's and Standard Chartered's treasury desks have to hold prime steady the next time the Fed moves. The households funding their mortgage payments off HIBOR and the depositors earning near nothing on cash absorb whichever side of the spread the banks decide not to close.

The HKMA's Base Rate still sets the peg's credibility, but it no longer sets the price of money in Hong Kong; the Southbound wall does that, as long as Southbound turnover and the IPO pipeline stay at current levels and the Fed holds. If either turns, HIBOR converges back toward the Base Rate quickly, and the households who felt nothing this month absorb the difference all at once. The peg held. The price of credit moved somewhere else.
