Beijing's Taiwan Arsenal Signal Meets a Different Ledger

China's Ministry of National Defense this week put a new nuclear-capable bomber on display and moved forward a total war mobilization law, both timed to the same message: that Beijing can outlast Washington's arms sale calendar. The Economist's war-gaming on September 15 modeled a drone-saturated Taiwan Strait scenario premised on exactly this kind of hardware signal, and prediction markets have already started pricing Chinese action against Taiwan's outlying islands rather than the main island itself, a distinction the PLA's own force posture around Kinmen and Matsu has made for years without needing a headline to explain it.

The mobilization law lands on a different desk than the one issuing the threats. Or, more precisely, the law is drafted by the State Council's legislative affairs office and the Central Military Commission jointly, and its practical effect is fiscal before it is martial: it authorizes local governments to requisition civilian shipping and logistics capacity, which is a budget line item at the Ministry of Finance long before it is a tank column. Premier Li's economic working group is the desk that has to reconcile that requisition authority against a mainland banking system already absorbing a 360 billion yuan rescue this month, and a war mobilization statute that lets provinces commandeer commercial assets is not a signal Beijing sends casually while its own insurers are being backstopped by the tobacco monopoly. The bomber gets the photograph. The mobilization law gets the balance sheet, and Li's working group has until the fiscal year-end reporting cycle to show whether requisition authority stayed on paper or started drawing against provincial accounts.
