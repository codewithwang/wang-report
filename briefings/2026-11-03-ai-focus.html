Pet portrait and workout apps hid tools for sexually abusive deepfakes
The Executive Brief, once a month, free. What it is

A pet portrait app could display pet portraits to one user and tools for making sexually abusive deepfakes to another. The developer’s server decided which version appeared. [2]

That switch sits at the centre of a network of more than 400 connected apps and domains investigated by WIRED. Researchers found ordinary-looking services, including language tuition and workout coaching, that could load a hidden web interface for generating explicit images from uploaded photographs. [2]

The app installed on the phone was only part of the product. Each download contacted a server with information including the user’s language and where they came from. The server then decided which content to display. Clicking an associated advertisement appeared to be one trigger for receiving the explicit version. [2]

Apple told WIRED that the apps complied with its rules when reviewed, and that developers subsequently replaced the website content the apps displayed with pornography. Approval of the app left the developer controlling what it showed. People whose photographs were uploaded faced an abusive service distributed through stores that had reviewed something else. [2]

By September, researchers counted more than 350,000 downloads across the network’s apps on Google Play. Advertisements on Facebook and Instagram supplied customers; Apple and Google payment systems allowed purchases. The image generator had a commercial delivery system around it. [2]

That distinction gives Google’s latest model-safety announcement a useful boundary. Ars Technica reported that Gemini 4 Carbon resisted every attempted prompt-injection attack in a cited evaluation. That result measures the model under those test conditions. It supplies no evidence that an app store can detect a developer switching the content users receive. [3] [1]

Researchers also found shared code listing connected domains and app details. That suggests enforcement has to account for the connected operation as well as each individual app. A removal count alone cannot establish how much of the service has been disabled. [2]

Apple removed 19 apps after WIRED contacted it. Google had already removed most of the identified apps before receiving the publication’s questions. Meta reported extensive enforcement against associated accounts and pages. [2]

As WIRED finished reporting, similar advertisements and apps were still being published. [2]
