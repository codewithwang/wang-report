Taiwan's Record Defence Budget Lands Beside PLA Cash-Seizure Drills

Taiwan's cabinet proposed a record NT$949.5 billion (roughly $35 billion) defence budget this week, the largest in the island's history, as PLA exercises rehearsed the wartime seizure of cash and financial assets from Taiwanese targets. The two events share a calendar, not a coincidence. A budget line is a statement of what Taipei believes it must survive; a drill is a statement of what Beijing believes it can inflict. The PLA's cash-seizure rehearsal, reported by the South China Morning Post, targets the mechanics of occupation, not deterrence, which is a different phase of planning than the missile drills Beijing ran in 2026's earlier quarters.

Taipei's defence ministry, under a cabinet that spent 2026 fighting the opposition-controlled legislature over every drone dollar, is no longer negotiating the number. It is setting it and daring the legislature to cut it during an active PLA rehearsal cycle. That sequencing matters more than the total. A record budget proposed beside a cash-seizure drill is Taipei pricing in a scenario the opposition spent months treating as rhetorical. The next test is whether the legislature, which restored every contested dollar once already this year, holds the full NT$949.5 billion through committee review before the fiscal year closes.

PBOC's Rate Path Now Waits on Washington, Not Beijing

The escalation calendar Vincent's desk-mate is tracking on Taiwan has a monetary counterpart most trading desks are not yet pricing correctly: the Strait of Hormuz. Washington's 60-day ceasefire deadline with Iran expired this week with no deal, and President Trump followed the lapse with a threat to bomb Oman and a demand for Iran's surrender, terms that raise the odds of a shipping interruption through the strait that carries roughly a fifth of global oil flow. The PBOC's open-market desk, or more precisely the currency and liquidity arm that sets the daily fixing band, has spent 2026 signaling that further easing depends on an external cost shock not materializing. An oil spike out of Hormuz is exactly that shock, and it now sits alongside the PLA's cash-seizure drills against Taiwan as a second calendar the mainland's monetary planners did not set and cannot control.

The mainland imports roughly 70% of its crude, and a large share still transits the strait despite Beijing's decade-long effort to diversify supply through pipelines from Russia and Central Asia. A sustained price shock forces Premier Li's economic working group to choose between absorbing imported inflation through the currency, which the PBOC has resisted since March, or loosening liquidity into a shock the fixing band was not built to buffer. The desk that matters here is not Beijing's alone: it is now reading Muscat and Tehran with the same attention it gives its own November fixing.
