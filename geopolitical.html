Taiwan Runs Invasion Drills as Iran Fights the Strait

Taipei emptied streets and sounded air-raid sirens across the island Friday, running its annual Wanan civil defense exercise alongside live invasion-response drills that simulated a PLA landing. The Ministry of National Defense, under Wellington Koo since 2024, timed the exercise to coincide with the sharpest week of Middle East escalation in months: Iran's forces moved on the Strait of Hormuz, the United Arab Emirates said Friday its state oil tankers took drone fire transiting the strait, and Washington ordered a second carrier group into the region. Beijing's Taiwan Affairs Office did not need to fly a single additional sortie to make its point. The point made itself: the United States has one Navy, and this week it is spoken for.

That is the arithmetic Taipei is drilling against, not a hypothetical. A carrier ordered to the Gulf is a carrier not staged in the Western Pacific, and Washington has not named what force posture it would hold in reserve for a Taiwan contingency arriving in the same month as an Iran contingency. Koo's ministry has run Wanan every year since the 1970s; what changed this year is the audience. The drill was scheduled before the Hormuz attacks, and Beijing now watches Taipei rehearse an invasion response while Washington's second carrier steams toward the Gulf instead of the Taiwan Strait. Track whether the carrier ordered to Hormuz this week returns to the Indo-Pacific before October, when Taiwan's next reservist call-up cycle begins.

Iran's Hormuz Move Puts Asian Refiners on the PBOC's Desk

Iran's seizure of shipping control in the Strait of Hormuz, following Friday's drone attacks on two UAE state tanker vessels, lands first not on a chart of oil futures but on the settlement desks of the mainland refiners who import roughly 1.4 million barrels a day of Iranian and Gulf-adjacent crude through discounted, sanctions-evading channels that PBOC-linked clearing has quietly serviced for three years. Call it the ledger before the headline: Sinopec's and PetroChina's trading arms have built procurement around Hormuz transit assumptions that Friday's drone strikes on the two Emirati tankers now put in question, and the State Administration of Foreign Exchange, or more precisely the desk within SAFE that clears energy-linked yuan payments to Tehran-adjacent intermediaries, has to reprice a war-risk premium it was not carrying a week ago.

The Ministry of Commerce has said nothing public, which is itself the signal, because Beijing's default posture on a Hormuz disruption is silence until the refiners' hedging books force a statement, not before. Washington's decision to route a second carrier group toward the Gulf, reported by the Wall Street Journal Friday, pulls naval attention away from the Western Pacific at the same moment Taipei is running invasion-response drills, but the more immediate mainland exposure sits in Shanghai, where the Shanghai International Energy Exchange prices its crude futures contract off a Middle East delivery basket that assumes Hormuz stays open. Watch whether SAFE widens the war-risk spread on energy settlement before the September contract rolls.
