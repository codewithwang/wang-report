Taipei Runs Its Largest War Drill With Abrams Tanks

Taiwan's Han Kuang exercises this week deploy US-made Abrams tanks for the first time in the drill's history, alongside reserve mobilization and urban resilience testing across the island. The exercise is the largest Han Kuang since the drill's creation, and it runs concurrent with a Taiwan defense ministry tally of 21 Chinese warplanes and 14 warships operating near the island, a presence count the ministry now publishes daily. The Abrams arrived under a 2019 sale approved in President Tsai Ing-wen's first term; their appearance in reserve-mobilization drills, not just live-fire ranges, is the tell. Tanks in a reservist call-up scenario are not a deterrence photo op. They are an inventory check for a mobilization Taipei's Ministry of National Defense now rehearses as a near-term contingency, not a hypothetical one.

Beijing's answer sits in a different weapons class. The DF-17, a hypersonic glide vehicle mated to a ballistic booster, is nearing operational readiness, according to reporting this week, and it renders the Abrams' battlefield utility beside the point. A tank division does not intercept a glide vehicle that separates from its booster mid-flight and maneuvers through the atmosphere. Taipei is drilling for the invasion that follows a landing; Beijing is fielding the weapon that would decide whether a landing force ever needs cover. The mismatch is the story Han Kuang's own footage does not show, and it is the reason the exercise reads less as deterrence than as triage: hardening what a ground force can reach, because the missile force is not Taiwan's to counter.

Han Kuang Drills Test a Reserve Call-Up China's Bond Market Prices Separately

The Ministry of National Defense's decision to run reservist mobilization alongside the Abrams tanks this week, rather than confine the new hardware to live-fire ranges, is a signal read in Taipei as inventory management for a contingency now treated as near-term. The People's Bank of China's open-market desk reads a different instrument entirely. Through the second week of the Han Kuang exercises, the PBOC's seven-day reverse repo rate has stayed anchored near 1.4 percent, and the offshore yuan has traded inside its recent band against the dollar, a signal that the mainland's liquidity desks are not pricing in a near-term cross-strait shock, or, more precisely, are not being instructed by Beijing's economic planners to build a contingency premium into the rate corridor the way they did during the August 2022 Pelosi-visit window, when overnight funding costs spiked and the central parity rate moved by more than the daily band typically allows.

That gap between Taipei's calendar and the PBOC's calendar is the trade. Han Kuang runs through this week on a schedule set by Taiwan's Ministry of National Defense; the PBOC's Monetary Policy Committee does not meet again until its routine quarterly session, and the People's Liberation Army's warplane and warship presence around the island, tallied daily by Taiwan's defense ministry at 21 aircraft and 14 vessels as of this week, has not moved the mainland's funding desks to reprice risk the way a genuine mobilization signal would. Premier Li Qiang's economic working group, which has used liquidity tools rather than statements to signal policy intent all year, would be the first mover if Beijing's assessment of Han Kuang shifted from routine to provocative. Until the reverse repo rate or the central parity band moves outside its recent range, the PBOC's own ledger says this week's drills are read in Beijing as exercise, not preface.
