Taiwan's Capital Drill Assumes the Blockade Is Coming

Taipei ran police blockades on the bridges into its own capital this week, part of a war drill that also throttled internet speeds and rehearsed a tunnel blockade meant to stop People's Liberation Army forces from moving through the city. Taiwan's coast guard, the smaller service historically built for fisheries patrol and smuggling interdiction, is being armed for direct confrontation with Chinese vessels. Neither move treats invasion as a distant contingency. Both are logistics for a specific afternoon: the one when Beijing's coast guard and PLA assets are already inside Taiwan's contiguous waters and the government has hours, not weeks, to move personnel and slow an advance through Taipei's own streets. Washington's response, delivered through the State Department, was a request that Beijing halt pressure on Taiwan. The request and the drill are not answering the same question.

Taipei's planners are building for the days after a blockade begins, which means they have already concluded that deterrence, on its own, will not hold that day. A bridge blockade drill only pays off if the bridges are contested, which means the exercise assumes PLA forces reach the capital's perimeter. Washington's statement, filed the same week, asks Beijing to stop something without naming the something, which leaves Taipei to plan for the case where the ask goes unheeded. The next signal to watch is whether Taipei's Ministry of National Defense schedules a second capital-level drill before year end. One is a rehearsal. Two, within months, is a standing assumption.

By Vincent LaiGeopolitical Desk, Occasional ContributorFri, Aug 14
Taiwan's War Drills Have No Institutional Counterpart in Beijing

Taipei spent this week blockading its own bridges and throttling internet speeds in a capital-defense drill; Washington, through the State Department, asked Beijing to halt pressure on Taiwan. Read against a ledger rather than a headline, the asymmetry is the story: Taipei's drill has a budget line, a Ministry of National Defense sign-off, and a named logistics assumption about how long the capital can hold. Beijing's response, so far, has none. The PBOC's open-market desk has issued no defensive liquidity signal this month that would suggest the mainland's financial authorities are pricing a confrontation timeline anywhere near Taipei's, and Premier Li's economic working group has kept its cross-strait guidance unchanged since the spring tariff cycle.

That gap matters more than the drill itself. A capital-defense rehearsal is a fiscal commitment as much as a military one, and Taipei's finance ministry has to fund a second one if the Ministry of National Defense schedules it before year end, or, more precisely, has to fund the standing assumption a second drill would represent. Beijing has not moved a matching instrument, not a bond issuance, not a capital-control adjustment, not a PBOC liquidity facility, that would signal its own institutions are budgeting for the same afternoon. Until one of Beijing's desks puts a number against this, the mainland side of the ledger stays blank, and the next entry to watch is whether the PBOC's November fixing carries any language addressing cross-strait risk at all.
